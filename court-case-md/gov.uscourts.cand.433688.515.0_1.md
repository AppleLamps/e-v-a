 1          Plaintiff Elon Musk respectfully submits this brief pursuant to the Court’s April 29, 2026
 2 order on the record that the parties file briefs addressing Plaintiff ’s breach of charitable trust claim

 3 under California Business and Professions Code § 17510.8.

 4                                              ARGUMENT
 5          Plaintiff asserts breach of charitable trust on three independent legal grounds: (1) common

 6 law breach of charitable trust under cases such as Holt v. College of Ostheopathic Physicians &

 7 Surgeons, 61 Cal. 2d 750 (1964), and L.B. Research & Education Foundation v. UCLA Foundation,

 8 130 Cal. App. 4th 171 (2005), as well as the Restatement (Third) of Trusts § 94 (2012); (2) statutory

 9 breach of charitable trust under California Corporations Code § 5142(a); and (3) statutory breach of

10 charitable trust under California Business and Professions Code § 17510.8. This brief addresses

11 only the third of those three grounds.

12          The evidence at trial supports a claim against Defendants under that statute. OpenAI and

13 Altman “solicited” charitable donations from Musk. And Section 17510.6’s exclusion for the

14 “membership” of a charity does not apply.

15 I.       OPENAI AND ALTMAN “SOLICITED” CONTRIBUTIONS FROM MUSK

16          Section 17510.8 provides: “Notwithstanding any other provision of this article, there exists

17 a fiduciary relationship between a charity or any person soliciting on behalf of a charity, and the

18 person from whom a charitable contribution is being solicited.” Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.8.

19 “The acceptance of charitable contributions by a charity or any person soliciting on behalf of a

20 charity establishes a charitable trust and a duty on the part of the charity and the person soliciting

21 on behalf of the charity to use those charitable contributions for the declared charitable purposes for

22 which they are sought.” Id.

23          Under Section 17510.8, “when the fundraiser solicits money for a declared purpose, the

24 donation must be used for that purpose.” People v. Orange County Charitable Servs., 73 Cal. App.

25 4th 1054, 1069 n.7 (1999); see also Exec. Comm. Representing Signing Petitioners of Archdiocese

26 of W. U.S. v. Kaplan, No. 03-cv-8947, 2004 WL 6084228, at *8 (C.D. Cal. Sept. 17, 2004)

27 (“[Section] 17510.8 states that a fiduciary relationship exists between a charity or a person soliciting

28 on behalf of a charity and the person from whom the charity is soliciting.”). “In California, any

 1 individual or organization that solicits charitable funds is considered a ‘charitable trustee’ . . . and

 2 is accountable for such funds.” Cal. Dep’t of Justice, Attorney General’s Guide for Charities 4

 3 (2024). By making solicited parties the express statutory object and beneficiary of a traditional

 4 common law duty, the statute “establish[es] elements of [a] pre-existing common law cause[ ] of

 5 action.” Crusader Ins. Co. v. Scottsdale Ins. Co., 54 Cal. App. 4th 121, 125 (1997).

 6          By its plain terms, Section 17510.8 establishes two requirements: The plaintiff must be a
 7 “person from whom a charitable contribution [was] solicited”; and the defendant must have

 8 “accept[ed]” the contributions. Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.8. Both requirements are met here,

 9 multiple times over.

10          First, Sam Altman explicitly solicited donations from Musk when they were founding
11 OpenAI together. On October 18, 2015, Altman emailed Musk: “As discussed I think starting with

12 a $100MM commitment (and leaving the time unspecified) is the way to go. . . . Specific asks for

13 you: 1) Can you donate $30MM over the next 5 years?” PX007 (emphasis added) (introduced at

14 4/28 Tr. 348:5-349:19 (Musk)). A few months later, Altman renewed his request: “Can you do

15 $20MM a year for the each of the next 3 years? ” PX069 (emphasis added). By their plain terms,

16 those express requests for financial assistance were solicitations for donations within the meaning

17 of the statute. OpenAI accepted the fruits of those solicitations by receiving Musk’s quarterly

18 donations and rent payments over the next five years. PX112.

19          Second, OpenAI solicited donations from Musk once again in 2020, after Musk informed
20 OpenAI that he planned to stop paying OpenAI’s rent. On July 22, 2020, OpenAI’s CFO Chris

21 Clark wrote to Musk’s financial advisor Jared Brichall and pleaded for more donations: “Since the

22 non-profit needs to remain on the lease, it would greatly help the nonprofit org if you’re willing to

23 assist with covering a portion of the anticipated near term landlord passthroughs and security

24 costs.” PX103 (emphasis added) (to be introduced through Birchall). Clark made two funding

25 proposals, one of which was that Musk “could simply keep the current $290k monthly Fidelity

26 grants going through the end of September.” Id. Musk agreed to that proposal and kept funding

27 OpenAI’s rent for another two months. PX112. Once again, OpenAI accepted those donations,

28 bringing this case squarely within the ambit of Section 17510.8.

 4 II.      SECTION 17510.6’S MEMBERSHIP EXCLUSION DOES NOT APPLY
 5          OpenAI claims that Section 17510.8 does not apply because another provision, Section
 6 17510.6, states that “[t]he provisions of this article shall not apply to solicitations, sales, offers, or

 7 attempts to sell within the membership of a charitable organization or upon its regular occupied

 8 premises.” Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.6 (emphasis added). OpenAI contends that Musk was

 9 a “member” of OpenAI from the time he founded the organization in 2015 until he left the OpenAI

10 board in February 2018. That argument fails for multiple reasons.

11          First, the argument is irrelevant to Chris Clark’s solicitation of donations from Musk in July
12 2020. PX103. That solicitation occurred over two years after Musk left the board in February 2018.

13 And Musk continued to make monthly rent payments for two more months after Clark’s solicitation.

14 PX112. There is no conceivable basis for applying the membership instruction to those donations.

15          Second, although the statute requires that the plaintiff be a “person from whom a charitable
16 contribution [was] solicited,” the charitable trust arises only upon “[t]he acceptance of charitable

17 contributions by a charity or any person soliciting on behalf of a charity.” Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code

18 § 17510.8 (emphasis added). Musk contributed almost $10 million in rent after leaving the board in

19 February 2018. PX112. The statute applies to OpenAI’s acceptance of those donations, even if

20 Musk was a member of OpenAI at some earlier time.

21          Third, Section 17510.6’s membership exclusion does not apply because Section 17510.8
22 contains a broad “notwithstanding” clause that renders Section 17510.6 inapplicable. Section

23 17510.8 states that it applies “[n]otwithstanding any other provision of this article.” Cal. Bus. &

24 Prof. Code § 17510.8 (emphasis added).           That broad “notwithstanding” clause displaces any
25 contrary provisions like Section 17510.6. “When the Legislature intends for a statute to prevail over

26 all contrary law, it typically signals this intent by using phrases like ‘notwithstanding any other law’

27 or ‘notwithstanding other provisions of law.’ ” In re Greg F., 55 Cal. 4th 393, 406 (2012); see also

28 Arias v. Superior Ct., 46 Cal. 4th 969, 983 (2009) (“The statutory phrase ‘notwithstanding any other

 1 provision of law’ has been called a ‘term of art’ that declares the legislative intent to override all

 2 contrary law.’ ”). That is precisely the sort of language the Legislature enacted here. Section

 3 17510.6 is another provision “of this article,” so Section 17510.8 applies “notwithstanding” that

 4 other provision.

 5          The enactment dates of the two provisions confirm that conclusion. “If conflicting statutes
 6 cannot be reconciled, later enactments supersede earlier ones . . . .” State Dep’t of Pub. Health v.

 7 Superior Ct., 60 Cal. 4th 940, 960 (2015); see also Cross v. Superior Ct., 11 Cal. App. 5th 305, 322

 8 (2017) (“It is well established that a statute enacted later in time controls over an earlier-enacted

 9 statute . . . .”). Section 17510.8 was enacted in 1992 (Stats.1992, c. 1100 (A.B. 3053), § 1), whereas

10 Section 17510.6 was enacted years earlier in 1980 (Stats. 1980, c. 1267, p. 4294, § 8). The more

11 recent statute therefore governs in any conflict.

12          Finally, Section 17510.8 itself declares that “[t]his section is declarative of existing trust law
13 principles.” Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.8. Existing trust law principles include no carveout

14 for the membership of charitable organizations. The statute should not be construed to impose such

15 a requirement either.

16          OpenAI relies on Cohen v. Kabbalah Centre International, 35 Cal. App. 5th 13 (2019), in
17 which a California intermediate appellate court construed Section 17510.6’s membership exclusion

18 to apply to Section 17510.8 notwithstanding the latter provision’s notwithstanding clause. Id. at 23.

19 But that case misread the statute. Cohen reasoned that “[Section 17510.8’s notwithstanding clause]

20 has no application here . . . because this section is within the article that does not apply to

21 ‘solicitations’ within the membership of a charitable organization.” 35 Cal. App. 5th at 23. That

22 reasoning gets the statutory interpretation backwards. Section 17510.8 is the provision that contains

23 a categorical “notwithstanding” clause that evinces a legislative intent to trump any contrary

24 provision. There is no similar language in 17510.6. Any conflict between the two provisions must

25 therefore be resolved in favor of Section 17510.8. That is the precise reason why the Legislature

26 included the notwithstanding clause in Section 17510.8 but not 17510.6.

27          State intermediate appellate decisions like Cohen “are not binding if a federal court is
28 ‘convinced by other persuasive data.’ ” City of Portland v. HomeAway.com, Inc., 240 F. Supp. 3d

 4                                            CONCLUSION
 5         For all the foregoing reasons, the trial evidence supports Plaintiff ’s statutory breach of

 6 charitable trust claim under Section 17510.8, and Section 17510.6’s membership exclusion does not

 7 preclude the claim.
