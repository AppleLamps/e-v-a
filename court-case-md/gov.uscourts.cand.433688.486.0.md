 1          Pursuant to Paragraph 3(g) of the Court’s Standing Order Re: Pretrial Instructions in Civil Cases

 2 (last updated March 17, 2025), Plaintiff Elon Musk respectfully submits the following Proposed Jury

 3 Instruction No. 30A-P on the continuing violation doctrine.

 4          The continuing violation doctrine extends the statute of limitations on a claim where the

 5 plaintiff ’s injuries “are the product of a series of small harms, any one of which may not be

 6 actionable on its own.” Aryeh v. Canon Bus. Sols., Inc., 55 Cal. 4th 1185, 1197 (2013). “Allegations

 7 of a pattern of reasonably frequent and similar acts may, in a given case, justify treating the acts as

 8 an indivisible course of conduct actionable in its entirety, notwithstanding that the conduct occurred

 9 partially outside and partially inside the limitations period.” Id. at 1198. The case must involve “a

10 wrongful course of conduct [that] became apparent only through the accumulation of a series of

11 harms.” Id.; see also Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc., 36 Cal. 4th 1028, 1058 (2005) (applying

12 doctrine where “some or all of the component acts might not be individually actionable” and the

13 plaintiff “may not yet recognize [the acts] as part of a pattern”).

14          The evidence adduced at trial may well support a continuing violation instruction here. All

15 parties agree that there is nothing per se improper about a nonprofit charity forming a for-profit

16 affiliate to support the nonprofit’s charitable objectives, so long as the for-profit genuinely supports

17 the charity and its mission rather than subverting that mission to the financial interests of outside

18 investors. The point at which a for-profit affiliate’s activities become disproportionate to or

19 inconsistent with a nonprofit’s charitable mission is often a matter of degree. The jury could

20 therefore reasonably find on these facts that the case involves “a wrongful course of conduct [that]

21 became apparent only through the accumulation of a series of harms.” Aryeh, 55 Cal. 4th at 1198.

22 The Court should give a continuing violation instruction so the jury can correctly apply the statute

23 of limitations to these circumstances.

24          At the April 17 hearing, Defendants argued that Plaintiff ’s counsel “waived” this instruction

25 at the pretrial conference. 4/17 Tr. 77:18-78:21. The record does not support a finding of waiver.

27 Inc., 596 U.S. 411, 417 (2022). Accordingly, a claim is waived only when a litigant’s conduct is

28 “clear, decisive and unequivocal of a purpose to waive the legal rights involved.” Plaskett v.

 8          The record does not support a finding of waiver under that demanding standard.

 9          First, Plaintiff ’s written pretrial statement made clear that Plaintiff was not waiving the

10 continuing violation doctrine. Shortly before the pretrial submissions were due, OpenAI added a

11 footnote asserting that Plaintiff had waived that theory. Dkt. 427 at 43 n.18. Plaintiff responded:

12 “Aryeh addressed both the ‘continuous accrual’ doctrine and the ‘continuing violation’ doctrine. 55

13 Cal. 4th at 1197-1202. For several reasons, the continuous accrual doctrine is the better fit for the

14 facts of this case. But this Court can and should grant the relief sought under whichever theory

15 it considers more appropriate.” Dkt. 427 at 46 n.19 (emphasis added). Plaintiff thus expressly

16 reserved the continuing violation doctrine.

17          Second, counsel’s oral response to the Court at the hearing never suggested that Plaintiff

18 was giving up the right to propose a continuing violation instruction. Rather, the Court asked,

19 “You’re not arguing continuing violation, correct?,” and Plaintiff ’s counsel responded, “So Aryeh

20 discusses both doctrines—.” Dkt. 445 at 33:3-5. The Court then cut off that response and instructed

21 counsel to answer “yes or no,” to which counsel responded: “We aren’t advancing the continuing

22 violation doctrine, Your Honor.” Id. at 33:5-10. That response was intended as a description of the

23 instructions Plaintiff was advancing from his submissions. It was not intended to be a waiver or

24 relinquishment of the right to submit additional instructions.

26 supplemental jury instructions as the case proceeds. Rule 51(a) provides that, even after the close

27 of the evidence, a party may “file requests for instructions on issues that could not reasonably have

28 been anticipated by an earlier time that the court set for requests” or, “with the court’s permission,

 2 order similarly provides for the filing of supplemental instructions. See Standing Order Re: Pretrial

 5          Those rules contemplate that parties will continue to submit proposed instructions

 6 throughout the trial as the case develops. As the advisory committee notes explain, “trial evidence

 7 may raise new issues or reshape issues the parties thought they had understood.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 51

 8 advisory comm. n. (2003). And while the Court may require permission where a party proposes a

 9 new instruction after the close of evidence, even that discretion is limited: “[D]iscretion to decline

10 [a] request because it is untimely should be exercised sparingly and cautiously.” 9C Wright &

11 Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure § 2552 (3d ed. rev. 2026) (emphasis added). Because the

12 submission of supplemental instructions is a routine feature of federal trial practice, a party’s

13 statement about what instructions the party is currently advancing cannot fairly be construed as a

14 waiver of the right to seek supplemental instructions on the same topic at a later date as the case

15 develops.

16          Fourth, Rule 51(c) expressly provides that “[a]n objection [to another party’s instruction] is

17 timely” so long as the party objects at the charge conference following the Court’s delivery of its

18 own proposed instructions to the parties – a step that indisputably has not yet occurred. Fed. R. Civ.

19 P. 51(c)(2). Under the plain language of that rule, Plaintiff may object at the charge conference to

20 Defendants’ instructions as misstating the relevant statute of limitations principles, even on grounds

21 not previously asserted in the case. Because these issues will resurface at the charge conference

22 regardless, permitting Plaintiff to propose a continuing violation instruction now will help ensure

23 that the parties try the case with the relevant legal standards firmly in view.

24          Finally, Plaintiff ’s timing was reasonable under the circumstances. Plaintiff did not submit

25 a separate proposed continuing violation instruction sooner because he reasonably believed that the

26 continuous accrual doctrine was the better fit for the case. Dkt. 427 at 46 n.19. This Court disagreed.

27 Dkt. 456 at 7. That ruling caused Plaintiff to reevaluate his position and consider whether the

28 continuing violation doctrine might well be an appropriate fit. There was nothing inappropriate

 1 30A-P. CONTINUING VIOLATION DOCTRINE (Plaintiff Only)

 2          If the Defendants prove that Plaintiff ’s claim accrued before the applicable date for that

 3 claim as set forth in the preceding instruction, Plaintiff ’s claim was still filed on time if Plaintiff

 4 proves by a preponderance of the evidence that Defendants engaged in a wrongful course of conduct

 5 that occurred partially before and partially after the applicable date for that claim, and which became

 6 apparent only through the accumulation of a series of wrongful acts, any one of which may not have

 7 been actionable on its own.
