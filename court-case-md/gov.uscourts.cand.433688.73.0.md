 1                                          INTRODUCTION

 2          The moving papers and oppositions now frame for the Court three questions of fact and

 3 six legal issues for decision.

 4          Defendants’ Group Boycott

 5          The first question of fact is whether OpenAI issued the “Fund No Competitors” edict as

 6 part of its October 2024 funding round. As consistently reported in The Wall Street Journal,

 7 Financial Times, and Reuters, based on several sources with knowledge, it did. Defendants

 8 collectively produced two declarations from lower-level employees that speak past those news

 9 accounts; they refer only to investors’ information rights in written documents and Microsoft’s

10 supposed non-involvement. Wu Decl.; Wetter Decl. But it has never been Plaintiffs’ contention

11 that the “Fund No Competitors” restriction was written into documents by lawyers who must

12 surely know better. The news accounts reported that OpenAI issued the edict verbally. Plaintiffs’

13 ongoing investigations, without the benefit of discovery, now show this is exactly what

14 happened. Sam Altman told investors their allocation in OpenAI’s oversubscribed funding

15 round was contingent on not investing in competitors. See Musk Decl. ¶¶ 3-4; Birchall Decl. ¶¶ 3-

16 5; Gracias Decl. ¶¶ 3-4; Baker Decl. ¶¶ 3-4. A declaration from Altman is conspicuously absent.

17          On this record, the Court should conclude that Plaintiffs will likely prove the existence

18 of the Fund No Competitors edict after discovery. The first legal question, then, is whether the

19 edict constitutes a group boycott. Plaintiffs will likely prove it does in two ways: (i) as a hub-

20 and-spoke conspiracy, because all “plus factors” courts consider point to an agreement not just

21 between OpenAI and its investors, but among the investors as well; and (ii) as a horizontal

22 restraint by OpenAI and Microsoft, because OpenAI’s conduct should be attributed to

23 Microsoft as a matter of law given its effective control of OpenAI. See infra pp. 6-9.

26 raised Section 8’s safe-harbor defenses. The Court must therefore decide whether Defendants

27 proved those defenses with evidence demonstrating that OpenAI and Microsoft had competitive

13

14          OpenAI argues incorrectly that Musk and xAI lack Article III and antitrust standing. But

15 “[b]ecause Article III standing imposes a lower bar than antitrust standing,” and because Musk

16 and xAI have antitrust standing, they necessarily “meet[] the requirements for Article III

17 standing.” Tawfilis v. Allergan, Inc., 157 F. Supp. 3d 853, 863 (C.D. Cal. 2015); see City of

18 Oakland v. Oakland Raiders, 20 F.4th 441, 452-53 (9th Cir. 2021) (discussing lower showing

19 required under Article III). Plaintiffs therefore address only statutory antitrust standing.

20          Defendants claim Plaintiffs lack statutory standing because they do not show a

21 sufficient antitrust injury. 2 But Musk and xAI alleged concrete harms in their FAC, which

22 because it was verified, operates as a declaration. FAC ¶ 227; Talavera v. Glob. Payments, Inc.,

23 670 F. Supp. 3d 1074 (S.D. Cal. 2023). And since the filing of the FAC, further evidence has

24 come to light. OpenAI’s edict explicitly created a belief among investors that they will lose the

25 opportunity to participate in OpenAI’s growth if they invest in OpenAI’s competitors. Musk
