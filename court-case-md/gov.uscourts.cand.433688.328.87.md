15                                             BACKGROUND

16           OpenAI, Inc. (“OpenAI”) is a Delaware nonprofit corporation headquartered in California.

17    It was founded in 2015 by Sam Altman, Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, and Elon Musk.

18           Musk filed this lawsuit against Altman, Brockman, OpenAI, Inc., and other OpenAI entities

19    in August 2024. Dkt. 1. Musk amended his complaint in November 2024, Dkt. 32, and again in

20    May 2025, Dkt. 170 (“SAC”). Musk and his entity X.AI Corp. asserted 26 causes of action against

21    the OpenAI Defendants. In its rulings on the OpenAI Defendants’ motions to dismiss, the Court

22    dismissed Musk’s claims for breach of express contract, breach of the implied covenant of good

23    faith and fair dealing, tortious interference with contract, aiding and abetting fraud, a Clayton Act

24    violation, false advertising under the Lanham Act, false advertising under California law, aiding

25    and abetting breach of fiduciary duty to Musk, breach of fiduciary duty to OpenAI, self-dealing,

26    aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty to OpenAI, civil RICO, and RICO conspiracy. Dkt.

27    121 at 11 n.8, 16 n.13; Dkt. 163; Dkt. 228 at 9-13. The Court permitted Musk’s claims for breach

28    of implied contract, breach of quasi-contract/unjust enrichment, constructive fraud, fraud, and

 4                                                ANALYSIS

 5           The Court “shall grant summary judgment if the movant shows that there is no genuine

 6    dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R.

 7    Civ. P. 56(a). Because Musk “will have the burden of proof at trial,” the OpenAI Defendants “need

 8    only point out that there is an absence of evidence to support [his] case.” Olivier v. Baca, 913 F.3d

 9    852, 857 (9th Cir. 2019). Musk must “designate specific facts demonstrating the existence of

10    genuine issues for trial.” In re Oracle Corp. Secs. Litig., 627 F.3d 376, 387 (9th Cir. 2010). Musk’s

11    “burden is not a light one,” id., as he must present “significant probative evidence” to show a

12    genuine factual dispute, Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 249 (1986). Applying this

13    standard, summary judgment is warranted on each of Musk’s remaining Phase I claims.

14    I.     BREACH OF CHARITABLE TRUST (COUNT XVIII)

15           Summary judgment in favor of the OpenAI Defendants is appropriate on Musk’s claim for

16    breach of charitable trust because there is no genuine dispute of material fact that Musk lacks

17    standing to pursue the claim. Musk did not make any cash donations to OpenAI. Instead, Musk

18    donated to two donor-advised funds (“DAFs”) and to OpenAI’s fiscal sponsor, YC Org. (“YC”).

19    At the time of Musk’s donations, those entities took ownership and control of the funds, and later

20    donated to OpenAI. Musk received tax benefits upon his donations to the DAFs and YC. Those

21    donations to the DAFs and YC severed Musk’s legal interest in the funds before they were later

22    donated to OpenAI. As a matter of law, Musk thus lacks Article III standing to bring a charitable

23    trust claim based on those donations. See Pinkert v. Schwab Charitable Fund, 48 F.4th 1051 (9th

24    Cir. 2022), aff’g, 2021 WL 2476869 (N.D. Cal. June 17, 2021).

25           Even if Musk could surmount Article III’s bar, he also lacks standing under California law.

26    In Holt v. Coll. of Osteopathic Phys. & Surgeons, the California Supreme Court explained that “the

27    only person who can object to the disposition of the trust property is one having some definite

28    interest in the property—he must be a trustee, or a cestui [i.e., a beneficiary], or have some

 2    on any of these bases. Pinkert, 2021 WL 2476869, at *5.

 3           Musk also cannot pursue his claim on the basis of “settlor standing.” Before discovery, the

 4    Court ruled that “for the purposes of” the OpenAI Defendants’ motion to dismiss under Rule

 5    12(b)(6), Musk’s standing was “sufficient as a settlor,” referencing § 94(2) of the Restatement

 6    (Third) of Trusts. Dkt. 121 at 15 n.11. But there is no trust for Musk to enforce within the meaning

 7    of the Third Restatement because the undisputed facts show that Musk’s claimed donations were

 8    not subject to any specific restrictions; and even if a charitable trust was created within the meaning

 9    of the Third Restatement, the “settlors” would be Vanguard, Fidelity, and YC, not Musk.

10           Even if Musk had contributed directly to OpenAI, he would still lack standing to pursue his

11    claim. Musk’s reliance on § 5142 of California’s Corporations Code is misplaced because: (a) that

12    provision does not apply to nonprofit corporations incorporated outside of California like OpenAI,

13    and (b) Musk does not meet any of the provision’s standing requirements, as he is neither “[t]he

14    corporation,” nor an “officer” or “director” of the corporation, Cal. Corp. Code § 5142(a)(1)-(3),

15    and has also disclaimed any “reversionary interest” in his claimed donations.

16           Musk’s lack of reversionary interest in his alleged donations also forecloses any claim to

17    “special interest” standing. Under California law, “[i]t is well established that the settlor of a

18    charitable trust who retains no reversionary interest in the trust property lacks standing to bring an

19    action to enforce the trust.” Horiike v. Humane Soc., 2016 WL 11744969, at *16 (C.D. Cal. June

20    20, 2016) (quoting Patton v. Sherwood, 152 Cal. App. 4th 339, 342 (2007)). Section 17510.8 of

21    California’s charitable solicitations statute does not give Musk standing either, as it is simply

22    “declarative of existing trust law principles,” and does not purport to extend standing to anyone.

23    Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.8. It also applies to public “solicitations,” and not, as here, to

24    solicitations “within the membership of a charitable organization.” §§ 17510, 17510.6.

25           Finally, Musk’s noncash contributions do not save his claim. To the extent the vehicles he

26    donated could be deemed a restricted gift, Musk has identified no evidence that he retained a

27    reversionary interest in them, nor that OpenAI used them in a manner inconsistent with any

28    restriction. And Musk’s alleged contributions of “personal investment of time, expertise, guidance,

 3    II.    CONSTRUCTIVE FRAUD (COUNT VI)

 4           Musk’s constructive fraud claim is premised on the existence of an alleged “fiduciary

 5    relationship” arising from § 17510.8 and the same contributions on which Musk bases his charitable

 6    trust claim. SAC ¶¶ 291-93. Summary judgment is warranted on this claim, because (i) § 17510.8

 7    does not apply to Musk’s alleged contributions; (ii) even if it did, Musk did not make those

 8    contributions, severing any alleged fiduciary relationship; and (iii) Musk lacks standing to sue

 9    OpenAI for an alleged failure to “use [the] charitable contributions for the declared charitable

10    purposes for which they [were] sought,” SAC ¶ 292 (citing § 17510.8). See Fabian v. LeMahieu,

11    2019 WL 4918431, at *14 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 4, 2019); Pinkert, 2021 WL 2476869, at *1.1

12    III.   FRAUD (COUNT VII)

13           Musk’s fraud claim fails on multiple independent grounds. First, the record is devoid of any

14    actionable misrepresentation of a “material and knowingly false representation of fact.” Huntsman

15    v. Corp. of the President of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints, 127 F.4th 784, 790

16    (9th Cir. 2025). The fraud theory Musk pleaded is that Altman and Brockman made false

17    representations in two September 2017 communications: an email from Altman to Musk saying

18    that he was “enthusiastic about the non-profit structure,” and a second-hand report to Musk, filtered

19    through Shivon Zilis, that Brockman “would like to continue with the non-profit structure” and that

20    Altman was “[g]reat with keeping [OpenAI] non-profit.” SAC ¶¶ 303-05. The record does not

21    support the allegations of Musk’s complaint. Because there was no actionable misrepresentation,

22    the fraud claim must now be dismissed for want of evidentiary support. Louis v. Nailtiques

23    Cosmetic Corp., 423 F. App’x 711, 713 (9th Cir. 2011).

24           Second, the record evidence negates the element of justifiable reliance. Huntsman, 127

25    F.4th at 789-90. To prove justifiable reliance, “plaintiff[s] must show (1) that they actually relied

11    1203, 1207 (9th Cir. 2007). Musk’s own testimony establishes that he believed he had “reason to

12    be suspicious and should have investigated [the alleged] fraud” no later than September 2017. Vera

13    v. REL-BC, LLC, 66 Cal. App. 5th 57, 70 (2021). Thus, the statute of limitations expired long before

14    Musk filed suit in 2024.

15    IV.    UNJUST ENRICHMENT (COUNT IV)

16           Unjust enrichment “is not a standalone cause of action” under California law. Silver v.

17    Stripe Inc., 2021 WL 3191752, at *8 (N.D. Cal. July 28, 2021). In the Ninth Circuit, “courts have

18    construed purported claims for unjust enrichment as quasi-contract claims seeking restitution.”

19    Brodsky v. Apple Inc., 2019 WL 4141936, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 30, 2019); Dkt. 298 at 2. The

20    claim requires proof of the defendant’s “receipt of a benefit and unjust retention of the benefit at

21    the expense of another.” Dkt. 163 at 3.

22           Musk’s unjust enrichment claim fails on several independent grounds. First, California law

23    bars the claim because Musk is pursuing tort claims based on the same alleged conduct. Silver,

24    2021 WL 3191752, at *8; McBride v. Boughton, 123 Cal. App. 4th 379, 388 (2004).

25           Second, any request-based quasi-contract theory fails because there was no expectation

26    Musk would be compensated for his alleged contributions. Taylor v. Google, LLC, 2024 WL

27    837044, at *2 (9th Cir. Feb. 28, 2024); Anderson v. Am. Airlines, Inc., 2011 WL 855820, at *5

28    (N.D. Cal. Mar. 9, 2011).

 1           Third, any fraud-based unjust enrichment theory is impermissibly duplicative and fails in

 2    all events. Young v. Cree, Inc., 2021 WL 4749384, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 12, 2021); Dkt. 163 at 3.

 3           Finally, the claim is untimely under either theory. The two-year statute of limitations

 4    applicable to quasi-contract claims began to run no later than 2020, expiring long before Musk filed

 5    suit in 2024. Wu v. Sunrider Corp., 2018 WL 6266577, at *6 (C.D. Cal. May 22, 2018), aff’d, 793

 6    F. App’x 507 (9th Cir. 2019). The three-year statute of limitations applicable to fraud-based claims

 7    began to run at the same time as Musk’s fraud claim and is untimely for the same reasons. Zhang

 8    v. Yuan, 2024 WL 3906751, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 21, 2024).

 9                                             CONCLUSION

10           For the foregoing reasons, it is ORDERED that the OpenAI Defendants’ motion for

11    summary judgment is GRANTED.
