 1                                           INTRODUCTION

 2          Defendant Microsoft Corp. seeks to preclude Plaintiff from cross-examining Microsoft’s

 3 damages expert, Dr. Jonathan Arnold, about prior litigation in which he asserted a fraud claim that

 4 lacked any factual basis and was ultimately sanctioned for pursuing the claim on appeal. The Court

 5 should deny that motion. This questioning is plainly proper under Rule 608(b), and Microsoft fails

 6 to justify exclusion under Rule 403.

 7                                              ARGUMENT

 8 I.       ARNOLD’S LITIGATION HISTORY IS ADMISSIBLE UNDER RULE 608(b)

 9          Under Rule 608(b), “extrinsic evidence is not admissible to prove specific instances of a

10 witness’s conduct in order to attack or support the witness’s character for truthfulness,” but “the

11 court may, on cross-examination, allow them to be inquired into if they are probative of the character

12 for truthfulness or untruthfulness.” Fed. R. Evid. 608(b). Courts regularly permit cross-examination

13 under that rule where a witness previously engaged in conduct that bears on the witness’s character

14 for truthfulness. See, e.g., United States v. Reid, 634 F.2d 469, 473 (9th Cir. 1980) (“Appellant

15 placed his credibility in issue when he took the witness stand. Cross-examination concerning the

16 false statements in the letter was entirely proper to impeach appellant’s general credibility.”); United

17 States v. Durant, 89 F. App’x 56, 60 (9th Cir. 2004) (“FRE 608(b) squarely permits the admission

18 of evidence that [defendant] lied on a lease application and on a bankruptcy filing.”).

19          That rule applies squarely here. Arnold previously pursued a fraud claim that multiple

20 federal judges rejected as “frivolous” and “preposterous” because Arnold was well aware of the

21 allegedly concealed fact: He accused someone of defrauding him when he plainly knew he had not

22 been defrauded. Mot. Ex. A at 48:4-52:25; Arnold v. Villarreal, 853 F.3d 384, 386-87 (7th Cir.

23 2017) (recounting litigation). When Arnold appealed, the Seventh Circuit affirmed the judgment

24 against him in a published opinion and ordered Arnold to pay sanctions for bringing the frivolous

25 appeal. Mot. Ex. A at 53:1-54:24; Arnold, 853 F.3d at 389 (“There is no theory of justifiable reliance

26 that could overcome the undisputed fact that [Arnold] knew [the allegedly concealed facts] . . . .

27 Arnold has utterly failed to confront what two district judges recognized: that his fraud claims are

28 not merely meritless but are frivolous.”).

 1          That litigation history is plainly “probative of [Arnold’s] character for truthfulness or

 2 untruthfulness.” Fed. R. Evid. 608(b). Microsoft urges that “[t]he sanctions order related to

 3 Dr. Arnold’s pursuit of a frivolous appeal – not for making a false statement.” Mot. at 3. But the

 4 reason the appeal was frivolous was that Arnold lacked any factual basis for his claim: He accused

 5 someone of defrauding him when he plainly knew he had not been defrauded. See Arnold, 853 F.3d

 6 at 386-87. Prior false accusations, like other false statements, fall squarely within the ambit of Rule

 7 608(b). Cf. Williams v. City of Long Beach, No. 2:19-cv-05929, 2021 WL 9314952, at *6 (C.D.

 8 Cal. Dec. 27, 2021) (plaintiff was “entitled on cross-examination to inquire about specific instances

 9 of prior frauds, such as a past falsification of a police report, in order to undermine Officer Reyes’s

10 truthfulness”), aff ’d, 2022 WL 59629 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 5, 2022).

11          Microsoft tries to blame the sanctions the Seventh Circuit imposed on bad legal advice

12 Arnold received. Mot. at 3. But Arnold repeatedly admitted at his deposition that he authorized the

13 filings his lawyer submitted. Mot. Ex. A at 51:18, 52:15. And as the Seventh Circuit’s description

14 of the events makes clear, even a layperson should have appreciated that Arnold’s fraud claim lacked

15 any factual basis. See Arnold, 853 F.3d at 386-87.

16          Finally, Microsoft’s argument that Arnold’s prior litigation is not “remotely related to his

17 damages testimony” (Mot. at 2-3) ignores how Rule 608(b) works. Rule 608(b) evidence need not

18 be (and normally is not) substantively relevant to the claims in the case. The evidence comes in

19 because it is probative of the witness’s truthfulness. Arnold, no less than any other witness, will

20 “place[ ] his credibility in issue when he [takes] the witness stand.” Reid, 634 F.2d at 473. He will

21 invite the jury to accept his opinions as careful and objective analyses. The jury should be permitted

22 to consider Arnold’s prior litigation history when deciding whether to credit his opinions. In

23 evaluating Arnold’s testimony at trial, the jury may well find it relevant that Arnold is not always

24 discriminating about what he asserts in court.

     II.    RULE 403 DOES NOT JUSTIFY PRECLUDING ARNOLD’S
26          LITIGATION HISTORY

27          Microsoft seeks to preclude Plaintiff ’s questioning under Rule 403. But that rule applies

28 only when the evidence’s “probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of . . . unfair

 1 prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, [or] wasting time.” Fed. R. Evid.

 2 403 (emphasis added). Microsoft has not made that showing.

 3          Arnold’s litigation history is highly probative Rule 608(b) evidence. That history relates

 4 specifically to false accusations made in court and thus is uniquely probative of Arnold’s credibility

 5 in judicial proceedings. And unlike cases involving mere allegations of untruthfulness, five federal

 6 judges have now found that Arnold’s fraud accusations were so wholly lacking in factual basis that

 7 they were “preposterous” or “frivolous.” Mot. Ex. A at 48:4-54:18. The fact that those are judicial

 8 findings increases their probative value. See Zucchella v. Olympusat, Inc., No. 19-cv-7335, 2023

 9 WL 2633947, at *4 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 10, 2023) (collecting cases admitting “prior judicial findings on

10 a witness’s credibility”).

11          There is no risk that this questioning will “distract” or “confuse” the jury. Mot. at 3. Plaintiff

12 anticipates devoting only a few questions to this topic and does not seek to introduce any extrinsic

13 evidence. And there is no risk that the jury will consider Arnold’s prior litigation history for some

14 purpose other than his credibility, precisely because the prior litigation is so clearly unrelated to the

15 present dispute. Microsoft’s categorial claim that “[e]vidence of sanctions against an expert witness

16 is inadmissible under Federal Rules of Evidence 608(b) and 403” plainly is not the law. Mot. at 4.

17 The only case Microsoft cites for that dubious proposition, Barten v. State Farm Mutual Automobile

18 Insurance Co., No. 12-cv-399, 2015 WL 11111309 (D. Ariz. Apr. 8, 2015), involved a discovery

19 sanction for failure to produce financial documents, and the court was unable to determine whether

20 the order had any bearing on the expert’s credibility at all. See id. at *3-4 (“The nature of the non-

21 compliance is insufficiently clear from the order to determine that it is relevant or probative of [the

22 expert’s] truthfulness or untruthfulness.”).

23          Unable to show that Plaintiff ’s modest questioning would threaten distraction, confusion, or

24 other prejudice, Microsoft argues that Arnold’s responses may implicate those concerns because

25 Arnold supposedly may want to “provide the full context” of his earlier litigation. Mot. at 4:17.

26 That is not a proper basis for precluding the questioning. Plaintiff plans to elicit only the facts that

27 Arnold asserted a fraud claim against a defendant, that multiple courts determined that the claim

28 was preposterous and frivolous because Arnold knew he had not been defrauded, and that Arnold

 2 actions on the theory that the contentious and personal nature of the prior litigation clouded his

 3 judgment (see Mot. Ex. A at 56:4-57:4), that is not a valid defense for filing factually baseless claims

 4 (as the Seventh Circuit’s sanctions make clear). Nor does that theory explain why Arnold needs to

 7 not be permitted to avoid legitimate Rule 608(b) questioning by threatening to bring up gratuitous

10          The Court should deny Microsoft’s motion.
