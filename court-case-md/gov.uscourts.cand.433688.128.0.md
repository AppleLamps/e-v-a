 1                            STATEMENT OF ISSUES TO BE DECIDED

 2          1.      Whether Plaintiff Elon Musk’s contract claims (Counts I, II, III, and V) should be

 3 sustained based on well-pled allegations in the First Amended Complaint of (i) an enforceable

 4 agreement with specific terms conveyed in communications that collectively memorialize the
 5 agreement, (ii) mutual assent evidenced by direct statements including “Agree on all,” (iii) adequate
 6 consideration, including Musk’s over $44 million in contributions plus other valuable support, and
 7 (iv) breach due to Defendants’ systematic conversion of the OpenAI, Inc. non-profit to a for-profit
 8 enterprise.
 9          2.      Whether Musk’s quasi-contract/unjust enrichment claim (Count IV) adequately

10 alleges (i) Defendants’ receipt of benefits derived from misappropriated charitable assets, (ii) their
11 deliberate exploitation for private gain of technology developed with Musk’s charitable
12 contributions contrary to the charity’s explicit mission, and (iii) the inequity of allowing Defendants
13 to retain benefits from this wrongful conduct.
14          3.      Whether Plaintiffs’ fraud claims (Counts VII and VIII) satisfy Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b) by

15 (i) specific allegations regarding false representations about OpenAI’s non-profit form, mission, and
16 charitable purpose, (ii) particularized and plausible pleading of fraudulent intent, and as
17 demonstrated by contemporaneous for-profit conversion plans, and (iii) timely filing under the
18 discovery rule, as Defendants’ scheme was deliberately concealed and only recently revealed.
19          4.      Whether Plaintiffs’ civil RICO claims (Counts XXV and XXVI) adequately plead (i)

20 predicate acts of wire fraud with the required specificity regarding who, what, when, where, and
21 how, (ii) distinctiveness between RICO “persons” (Sam Altman and Greg Brockman) and the
22 “enterprise” (OpenAI, Inc.), (iii) investment-specific injury under § 1962(a), (iv) control-specific
23 injury under § 1962(b), and (v) a conspiracy among all Defendants.
24          5.      Whether Musk has standing to bring claims for breach of charitable trust (Count

25 XX), constructive fraud (Count VI), and aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty (Count XXI)
26 given (i) the Court’s previous ruling confirming standing for breach of charitable trust, and (ii)
27 Defendants’ breach of confidential/fiduciary relationships with Musk individually.

 6          7.      Whether Plaintiffs’ false advertising claims (Counts XVIII and XIX) properly allege

 7 (i) commercial statements about the safety of OpenAI’s products designed to drive consumer
 8 adoption, (ii) falsity through evidence of OpenAI sidelining safety to rush products to market, and
 9 (iii) direct harm to xAI from the resulting diversion of customers, and to Musk from OpenAI’s
10 fraudulent solicitation of his charitable contributions.
11          8.      Whether Plaintiffs’ claim under Section 1 of the Sherman Act (Count IX) and

12 Section 16720 of the Cartwright Act (Count XI) properly allege (i) per se unlawful group boycotts
13 targeting investment in xAI, (ii) Microsoft-OpenAI’s below-cost pricing strategies; (iii)
14 anticompetitive joint conduct, and (iv) substantial market power, given Microsoft-OpenAI’s 69%
15 market share.
16          9.      Whether Plaintiffs’ claim under Section 2 of the Sherman Act (Count X) sufficiently

17 alleges (i) Microsoft-OpenAI’s joint monopolization with an approximately 69% market share, (ii)
18 exclusionary conduct, including Microsoft’s agreement to be OpenAI’s exclusive provider of
19 compute resources, and OpenAI’s exclusive license of its technology to Microsoft, (iii) predatory
20 pricing as to Microsoft’s provision of compute to OpenAI at below-cost and OpenAI’s below-cost
21 products, (iv) group boycott restricting xAI’s access to capital markets, and (v) Microsoft-OpenAI’s
22 de facto merger, substantially lessening competition.
23          10.     Whether Plaintiffs’ Unfair Practices Act claim (Count XII) adequately alleges (i)

24 below-cost pricing with OpenAI projected to lose $5 billion this year and $14 billion by 2026, and
25 (ii) secret rebates through preferential pricing arrangements not extended to competitors.
26          11.     Whether Plaintiffs’ claim under Section 3 of the Clayton Act (Count XIII) and

27 Section 16727 of the Cartwright Act (Count XIV) adequately plead (i) Microsoft’s compute
28 services and OpenAI’s advanced AI models, both involving “tens of thousands of computer

25 xAI, (ii) detailed factual allegations of conduct violating all three UCL prongs, and (iii) alongside
26 other causes of action based on the same unlawful conduct, as permitted under California law.

 9                                          LEGAL STANDARD

10          When considering a motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), the

11 Court must accept all well-pled factual allegations as true and construe them in the light most
12 favorable to the plaintiff and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff. Ashcroft v.
13 Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009); Shwarz v. United States, 234 F.3d 428, 435 (9th Cir. 2000). To
14 survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter to “state a claim to
15 relief that is plausible on its face” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007).
16          A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that permits the

17 reasonable inference the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.
18 While the plausibility standard “asks for more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted
19 unlawfully,” it “is not akin to a ‘probability requirement.’” Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).
