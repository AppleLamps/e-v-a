10          A.      California law does not allow a plaintiff to plead an implied covenant claim
                    based on the same conduct alleged to breach an implied-in-fact contract
11

12          Musk begins by swiping at a straw man: Defendants do not “contend that . . . any claim

13   based on [the implied covenant] must be duplicative of a breach of contract claim,” or that a

14   “covenant claim must be dismissed whenever there is a breach of contract claim.” Opp. 2, 4.

15   Defendants maintain instead, consistent with black-letter California law and this Court’s order, that

16   an implied covenant claim predicated on the same alleged conduct supporting a breach of contract

17   claim must be dismissed as duplicative. See Guz v. Bechtel Nat’l, Inc., 24 Cal. 4th 317, 352 (2000);

18   Careau & Co. v. Sec. Pac. Bus. Credit, Inc., 222 Cal. App. 3d 1371, 1395 (1990). The same conduct

19   may violate both an agreed term and the implied covenant, but an implied covenant claim will in

20   that case be sustained only if, beyond breaching the contract, the defendant did something that

21   “unfairly frustrates the agreed common purposes and disappoints the reasonable expectations of

22   the other party thereby depriving that party of the benefits of the agreement.” Id. at 1395. Otherwise,

23   “in most cases, a claim for breach of the implied covenant can add nothing to a claim for breach of

24   contract.” Lamke v. Sunstate Equip. Co., LLC, 387 F. Supp. 2d 1044, 1047 (N.D. Cal. 2004).

25          That is especially true where, as here, the alleged contract is implied rather than express.

26   Ordinarily “the covenant is implied as a supplement to the express contractual covenants.” Waller

27   v. Truck Ins. Exch., Inc., 11 Cal. 4th 1, 36 (1995) (emphasis added); see also, e.g., Foley v.

28   Interactive Data Corp., 47 Cal. 3d 654, 690 (1988) (the “covenant of good faith is read into

 1   contracts in order to protect the express covenants or promises of the contract” (emphasis added)).

 2   Musk makes this point, saying the covenant “exists because parties can violate the fundamental

 3   purpose of an agreement while technically adhering to contractual language,” Opp. 1 (emphasis

 4   added), and relying on cases involving express contracts, see id. at 2-7 (citing Carma Devs. (Cal.),

 5   Inc. v. Marathon Dev. Cal., Inc., 2 Cal. 4th 342, 373-74, 376 (1992) (implied covenant claim

 6   dismissed as inconsistent with express contract); Thrifty Payless, Inc. v. The Americana at Brand,

 7   LLC, 218 Cal. App. 4th 1230, 1236 & n.4, 1244 (2013) (allowing implied covenant claim with

 8   express contract breach claim); Locke v. Warner Bros., Inc., 57 Cal. App. 4th 354, 358, 367 (1997)

 9   (allowing implied covenant claim with express contract breach claim); Storek & Storek, Inc. v.

10   Citicorp Real Est., Inc., 100 Cal. App. 4th 44, 58, 64 (2002) (rejecting implied covenant claim

11   alleging bad faith in performing express term)).

12          Because an implied-in-fact contract is “evidenced in part or whole by the parties’ conduct

13   rather than by an express exchange of promises,” Mitsui O.S.K. Lines, Ltd. v. Dynasea Corp., 72

14   Cal. App. 4th 208, 212 (1999), the implied covenant necessarily plays a lesser role in this context.

15   The covenant is meant to track the parties’ “reasonable or justified expectations,” which, “in turn,

16   are to be determined by considering the various factors and circumstances that surround the parties’

17   relationship and thereby shape or give contour to the expectations in the first instance.” 23 Williston

18   on Contracts § 63:22 (4th ed.). Yet those same considerations guide the identification of an implied

19   contract’s terms, see, e.g., Foley, 47 Cal. 3d at 681, rendering an implied covenant inquiry

20   redundant in most cases. See, e.g., Tam v. Qualcomm, Inc., 300 F. Supp. 3d 1130, 1146 (S.D. Cal.

21   2018) (“[A]lleging breach of both an implied-in-fact contract and the implied covenant, for the

22   same term . . . is superfluous and both causes cannot be maintained.”); Sako v. Wells Fargo Bank,

23   Nat’l Ass’n, 2015 WL 5022326, at *6 (S.D. Cal. Aug. 24, 2015) (“a breach of the implied covenant

24   of good faith and fair dealing cannot be based on breach of an implied in fact contract”).

25          But even setting aside this redundancy, the circumstances that will support assertion of an

26   implied covenant claim in any case are limited. To avoid the Guz bar, the implied covenant claim

27   must plead violative conduct different from breach of the contractual term that the plaintiff claims

28   conferred a benefit. So, for example, a producer’s payment of low royalties may or may not violate

 1   a contractual obligation, but an artist states a claim under the implied covenant only if the producer

 2   is alleged to have done something more than withhold the benefit—for instance, manipulating

 3   royalty statements to avoid triggering the higher payment obligation. See Tavares v. Capitol

 4   Records, LLC, 2013 WL 968272, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 12, 2013).

 5          The implied covenant can also be invoked where the contract grants one party unfettered

 6   discretion to deny a counterparty a benefit, or conditions conferral of the benefit on one party’s

 7   subjective judgment. Because strict construction of such a provision would render the contract’s

 8   promise illusory, courts may use the covenant to read in a duty to exercise the discretion or

 9   judgment in subjective good faith. See, e.g., Carma Devs., 2 Cal. 4th at 371-72 (recognizing this

10   application of the covenant); Guz, 24 Cal. 4th at 353 n.18 (allowing that implied covenant could

11   prohibit bad-faith exercise of unfettered discretion to terminate an at-will employee); Locke, 57

12   Cal. App. 4th at 363 (“when it is a condition of an obligor’s duty that he or she be subjectively

13   satisfied with respect to the obligee’s performance, the subjective standard of honest satisfaction is

14   applicable”); Thrifty, 218 Cal. App. 4th at 1236 & n.4, 1244 (allowing implied covenant claim

15   where express term granted landlord “reasonable discretion” to allocate costs); cf. Storek & Storek,

16   100 Cal. App. 4th at 61 (where defendant’s obligation was conditional on “objectively reasonable

17   satisfaction” that project budget was in balance, implied covenant had no role (emphasis added)).

18          In the above-described limited circumstances, the implied covenant fills a gap in the

19   contract’s terms by prohibiting conduct that is not addressed by the contract but undertaken to

20   frustrate the contract’s benefits. Those circumstances do not obtain here, as the SAC makes plain.

21          B.      The only pleaded bases for Musk’s implied covenant claim are coextensive with
                    the bases for his contract claim
22

26   third theory and offers only perfunctory defenses of the first two. No wonder; the pleaded theories

27   are all foreclosed by the Guz rule.

 1    Defendants “fail[ed] to observe a reasonable construction of the terms” of the alleged contract, FAC

 2    ¶ 273, and it fails for the reason the Court already found: the theory is based on the exact same

 3    conduct underlying Musk’s claim that Defendants breached the supposedly “redefined” terms. Take

 4    the example Musk uses: the purported contract term requiring Defendants to “assiduously act to

 5    minimize conflicts of interest.” Opp. 5 (quoting ¶ 247(i)). If this obligation existed and Defendants

 6    did not perform—because they impermissibly “redefined” the obligation, or for any other reason—

 7    then they would be in breach of that term. If the obligation did not exist, or could not be enforced,

 8    then there was no contractual benefit to protect, and any “redefinition” cannot therefore have

 9    violated the implied covenant. The covenant does not operate as a backstop for failed contract

10    claims; it serves to protect the benefits conferred by actual contract terms. See, e.g., Airwair Int’l

11    Ltd. v. Zoetop Bus. Co., Ltd., 2025 WL 1282633, at *5 (N.D. Cal. May 2, 2025).

12           Musk’s efforts to salvage his “redefinition” theory only further expose its infirmity. Musk

13    says the alleged redefinition “differs categorically from breach” and is “bad faith performance.”

14    Opp. 5-6. This is unintelligible. “[B]ad faith performance” does not violate the implied covenant

15    unless a good-faith performance duty is read into an existing contractual provision giving the

16    defendant the right, in its absolute discretion or sole judgment, to withhold a benefit. Storek &

17    Storek, 100 Cal. App. 4th at 58-59, 63. Where, as here, no such provision is alleged, the concept

18    has no relevance. Id. There is no freestanding obligation to act in good faith apart from what the

19    contract requires. See, e.g., Guz, 24 Cal. 4th at 349-50 (the implied covenant cannot “be endowed

20    with an existence independent of its contractual underpinnings”); Foley, 47 Cal. 3d at 690 (covenant

21    does not “protect some general public policy interest not directly tied to the contract’s purposes”).

22           Likewise untenable is Musk’s theory that OpenAI, Inc. and Altman breached the implied

23    covenant by “obscur[ing]” the “for-profit activities” that are alleged to have breached the supposed

24    contract. ¶ 269(b). As Defendants pointed out in their opening brief, concealment of a purported

25    contractual breach is not actionable under the implied covenant where the “core allegation” is the

26    underlying breach. MTD 5-6 (citing Synopsys, Inc. v. Real Intent, Inc., 2024 WL 5374480, at *26

27    (N.D. Cal. Aug. 26, 2024)). Rather than deny this, Musk argues that the alleged concealment here

28    independently “violated the fundamental expectation inherent in charitable giving—that fiduciaries

14           The Ninth Circuit has long held that “[p]laintiffs cannot claim that a conspiracy to violate

15    RICO existed if they do not adequately plead a substantive violation of RICO.” Sanford v.

16    MemberWorks, Inc., 625 F.3d 550, 559 (9th Cir. 2010); see, e.g., Turner v. Cook, 362 F.3d 1219,

17    1231 n.17 (9th Cir. 2004); Howard, 208 F.3d at 751; Religious Tech. Ctr. v. Wollersheim, 971 F.2d

18    364, 367 n.8 (9th Cir. 1992). And district courts, including this Court, regularly apply the rule. E.g.,

19    Dkt. 163 at 10 (“Because the motions as to the underlying RICO offenses are granted, the Court

20    also grants the motions to dismiss” the RICO conspiracy count); Verde Media Corp. v. Levi, 2015

21    WL 374934, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 28, 2015). Plaintiffs did not dispute the rule when the OpenAI

22    Defendants moved to dismiss the FAC. See Dkt. 128 at 18-19. Yet now, wielding a single line from

23    Howard and case law about proof rather than pleading standards, they contend that Sanford

24    misstated the law and that pleading “an agreement alone is sufficient.” Opp. 18.7 That is flat wrong.

      adequately pled violations of § 1962(a)-(c), the FAC properly states a claim for RICO conspiracy

 1           The holding of Howard, stated no fewer than three times in the opinion, is that “the failure

 2    to adequately plead a substantive violation of RICO precludes a claim for conspiracy.” 208 F.3d at

 3    751; see also id. (“Plaintiffs cannot claim that a conspiracy to violate RICO existed if they do not

 4    adequately plead a substantive violation of RICO.”); id. (“[T]he failure to allege substantive

 5    violations precludes their claim that there was a conspiracy to violate RICO.”). Oki Semiconductor

 6    Co. v. Wells Fargo Bank, which Plaintiffs cite, did not purport to change this pleading rule, did not

 7    address the rule at all, and indeed did not reach the adequacy of the conspiracy allegations in that

 8    case. Instead, it assumed the existence of a RICO conspiracy, 298 F.3d 768, 775 (9th Cir. 2002),

 9    and dismissed the plaintiff’s claim on an unrelated issue of vicarious liability, id. at 777. The line

10    that Plaintiffs quote—that under § 1962(d) “it is not necessary to prove any substantive RICO

11    violations ever occurred as a result of the conspiracy,” id. at 774-75—is on its face about proof of

12    the object’s completion, not pleading of the object.

13           Finally, Salinas v. United States, which Plaintiffs also cite, supports rather than undercuts

14    that a RICO conspiracy count must plead the substantive RICO violation that is identified as the

15    conspiracy’s object. The issue in Salinas was whether, “under the RICO conspiracy provision, the

16    defendant must himself commit or agree to commit two or more predicate acts.” 522 U.S. 52, 61

17    (1997) (emphasis added). The answer was no, but the Supreme Court reaffirmed that the object of

18    a conspiracy—what the conspirators intend to accomplish—must be “an endeavor which, if

19    completed, would satisfy all of the elements of a substantive criminal offense.” Id. at 65 (emphasis

20    added); accord Howard, 208 F.3d at 751 (quoting this language). Indeed, the Court upheld the

21    defendant’s RICO conspiracy conviction in that case because there was “ample evidence that he

22    conspired to violate subsection (c).” Salinas, 522 U.S. at 65 (emphasis added).

23           To sustain their RICO conspiracy claim, Plaintiffs were required under longstanding law to

24    plead the substantive RICO offense. Having failed to do so, their claim must be dismissed.
