 1                            STATEMENT OF ISSUES TO BE DECIDED

 2          1.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Sherman Act Section 1 claim (Count 9) and their Cartwright Act

 3   Section 16720 claim (Count 11) should be dismissed for failure to adequately plead a per se

 4   unlawful restraint, a Rule of Reason violation, or for lack of concerted action.

 5          2.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Sherman Act Section 2 claim (Count 10) should be dismissed

 6   for failure to adequately plead sufficient market power or exclusionary conduct, including

 7   regarding any investment agreement, exclusive supply or distribution agreement, or below-cost

 8   pricing followed by a recoupment period featuring supra-competitive pricing.

 9          3.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Unfair Practices Act claim (Count 12) should be dismissed for
10   failure to adequately plead below-cost pricing, a loss leader, or a secret payment, allowance, or

11   unearned discount.

12          4.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Clayton Act Section 3 claim (Count 13) should be dismissed for

13   failure to adequately plead either an agreement relating to tangible goods or commodities, or a

14   foreclosure of competition in a substantial share of any line of commerce affected.

15          5.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Cartwright Act Section 16727 claim (Count 14) should be

16   dismissed for failure to adequately plead substantial foreclosure of any relevant market.

17          6.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Clayton Act Section 7 claim (Count 15) should be dismissed for

18   failure to adequately plead an appreciable danger or reasonable probability of an anticompetitive

19   merger, any acquisition by OpenAI of Microsoft assets, or that Microsoft acquired OpenAI assets
20   through providing a service or by acquiring a non-exclusive license to OpenAI intellectual property.

21          7.      Whether Plaintiffs’ Clayton Act Section 8 claim (Count 16) should be dismissed for

22   failure to adequately plead a violation of the statute given that Templeton was not a Microsoft

23   director or officer at the relevant time, that she was not an OpenAI director or officer, that Hoffman

24   resigned from the OpenAI board within the statutory safe harbor, that no current board interlock

25   exists, or that there is a cognizable danger of a recurrent board interlock.

26          8.      Whether Plaintiffs’ antitrust claims (Count 9-16) should be dismissed for the

27   additional, independent reasons that Plaintiffs have not adequately pled antitrust injury, and that

28   Musk lacks antitrust standing.

 1           9.     Whether Plaintiffs’ California Unfair Competition Law claim (Count 17) should be

 2    dismissed for failure to adequately plead any unlawful prong, unfair prong, or fraudulent prong

 3    violation, or that Plaintiffs have lost any money or property due to any alleged violation by the

 4    Microsoft Defendants.

 5           10.    Whether Plaintiffs’ California Unfair Competition Law claim (Count 17) should be

 6    dismissed for the additional, independent reason that, to the extent it incorporates Counts 8, 25,

 7    and 26, Plaintiffs have failed to plead fraud or aiding and abetting any fraud with the particularity

 8    required by Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b).

 9           11.    Whether Musk’s quasi-contract claim (Count 4) should be dismissed for failure to
10    adequately plead a quasi-contract or that Microsoft benefited from its allegedly wrongful conduct.

11           12.    Whether Musk’s claim for tortious interference with contract (Count 5) should be

12    dismissed for failure to adequately plead a valid contract, Microsoft’s knowledge of it, Microsoft’s

13    intentional inducement of a breach, an actual breach, or resulting damage.

14           13.    Whether Musk’s aiding and abetting claims (Counts 8, 21) and Musk’s and Zillis’

15    aiding and abetting claim (Count 24) should be dismissed for failure to adequately plead an

16    underlying tortious act by a third party, Microsoft’s knowledge that the act constituted a wrong,

17    or substantial assistance or encouragement to so act.

18           14.    Whether Count 8 should be dismissed for the additional, independent reason that

19    Plaintiffs have failed to adequately plead fraud or aiding and abetting any fraud with the
20    particularity required by Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b).

21           15.    Whether Counts 4, 5, 8, 21, and 24 should be dismissed for the additional,

22    independent reason that Plaintiffs lack standing.

23           16.    Whether Plaintiffs’ claims for violations of RICO Sections 1962(a)-(d) (Counts 25-

24    26) should be dismissed for failure to adequately plead predicate acts with the necessary

25    particularity, conduct of an enterprise, or any facts specific to Microsoft in support of the claims.

 1    III.   LEGAL STANDARD

 2           “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter,

 3    accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556

 4    U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)).

 5    “Threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory

 6    statements, do not suffice.” Id. An antitrust claim must also be economically plausible. See

 7    Twombly, 550 U.S. at 565.

 8           In addition, any claim grounded in allegations of fraud or false representations must

 9    satisfy Rule 9(b)’s heightened pleading standard. The complaint must plead with particularity
10    “what is false or misleading about a statement, and why it is false.” Ebeid ex rel. United States

11    v. Lungwitz, 616 F.3d 993, 998 (9th Cir. 2010) (citation omitted). And it must be “specific

12    enough to give defendants notice of the particular misconduct which is alleged to constitute the

13    fraud charged so that they can defend against the charge and not just deny that they have done

14    anything wrong.” Semegen v. Weidner, 780 F.2d 727, 731 (9th Cir. 1985).

21    “specific intent.” Paladin Assocs., Inc. v. Mont. Power Co., 328 F.3d 1145, 1158 (9th Cir. 2003).

                           24)
 7

 8           Musk asserts several aiding and abetting claims against Microsoft alleging that Microsoft

 9    assisted OpenAI to commit fraud and breach fiduciary duties to Musk. (Zillis joins Count 24.)
10    Such claims require an underlying tortious act by a third party, knowledge that the act constituted

11    a wrong, and substantial assistance or encouragement to so act. See Marcelos v. Dominguez, 2008

12    WL 2788173, at *8 (N.D. Cal. July 18, 2008). The FAC does not adequately plead any of these

13    elements. Count 8 (aiding and abetting fraud) summarily asserts that Microsoft knew that Altman

14    and Brockman were scheming to defraud Musk, because “on information and belief,” Microsoft

15    conducted due diligence requiring review of certain OpenAI documents and knew the conditions

16    of Musk’s donations. FAC ¶ 323. Such “[b]oilerplate and conclusory allegations are insufficient

17    to establish that a [defendant] had actual knowledge” of a third party’s breach of fiduciary duty.

18    Namer v. Bank of Am., N.A., 2017 WL 1180193, at *5 (S.D. Cal. Mar. 30, 2017). Merely alleging

19    that an investigation by a third party “would have set off alarm bells” does not plead knowledge
20    of wrongdoing. Lorenz v. E. W. Bancorp, Inc., 2016 WL 199392, at *8 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 14, 2016).

21           Even worse are the FAC’s generalized allegations of substantial assistance. FAC ¶ 325.

22    These fall short of meeting Rule 9(b)’s heightened pleading standard for fraud claims, which

23    require plaintiffs to specifically plead the “who, what, when, where, and how of the misconduct

24    charged.” Kearns v. Ford Motor Co., 567 F.3d 1120, 1126 (9th Cir. 2009); see also Impac Funding

25    Corp. v. Endresen, 2015 WL 13916649, at *4 (C.D. Cal. Dec. 16, 2015) (applying the Rule 9(b)

26    standard to aiding and abetting fraud claim). Although the FAC alleges that the For-Profit Entities

27    and Microsoft “provid[ed] the corporate entities necessary to loot OpenAI,” “draft[ed] and

28    execut[ed] exclusive licensing and other contracts contrary to the purpose of OpenAI,” and

 1                  1.      The FAC Does Not Allege with Particularity Predicate Offenses by
                            Microsoft
 2

 3           The RICO statute requires plaintiffs to plead “(1) conduct (2) of an enterprise (3) through

 4    a pattern (4) of racketeering activity (known as ‘predicate acts’) (5) causing injury to plaintiff’s

 5    business or property.” Coronavirus Rep. v. Apple, Inc., 85 F.4th 948, 958 (9th Cir. 2023) (citation

 6    omitted). When the pattern of racketeering activity alleged is based on wire fraud, plaintiffs must

 7    plead, with heightened particularity, “(A) the formation of a scheme to defraud, (B) the use of the

 8    . . . wires in furtherance of that scheme, and (C) the specific intent to defraud.” Eclectic Props. E.,

 9    LLC v. Marcus & Millichap Co., 751 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2014). Plaintiffs cannot rely on vague

10    group pleading to discharge this burden. See id. In other words, they cannot lump parties together

11    as “defendants” without specifying who did what. They must specifically allege at least two

12    predicate acts by Microsoft and its intent to defraud. See id.

13           Plaintiffs base their RICO claim on alleged wire fraud predicate offenses—emails between

14    Altman, Brockman, and Musk involving “repeated misrepresentations” to induce Musk’s

15    “financial and other contributions” to OpenAI, and allegedly false online marketing statements.

16    FAC ¶¶ 486-87, 493. As detailed in Section II.C of OpenAI’s Motion to Dismiss, Plaintiffs have

17    not sufficiently pled wire fraud as a RICO predicate against any Defendant, much less with respect

18    to Microsoft. Plaintiffs shoehorn Microsoft into their prior complaint against Altman and

19    Brockman, merely appending “and Microsoft” to these allegations without a single new “fact”

20    about Microsoft that satisfies the requirement to plead predicate acts by Microsoft.15 This does not

21    suffice, especially under Rule 9(b)’s heightened standard. The predicate act allegations of fraud

22    must identify “the role of each defendant in each scheme,” which Plaintiffs fail to do. Moore v.

23    Kayport Package Exp., Inc., 885 F.2d 531, 541 (9th Cir. 1989); see also McLaughlin v. Anderson,

24    962 F.2d 187, 192 (2d Cir. 1992) (“the bare minimum of a RICO charge is that a defendant

25    personally committed or aided and abetted the commission of two predicate acts”).

26           Further, Plaintiffs rely on vague group pleading to implicate Microsoft in actions allegedly
