 8          OpenAI argues that, by seeking an order directing any proceeds to the OpenAI charity, Musk

18 (recognizing authority to direct that trust assets “be transferred to another corporation, organization,

19 society, or trust so that the original trust purposes can be carried out”); Restatement (Third) of Trusts

20 § 67 (2003). Exercising that authority here does not make the suit derivative merely because

21 charitable assets are being transferred back to the charity itself.2

     III.   OPENAI’S ARGUMENTS ON THE MERITS OF PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS FOR
 3          EQUITABLE RELIEF ARE PREMATURE

 4          OpenAI’s remaining arguments focus on the merits of the injunctive relief Musk seeks.

 5 Those arguments are as meritless as they are premature. If the jury finds Defendants liable at trial,

 6 there will be time enough in post-trial proceedings to make submissions over what the Court should

 7 order. There is no reason to address those issues now. But if the Court is nonetheless inclined to

 8 do so, it should have no difficulty rejecting all of OpenAI’s arguments.

 9          Prohibitory Injunction. OpenAI urges that an injunction requiring it to comply with its

10 charitable obligations would be “exorbitant” because it would require the Court to monitor

11 compliance on an ongoing basis. But California law expressly contemplates such remedies by

12 authorizing courts to “enjoin” breaches of charitable trust. Cal. Corp. Code § 5142(a). Oversight

13 mechanisms to ensure a charity complies with its obligations are thus routine features of charitable

14 trust litigation. See, e.g., People v. Wounded Warriors Support Grp., No. RG-17-856929, 2018 WL

15 6581211, at *1-2 (Cal. Super. Ct. Nov. 6, 2018) (ordering “[a]ppointment of a receiver to manage

16 the affairs of [defendant charities]”); L.B. Rsch. & Educ. Found. v. UCLA Found., 130 Cal. App.

17 4th 171, 176 (2005) (seeking specific performance of charitable endowment); Smithers v. St. Luke’s-

18 Roosevelt Hosp. Ctr., 281 A.D.2d 127, 133 (N.Y. App. Div. 2001) (same); cf. Melendres v. Skinner,

19 113 F.4th 1126, 1136 (9th Cir. 2024). The Court will presumably wait until post-trial proceedings

20 to fashion appropriate relief that ensures that OpenAI complies with its charitable obligations

21 without entangling itself in OpenAI’s operations. But the suggestion that a court of equity lacks

22 power to compel a charity to do what it promised has no support in the law.

23          Removal from Office. OpenAI argues that Plaintiff cannot seek Altman or Brockman’s
24 removal from office because California Corporations Code § 5223 authorizes only directors,

25 members, or the Attorney General to seek removal of a charity’s directors. But Plaintiff is not

26 relying on that statute. Instead, Plaintiff invokes Section 5142, which grants the Court broad

27 authority “to enjoin, correct, obtain damages for or to otherwise remedy a breach of a charitable

28 trust.” Cal. Corp. Code § 5142(a) (emphasis added). OpenAI cites no authority holding that Section
