 8   74)—a day after the already-extended reply deadline (Dkt. No. 68).

 9   I.     Plaintiffs’ new declarations are untimely and should be stricken

10          Courts in the Ninth Circuit “recognize the potential inequities that might flow from the

11   injection of new [factual] matter” on reply. Dutta v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 895 F.3d 1166,

12   1171-72 (9th Cir. 2018). “New evidence submitted as part of a reply is improper because it does

13   not allow the defendant an adequate opportunity to respond.” Stiner v. Brookdale Senior Living,

14   Inc., 665 F. Supp. 3d 1150, 1179 (N.D. Cal. 2023) (cleaned up); see also Contratto v. Ethicon, Inc.,

15   227 F.R.D. 304, 308 n.5 (N.D. Cal. 2005).

16          Local Rule 7-3(d) thus provides a remedy—among other relief, the court may “simply

17   strik[e] the new matter from consideration” if it was available at the time of party’s initial filing.

18   See Dutta, 895 F.3d at 1172; see also Townsend v. Monster Beverage Corp., 303 F. Supp. 3d 1010,

19   1027-28 (C.D. Cal. 2018) (striking “new evidence presented in [a] [r]eply” where plaintiffs could

20   have disclosed the evidence “two months” prior); Citcon USA, LLC v. RiverPay Inc., 2019 WL

21   2603219, at *3 (N.D. Cal. June 25, 2019) (disregarding declaration in support of preliminary

22   injunction as “impermissible new evidence submitted on a reply brief in violation of Civil Local

23   Rule 7-3(d)(1)”); BoomerangIt, Inc. v. ID Armor, Inc., 2012 WL 2368466, *4 n.1 (N.D. Cal. June

24   21, 2012) (same on temporary injunction motion); Acosta, Inc. v. Kerdman Enters, G.P., 2008 WL

25   11336210, at *8 (C.D. Cal. June 2, 2008) (“It is well established that new arguments and evidence

26   presented for the first time in reply are waived.” (cleaned up)).

27          Plaintiffs’ new declarations should be stricken pursuant to this settled law as they all contain

28   purported “evidence”—in the form of unsupported conclusory statements—that could have been

 1   II.    The declarations are unreliable and inadmissible and should be given no weight

 2          Even had the declarations been timely filed, four of them—from Baker, Birchall, Gracias,

 3   and Musk—are riddled with unreliable hearsay and “information and belief” that should be

 4   afforded no weight. See Miyoko’s Kitchen v. Ross, 2020 WL 8361994, at *5 n.5 (N.D. Cal. Aug.

 5   21, 2020) (though hearsay “may . . . be taken into account for the purposes of a preliminary

 6   injunction, it need not be given weight where . . . it does not seem particularly reliable”); Flynt

 7   Distrib. Co., Inc. v. Harvey, 734 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir. 1984) (trial court considering

 8   preliminary injunction may give “inadmissible” hearsay “some weight,” but only “when to do so

 9   serves the purpose of preventing irreparable harm before trial”).

10          Gracias and Baker declare—with no support at all—that it was alleged “common

11   knowledge” that OpenAI requested funding exclusivity in its October 2024 funding round. See

12   Gracias Decl. ¶ 3; Baker Decl. ¶ 3. These declarants do not claim to have heard anything from

13   OpenAI directly, nor do they even identify anyone who did. And their observations are offered on

14   “information and belief” (Gracias Decl. ¶ 4) or “understanding and belief” (Baker Decl. ¶ 4), rather

15   than on personal knowledge of any kind. The evidence is plainly inadmissible hearsay, bears no

16   indicia of reliability, and should be accorded no weight. See Imagine Studios, LLC v. Robeji

17   Designs, LLC, 2012 WL 12882705, at *2 (C.D. Cal. Oct. 26, 2012) (“self-serving declaration”

18   containing “uncorroborated” “hearsay” based on “no personal knowledge of the facts” accorded

19   “no weight” on preliminary injunction motion); see also 11A C. Wright & A. Miller, Fed. Prac. &

20   Proc. Civ. § 2949 (3d ed. 2024) (“[W]hen the primary evidence introduced is an affidavit made on

21   information and belief rather than on personal knowledge, it generally is considered insufficient to

22   support a motion for a preliminary injunction.”) (collecting cases).

23          While Birchall at least claims he heard something from a specific person about statements

24   Altman purportedly made, see Birchall Decl. ¶¶ 3, 5, he refuses to identify the source, id. ¶ 6—

25   rendering his inadmissible “evidence” just as unreliable as the “information and belief” from Baker

26   and Gracias. Prasad v. Cnty. of San Mateo, 2024 WL 457811, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 6, 2024)

27   (hearsay from “unidentified” third party could not justify temporary restraining order, where there

28   was no compelling basis to “consider” it).

 1          Finally, Musk’s “evidence” on this topic is of no better—and in many respects worse—

 2   quality. Musk claims to have been “informed” by unidentified sources of statements made by

 3   Altman, and says he “believe[s]” based on conversations with unidentified “other market

 4   participants,” that other unidentified “investors” themselves “believe” that OpenAI “may deny

 5   allocations to those who invest in xAI and other competitors of OpenAI.” Musk Decl. ¶¶ 3-4. This

 6   unattributed hearsay is at least three layers deep. As for Musk’s fact-free and conclusory assertion

 7   that he is “deeply concerned” about “OpenAI and Microsoft’s obvious sharing of competitively

 8   sensitive information,” that is likewise rooted in pure speculation, rather than personal knowledge,

 9   and lacks any foundation. See Musk Decl. ¶ 9.

10          The “evidence” Plaintiffs seek to offer through these declarants is thus fatally defective, and

11   certainly cannot form a basis for the extraordinary relief sought.

12   III.   If the Court is inclined to consider any of Plaintiffs’ newly-submitted declarations, the
            OpenAI Defendants should be permitted to respond
13

14          In the alternative—and only to the extent the Court finds it appropriate to consider

15   Plaintiffs’ new declarations—the OpenAI Defendants request leave to file a brief surreply (not to

16   exceed five pages) to respond to the newly-filed declarations. See Dutta, 895 F.3d at 1171 (“[Civil

17   Local] Rule 7-3(d) provides the aggrieved party with the opportunity to object to the district court’s

18   consideration of the newly submitted evidence or to request leave to file a sur-reply opposition to

19   it.”); Provenz v. Miller, 102 F.3d 1478, 1483 (9th Cir. 1996) (“[W]here new evidence is presented

20   in a reply . . . the district court should not consider the new evidence without giving the non-movant

21   an opportunity to respond.” (cleaned up)); Balsam Brands Inc. v. Cinmar, LLC, 2015 WL 7015417,

22   at *4 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 12, 2015) (untimely evidence could “only [be] properly considered” if the

23   non-movant was “given an opportunity to respond”). The surreply will explain why the

24   declarations, even if considered, do not support injunctive relief on any of Plaintiffs’ claims.
