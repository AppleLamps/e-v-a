                                   3           No. 3: To Exclude Expert Testimony from Dr. C. Paul Wazzan Related to
                                               Calculations of Wrongful Gains Attributable to Microsoft
                                   4

                                   5           The motion is DENIED.

                                   6           Microsoft seeks to exclude testimony by Musk’s damages expert, Dr. C. Paul Wazzan,

                                   7   under Federal Rules of Evidence 702 and 403 for failing to calculate what portion of Microsoft’s

                                   8   gains are attributable to Microsoft’s alleged misconduct versus that of OpenAI. Microsoft argues

                                   9   that this renders Dr. Wazzan’s methodology unreliable as applied to Microsoft and irrelevant to

                                  10   Musk’s theory of Microsoft’s liability. In opposition, Musk argues that Dr. Wazzan is not required

                                  11   to apportion wrongful gains derived specifically from Microsoft’s misconduct because aiding and

                                  12   abetting is a theory of secondary liability for OpenAI’s primary wrong. The Court agrees with
Northern District of California
 United States District Court




                                  13   Musk.

                                  14           First, aiding and abetting liability is not limited to harm caused by the aiding and abetting

                                  15   itself. Rather, “[i]t is a basis on which a defendant is held liable for the tort that was aided and

                                  16   abetted, though some or all of the elements of the tort were fulfilled by the conduct of another.”

                                  17   Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liab. for Econ. Harm § 28 cmt. a (2020). “The defendant is subject

                                  18   to ‘secondary’ liability for what the primary wrongdoer has done.” Id. Thus, the defendant is “held

                                  19   to account . . . for harm to which the aiding and abetting made a contribution.” Id. cmt. e; see

                                  20   Neilson v. Union Bank of California, N.A., 290 F.Supp.2d 1101, 1134 (C.D. Cal. 2003) (“[T]he act

                                  21   of aiding and abetting is distinct from the primary violation; liability attaches because the aider

                                  22   and abettor behaves in a manner that enables the primary violator to commit the underlying tort.”).

                                  23   Here, the jury can be asked to resolve this factual issue.

                                  24           Second, the “profit for which the wrongdoer is liable . . . is the net increase in the assets of

                                  25   the wrongdoer, to the extent that this increase is attributable to the underlying wrong.”

                                  26   Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 51 cmt. e (2011). American Master

                                  27   Lease LLC v. Idanta Partners, Ltd., 225 Cal.App.4th 1451 (2014), is instructive. There, the court

                                  28   held that aiding and abetting defendants that purchased stock in a company whose founders
                                                                                           8
                                       Case 4:24-cv-04722-YGR           Document 452         Filed 03/25/26      Page 9 of 12




                                   1   violated their fiduciary duties to the plaintiff were properly “subject to disgorgement of the profit

                                   2   or ‘net increase in the assets’ they obtained” from their stock purchase. Id. at 1486.

                                   3          Lastly, Musk contends that all of Microsoft’s investments in OpenAI caused Musk harm

                                   4   by assisting OpenAI in breaching its fiduciary duties to Musk. (See Dkt. No. 396 at 13.) As noted

                                   5   above, this is the factual issue at play. The jury will be asked to determine which investments, if

                                   6   any, caused the relevant harm.

                                   7          As such, Dr. Wazzan’s methodology is not unreliable or “contrary to law” by assuming

                                   8   that Microsoft’s gains on those investments are attributable to OpenAI’s breach and then

                                   9   apportioning Musk’s stake in those profits that flow from his contributions to OpenAI. Neither

                                  10   does it risk confusing issues, misleading the jury, or unfair prejudice. For these reasons,

                                  11   Microsoft’s motion is denied.

                                  13          Next, the Court addresses defendants’ motion to exclude opinions three through six of Dr.

                                  14   Wazzan. (Dkt. No. 393.) 3 Defendants argue those opinions are inadmissible under Federal Rule of

                                  15   Evidence 702 and the cases applying it, including Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.,

                                  16   509 U.S. 579 (1993).

                                  17          Federal Rule of Evidence 702 permits an expert’s opinion testimony if the witness is

                                  18   qualified and based upon that qualification, the witness’s opinion is relevant and reliable.

                                  19   Reliability turns on “the soundness of [the expert’s] methodology.” Daubert v. Merrell Dow

                                  20   Pharms., Inc., 43 F.3d 1311, 1318 (9th Cir. 1995); Engilis v. Monsanto Co., 151 F.4th 1040, 1048

                                  21   (9th Cir. 2025) (“[T]he reliability test may be applied to an expert’s reasoning process.”).

                                  22   “[J]udges are entitled to broad discretion when discharging their gatekeeping function” under Rule

                                  23   702. United States v. Hankey, 203 F.3d 1160, 1168 (9th Cir. 2000).

                                  24          Defendants do not challenge Dr. Wazzan’s qualifications. Rather, they primarily challenge

                                  25   the methodology underlying Dr. Wazzan’s third opinion, which estimates that fifty to seventy-five

                                   2   then sought “to quantify the portion of Defendants’ profits or enrichment that may fairly be

                                   3   attributed to Mr. Musk’s Contributions.” (Id. ¶ 8.) Damages experts are “entitled to assume

                                   4   liability in order to model the damages.” Lin v. Solta Med., Inc., 2024 WL 5199905, at *5 (N.D.

                                   9   defendants liable for some, but not all, of Musk’s claims, or that defendants wrongfully profited

                                  10   from some, but not all, of Musk’s contributions, Dr. Wazzan’s testimony may still aid in deciding

                                  11   an appropriate award. See In re Exxon Valdez, 270 F.3d 1215, 1248 (9th Cir. 2001) (“Reasonable

                                  12   jurors . . . may make their own reasonable judgment on the evidence, accepting part, all, or none of

 United States District Court




                                  13   any witness’s testimony.”). Moreover, “[p]roof of causation often comes from fact witnesses, and

                                  14   it is appropriate for expert witnesses to assume causation will be established and then proceed to

                                  25   defendants’ enrichment” from investments that aided and abetted). Whether this methodology
