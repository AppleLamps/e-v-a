16                                           BACKGROUND
17          Elon Musk alleges that Altman, Brockman, and several OpenAI entities deceived him into
18   supporting OpenAI, Inc., a non-profit organization dedicated to researching and developing safe
19   and beneficial artificial intelligence for the benefit of humanity. Compl. ¶ 1. Musk claims that he
20   contributed over $44 million to the non-profit based on promises made to him about open-sourcing
21   of OpenAI’s technology and use of that technology for private gain. Id. ¶¶ 1, 82. Claiming “perfidy
22   and deceit . . . of Shakespearean proportions,” id. ¶ 2, Musk alleges that the OpenAI Defendants
23   broke those promises by, among other things, failing to open-source OpenAI’s most recent
24   technology and placing it behind a paywall, licensing its advanced technology, granting a non-
25   voting board seat to a for-profit entity (Microsoft), and engaging in self-dealing. Id. ¶ 250. Musk
26   brings 15 claims that fall into six categories: (1) contract claims (Counts VI, VII, VIII, XIV),
27   (2) fraud claims (Counts I, III, IX), (3) claims under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt
28   Organizations Act (“RICO”), 18 U.S.C. § 1962 (Counts IV, V), (4) claims for breach of fiduciary

 3                                                 ANALYSIS

 4             “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter,

 5   accepted as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.

 6   662, 678 (2009) (quotation marks omitted). Under this standard, “[t]hreadbare recitals of the

 7   elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id.

 8   Pleading fraud requires an account of “the time, place, and specific content of the false

 9   representations,” as well as “what is false or misleading about [the] statement, and why it is false.”

10   Yetter v. Ford Motor Co., 428 F. Supp. 3d 210, 219-20 (N.D. Cal. 2019) (first quoting Swartz v.

11   KPMG LLP, 476 F.3d 756, 764 (9th Cir. 2007) (per curiam), then quoting Ebeid ex rel. U.S. v.

12   Lungwitz, 616 F.3d 993, 998 (9th Cir. 2010)); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b); Smith v.

13   GlaxoSmithKline Consumer Healthcare Holdings (US) LLC, 660 F. Supp. 3d 863, 875 (N.D. Cal.

14   2023). None of Musk’s claims pass muster.

15     I.      CONTRACT CLAIMS

16             The complaint does not state a claim for breach of express contract (Count VI). To plead an

17   express contract, a plaintiff must (a) attach the writing to the complaint or quote its text; or (b) plead

18   in detail the writing’s “legal effect.” Heritage Pac. Fin., LLC v. Monroy, 215 Cal. App. 4th 972,

19   993 (2013). In either case, the essential elements of a contract claim—(1) mutual assent to the terms

20   alleged to have been breached and (2) bargained-for consideration—must be apparent from the

21   express terms of the writing itself. See Pac. Bay Recovery, Inc. v. Cal. Physicians’ Servs., Inc., 12

22   Cal. App. 5th 200, 215-16 (2017).

23             Musk attaches no writing to the complaint, nor does he attempt to recite the terms of the

24   contract verbatim. Instead, Musk invokes his “express written correspondence in 2015” with

25   Altman (Compl. ¶ 248), but identifies no language therein constituting an undertaking to Musk to

26   (a) open-source all technology “subject only to genuine safety considerations”; (b) not license

27   certain technology to Microsoft; (c) refrain from granting Microsoft a board observer seat;

28   (d) refrain from charging for use of any technology; (e) make no business deals with entities in

 1   which Altman has an interest; or (f) never contemplate a corporate reorganization. See id. ¶¶ 248,

 2   250. This failure to plead mutual assent to the purported contract’s terms is fatal. So is Musk’s

 3   failure to plausibly allege that his contributions to OpenAI were an “inducement to the promisor,”

 4   as necessary to plead bargained-for consideration. Cal. Civ. Code § 1605. Without these two

 5   foundational elements of contract formation, Musk cannot sustain this claim.

 6             Musk’s claim for breach of implied contract (Count VII) fails for the same reasons. Zenith

 7   Ins. Co. v. O’Connor, 148 Cal. App. 4th 998, 1010 (2007) (“[a]n implied contract in no less degree

 8   than an express contract, must be founded upon an ascertained agreement of the parties to perform

 9   it,” requiring the same showing of mutual and bargained-for consideration (cleaned up)). Musk

10   does not plausibly allege conduct evincing mutual assent or bargained-for consideration or even

11   identify with specificity the conduct that allegedly gave rise to his implied contract. See Haskins v.

12   Symantec Corp., 2014 WL 2450996, at *4 (N.D. Cal. June 2, 2024).

13             Musk’s implied covenant and tortious interference claims (Counts VIII, XIV) likewise fail

14   because they are predicated on the existence of an enforceable contract, and none has been pleaded.

15   The implied covenant claim fails for the additional, independent reason that it is “based on the same

16   conduct as [Musk’s] breach of implied contract” claim and is thus “impermissibly duplicative.”

17   Westron v. Zoom Video Commc’ns, Inc., 2023 WL 3149262, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 15, 2023). And

18   Musk’s own allegations undermine his tortious interference claim: Under California law, an agent

19   cannot interfere with its principal’s contract, see Mintz v. Blue Cross of Cal., 172 Cal. App. 4th

20   1594, 1604, 1607 (2009), and Musk alleges, on the face of the complaint, that Altman created the

21   OpenAI For-Profit Entities (the defendants to this count) as agents of his fraud, see ¶ 190. As a

22   result, these Entities cannot be sued for tortious interference under settled law. In any event, this

23   claim is inadequately pleaded, as the complaint offers only conclusory allegations that the OpenAI

24   For-Profit Entities committed “intentional acts designed to induce a breach or disruption” of

25   Musk’s purported “contractual relationship.” Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc., 9 Cal. 5th 1130,

26   1141 (2020).

27    II.      FRAUD CLAIMS

28             Musk impermissibly tries to repackage his allegations of breach of contract as claims of

 1   fraud. Rather than assert that Defendants made and then broke enforceable promises, Musk says

 2   those same promises were made to him with no present intention to fulfill them and therefore

 3   constituted fraud. See Compl. ¶¶ 149-70, 200-06, 276. But a failed contract claim cannot be so

 4   easily repurposed. See, e.g., JMP Secs. LLP v. Altair Nanotechnologies Inc., 880 F. Supp. 2d 1029,

 5   1043-44 (N.D. Cal. 2012) (dismissing fraud claim where plaintiff attempted to take “allegations

 6   underpinning a straightforward claim for breach of a commercial contract and recast them as [a]

 7   tort[]”). These claims—for promissory fraud (Count I), aiding and abetting fraud (Count III), and

 8   unjust enrichment (Count IX)—fail for the fundamental reason that Musk does not identify any

 9   promise that was made to him or any facts suggesting that any such promise was made with

10   fraudulent intent, let alone plead these facts with the particularity required under Federal Rule of

11   Civil Procedure 9(b). See Behnke v. State Farm Gen. Ins. Co., 196 Cal. App. 4th 1443, 1453, 1459

12   (2011); UMG Recordings, Inc. v. Glob. Eagle Ent., Inc., 117 F. Supp. 3d 1092, 1108 (C.D. Cal.

13   2015).

14             Musk’s claim for aiding and abetting fraud (Count III) is deficient for two additional

15   reasons. First, he fails to allege the OpenAI For-Profit Entities’ “substantial assistance” in the

16   purported underlying fraud with “heightened specificity.” McGraw Co. v. Aegis Gen. Ins. Agency,

17   Inc., 2016 WL 3745063, at *6 (N.D. Cal. July 13, 2016). Second, as Musk elsewhere alleges that

18   Altman and Brockman are agents of the OpenAI For-Profit Entities or vice versa (Compl. ¶ 190),

19   this claim is barred by the agency immunity rule, see Villains, Inc. v. Am. Econ. Ins. Co., 870 F.

20   Supp. 2d 792, 795-96 (N.D. Cal. 2012) (under the “agency immunity rule,” an “agent is, in effect,

21   immune from liability” because “a principal cannot aid and abet itself”).

22   III.      RICO CLAIMS

23             Musk’s RICO claims (Counts IV and V) are likewise untenable in light of his failure to

24   plead fraud.

25             The complaint attempts to plead a pattern of racketeering activity based on wire fraud,

26   requiring Musk to plead with particularity: “(A) the formation of a scheme to defraud, (B) the use

27   of the . . . wires in furtherance of that scheme, and (C) the specific intent to defraud.” Eclectic

28   Props. E., LLC v. Marcus & Millichap Co., 751 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2014). But Musk identifies

 1   no particular representation by any Defendant that was false when made, nor does he allege facts

 2   establishing an intent to defraud—that is, any Defendant’s knowledge of falsity at the time any

 3   alleged representation was made. Musk therefore does not plead cognizable predicate acts.

 4            Separately, the complaint fails to plead that the purportedly fraudulent acts committed by

 5   the Defendants named in these counts constituted the “conduct” “of an enterprise”—that is, of

 6   OpenAI, Inc. Cf. Ferrari v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, 2016 WL 7188030, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Dec.

 7   12, 2016). The alleged “enterprise” cannot “simply be the same ‘person’ referred to by a different

 8   name,” as “RICO enterprise liability ‘depends on showing that the defendants conducted or

 9   participated in the conduct of the ‘enterprise’s affairs,’ not just their own affairs.’” Id. (emphasis

10   omitted) (quoting Cedric Kushner Promotions, Ltd. v. King, 533 U.S. 158, 163 (2001)). Yet, in

11   support of his RICO claim, Musk identifies only acts allegedly committed by Altman and

12   Brockman and their purported agents, the OpenAI For-Profit Entities. See id. at *4.

13            These deficiencies also necessarily defeat Musk’s claim for RICO conspiracy (Count V).

14   See, e.g., Sanford v. MemberWorks, Inc., 625 F.3d 550, 559 (9th Cir. 2010) (“Plaintiffs cannot

15   claim that a conspiracy to violate RICO existed if they do not adequately plead a substantive

16   violation of RICO.” (quoting Howard v. Am. Online Inc., 208 F.3d 741, 751 (9th Cir. 2000))).

17   IV.      FIDUCIARY BREACH CLAIMS

18            Musk lacks standing to assert his fiduciary breach claims—framed as constructive fraud

19   (Count II), a violation of the UCL, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17200 et seq. (Count XI), and aiding

20   and abetting breach of fiduciary duty (Count XIII)—because he is a donor with no “reversionary,

21   contractual, or property interest in” the assets of OpenAI, Inc., nor is he an officer or director of the

22   non-profit. Cal. Corp. Code § 5142(a)(1)-(4); see also Pinkert v. Schwab Charitable Fund, 2021

23   WL 2476869, at *5-6 (N.D. Cal. June 17, 2021) (listing classes of plaintiffs with standing to assert

24   breach of charitable trust under California law and noting that “a donor . . . is not among them”).

25            Musk cannot manufacture standing by repackaging his fiduciary breach claim as one under

26   the UCL. See id. at *6 (plaintiff’s “lack[] [of] standing” to bring claims for breach of fiduciary duty

27   “dispos[ed] of [his] UCL claim too because it [was] predicated on” those claims).

28            Moreover, Musk’s fiduciary breach claims fail for the additional, independent reason that

 4    V.      FALSE ADVERTISING CLAIMS

 5            The complaint fails to state a false advertising claim under the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C.

 6   § 1125(a)(1)(B) (Count X), or California’s false advertising statute, Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17500 et

 7   seq. (Count XII), because Musk identifies no commercial advertisement or statement made

 8   primarily out of economic motivation. See Children’s Health Def. v. Meta Platforms, Inc., 112

 9   F.4th 742, 764 (9th Cir. 2024) (requiring a commercial advertisement for “goods, services, or

10   commercial activities” to state a Lanham Act claim (quoting 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(1)(B)); Bernardo

11   v. Planned Parenthood Fed’n of Am., 115 Cal. App. 4th 322, 356 (2004) (requiring a statement

12   made in connection with the disposal of “real or personal property” or the “perform[ance] [of]

13   services” to state a claim for false advertising under California law (quoting Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code

14   § 17500)). Each of the statements included in the complaint relates to OpenAI’s mission and

15   general philosophy. None is tethered to the promotion of OpenAI’s goods or services. And, in any

16   event, Musk’s suggestion that his reputation for technological openness has been tarnished through

17   association with OpenAI does not adequately plead commercial injury—an independent defect that

18   deprives Musk of standing to assert a claim under the Lanham Act. See Charlotte’s Web, Inc. v.

19   AAXLL Supply Co. LLC, 2020 WL 6891876, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 24, 2020).

20   VI.      DECLARATORY RELIEF

21            The complaint fails to state a claim for declaratory relief (Count XV) because it pleads no

22   viable underlying claim for which the Court may order declaratory relief. Rigsby v. GoDaddy Inc.,

23   59 F.4th 998, 1010 (9th Cir. 2023). Even if another of Musk’s claims were cognizable, his request

24   for declaratory relief “would be redundant” of such claim and fail for that reason. Strome v. DBMK

25   Enters., Inc., 2014 WL 6485533, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 19, 2014).

26                                             CONCLUSION

27            For the foregoing reasons, the OpenAI Defendants’ Motion to Dismiss is GRANTED, and

28   Plaintiff Elon Musk’s complaint is DISMISSED WITH PREJUDICE.
