 1                                            INTRODUCTION

 2           Musk’s damages expert, Dr. C. Paul Wazzan, purports to estimate the “economic interest”

 3   that Elon Musk does not have, but would like to have, in the OpenAI nonprofit. Relying on Wazzan,

 4   Musk proposes to transform his charitable contributions into a $100 billion-plus equity stake and

 5   to raid the nonprofit’s assets to cash out that imaginary interest. This approach is legally incoherent

 6   and inconsistent with Musk’s claims, but those are issues for another day. This motion asks the

 7   Court to exclude Wazzan’s Opinions 3 through 6, and Musk’s opposition confirms that it should.

 8   Wazzan’s opinions rest on an unreliable methodology; are the product of an unreliable application

 9   of that methodology; and are not supported by sufficient facts. See Fed. R. Evid. 702(b)-(d).

10           Seeking to salvage Wazzan’s opinions, Musk repeatedly attempts to shift his burden onto

11   Defendants. He invokes superseded case law and argues that the Court should apply Rule 702’s

12   requirements liberally, with a heavy thumb on the scale in favor of admissibility. But the Ninth

13   Circuit squarely rejected Musk’s argument last summer: there is no presumption of admission.

14   Musk also invites the Court to defer ruling on this motion and either let the jury decide whether

15   Wazzan’s methods are sound or address the admissibility of Wazzan’s opinions during the trial.

16   But the Court has a non-delegable duty under Rule 702 to allow only reliable expert testimony, and

17   the issue is ripe for decision: Wazzan has served two reports; he sat for a full-day deposition; and

18   the parties have now made two rounds of submissions addressing the admissibility of his opinions.

19   The Court should act now so the parties have clarity as they prepare for the fast-approaching trial.

20                                              ARGUMENT

21   I.      MUSK INVOKES A NONEXISTENT PRESUMPTION OF ADMISSIBILITY.

22           Musk contends that the threshold reliability requirements in Rule 702 “should be applied

23   with a ‘liberal thrust’ favoring admission.” Pl. Mem. 6 (quoting Messick v. Novartis Pharms. Corp.,

24   747 F.3d 1193, 1196 (9th Cir. 2014)). But the Ninth Circuit has specifically disapproved of the

25   standard Musk advocates. As the Ninth Circuit held last year, Rule 702 “did not establish a

26   categorical preference for admitting expert testimony,” and prior cases—including Messick in

27   particular—should not be read to do so. Engilis v. Monsanto Co., 151 F.4th 1040, 1049-50 (9th Cir.

 2   of admission. There is no such presumption[.]” Id. at 1050.

 3            Musk does not even acknowledge Engilis, let alone reconcile his position with it, even

 4   though Defendants cited it multiple times. Defs. Mem. 3, 6. He ignores the Ninth Circuit’s holding

 5   that “a proponent of expert testimony” has the burden of establishing “each of the requirements of

10   litigants to “rely[] on the safeguards of the adversary system” when the proponent of expert

11   testimony fails to “satisf[y] the threshold requirements established by Rule 702.” 151 F.4th at 1050.

12   That is the situation here.

13   II.      WAZZAN’S METHODOLOGY IS UNRELIABLE.

14            A.      Wazzan does not explain how he selected the factors he considered.

15            Wazzan’s Opinion 3 (upon which his subsequent opinions are based) is that the share of the

16   OpenAI nonprofit’s value attributable to Musk’s contributions is 50% to 75%. Ex. 1 ¶ 98. 2 Wazzan

17   says he considered four factors in forming this opinion, Ex. 3 at 1-2, but he does not explain how

18   he selected those factors or justify his decision to exclude other potential factors. Defs. Mem. 6-9.

19            To be admissible, an “expert’s testimony must be grounded in an accepted body of learning

20   or experience in the expert’s field, and the expert must explain how the conclusion is so grounded.”

21   Fed. R. Evid. 702 advisory committee note to the 2000 amendment. Wazzan’s methodology is

22   grounded in nothing: He cited neither experience, nor any literature, nor any accepted practices

23   when selecting the four factors used to quantify the value of Musk’s contributions to the nonprofit.

15            B.       Wazzan’s calculations cannot be replicated or tested.

16            Wazzan’s methodology is also unreliable because he does not explain how his chosen

17   factors combine to yield the conclusion that Musk’s contributions account for 50% to 75% of the

18   nonprofit’s value. Wazzan’s failure to provide this information means that his “judgment calls”

19   cannot be “tested and analyzed,” and his “methodology . . . cannot be tested or replicated.” In re

20   Apple iPhone Antitrust Litig., 2025 WL 3124160, at *7-8 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 27, 2025) (YGR). Wazzan

22   “mathematical calculation.” GPNE Corp. v. Apple, Inc., 2014 WL 1494247, at *2, *4 (N.D. Cal.

 3          C.      Wazzan’s one-size-fits-all approach is unreliable.

 4           Defendants previously showed that Wazzan’s methodology is also unreliable because his

 5   estimates of wrongful gains cannot be allocated among claims or contributions. Defs. Mem. 11.

 6   Musk concedes that Wazzan provides no framework for apportioning gains but asserts that

 7   Wazzan’s opinions “would still be helpful” to an apportionment exercise as there is supposedly

 8   “ample evidence of Mr. Musk’s contributions.” Pl. Mem. 11. Musk’s reference to the record misses

 9   the point. Under Wazzan’s approach, not all contributions are created equal: earlier donations are

10   purportedly more valuable, Ex. 1 ¶ 74, though he never quantifies how much. Moreover, while

11   Wazzan says Musk’s nonmonetary contributions are “[v]ery valuable,” Ex. 4 at 246:3-7, he never

12   quantifies that value or explains how they factor into his calculations. This gives jurors no reliable

13   basis to weigh the value allegedly created by those contributions if warranted by the verdict.

14          D.      Wazzan’s methodology ignores causation.

15          Musk says that wrongful gains should include only “net profit attributable to the underlying

16   wrong.” Pl. Mem. 12. But Wazzan conducts no analysis to determine what portions of Defendants’

17   stakes of the OpenAI for-profit affiliate are allegedly attributable to the underlying wrong rather

18   than to other causes. He simply assumes all gains realized by Defendants after the entity’s

19   “wrongful” creation are also wrongful and thus subject to disgorgement, without linking those gains

20   to any alleged wrongful acts.

21          Wazzan’s treatment of Microsoft illustrates this flaw. Musk claims that Microsoft is “liable

22   for disgorgement of wrongful gains derived from the primary violator’s misconduct.” Pl. Mem. 12-

23   13. That is incorrect. An aiding-and-abetting defendant is liable “only for harm to which the aiding

24   and abetting made a contribution.” Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liab. for Econ. Harm § 28 cmt. e

25   (2020). Musk’s own authority confirms this point: an aiding-and-abetting defendant is an

26   “independent wrongdoer” required to disgorge only “the net increase in assets of the wrongdoer, to

27   the extent that this increase is attributable to the underlying wrong.” Am. Master Lease LLC v.

28   Idanta Partners, Ltd., 225 Cal. App. 4th 1451, 1486 (2014) (emphasis added) (citation omitted).
