 1   I.     INTRODUCTION

 2          Plaintiffs have tried for the third time to stretch complex theories of a primarily criminal

 3   statute around what is at heart a civil dispute over the founding of a legitimate venture. But the

 4   RICO claims simply do not fit the alleged facts set forth in the Second Amended Complaint

 5   (“SAC”). There is no allegation in the SAC or elsewhere that Microsoft committed any alleged

 6   predicate acts, that Microsoft had any intent to defraud, or that Microsoft participated in any RICO

 7   enterprise. The facts alleged by Plaintiffs do not state civil RICO claims against Microsoft, and

 8   Counts 20 and 21 in the SAC should be dismissed.

 9          Dismissal of the SAC on these grounds is consistent with the Court’s reasons for dismissing
10   the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). This Court dismissed Plaintiffs’ RICO claims in the FAC

11   because Plaintiffs failed to allege facts to “identify how the named defendants operated the entity

12   through a pattern of racketeering activity.” Dkt. 163 at 10. Plaintiffs have not alleged any specific

13   new facts that show any pattern of racketeering activity, and certainly none involving Microsoft.

14   They have added no allegations that (i) Microsoft undertook even a single predicate act, much less

15   a pattern of racketeering activity, (ii) Microsoft had specific intent to defraud, or (iii) Microsoft

16   participated in the operation of a RICO enterprise. Consistent with the Court’s dismissal of the

17   RICO claims in the FAC, each of these defects precludes the RICO claims against Microsoft in the

18   SAC.1 The RICO claims have no place in this dispute and Counts 20 and 21 should be dismissed

19   with prejudice.2

 9    III.   LEGAL STANDARD
10           “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter,

11    accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556

12    U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)).

13    “Threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory

14    statements, do not suffice.” Id.

15           In addition, any claim grounded in allegations of fraud or false representations must

16    satisfy Rule 9(b)’s heightened pleading standard. The complaint must plead with particularity

17    “what is false or misleading about a statement, and why it is false.” Ebeid ex rel. United States

18    v. Lungwitz, 616 F.3d 993, 998 (9th Cir. 2010) (citation omitted). And it must be “specific

19    enough to give defendants notice of the particular misconduct which is alleged to constitute the
20    fraud charged so that they can defend against the charge and not just deny that they have done

21    anything wrong.” Semegen v. Weidner, 780 F.2d 727, 731 (9th Cir. 1985).

22    IV.    ARGUMENT

23           A.       The SAC Fails to State Any RICO Claim (Counts 20-21)

24           Despite the Court’s requirement to show how Defendants “operated the entity through a

25    pattern of racketeering activity,” Dkt. 163 at 10, Plaintiffs’ SAC fails to plausibly allege that

26    purported misrepresentations that Altman and Brockman supposedly made to Musk amounts to

27    operating a RICO enterprise through a pattern of racketeering activity. As laid out in more detail

 9          B.      The SAC Fails to State a RICO Claim Against Microsoft (Counts 20-21)
10           In addition to the reasons stated in the OpenAI Defendants’ Memorandum, Counts 20 and

11    21 fail specifically as to Microsoft. The SAC, like the FAC before it, does not plead any predicate

12    acts by Microsoft, much less with the necessary particularity; does not allege Microsoft’s specific

13    intent to defraud; and does not allege Microsoft’s participation in the operation of the purported

14    RICO enterprise. Because the RICO claim fails, so too does the conspiracy to commit RICO claim.

15                  1.      The SAC Does Not Allege with Particularity Predicate Offenses by
                            Microsoft
16

17           The RICO statute requires plaintiffs to plead a pattern of racketeering activity (known as

18    “predicate acts”) undertaken specifically by Microsoft. See Coronavirus Rep. v. Apple, Inc., 85

19    F.4th 948, 958 (9th Cir. 2023). When, as here, plaintiffs base the alleged pattern of racketeering
20    activity on wire fraud, plaintiffs must plead, with heightened particularity, “(A) the formation of

21    a scheme to defraud, [and] (B) the use of the . . . wires in furtherance of that scheme.” Eclectic

22    Props. E., LLC v. Marcus & Millichap Co., 751 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2014). Plaintiffs must also

23    sufficiently plead specific intent to defraud. Id. The pattern of racketeering must specifically

24    include at least two predicate acts by each defendant. See id. at 995 n.5, 997.

25           Here, the only predicate acts Plaintiffs allege are emails between Altman, Brockman, and

26    Musk involving “repeated misrepresentations” to induce Musk’s “financial and other

 5                  3.        The SAC Does Not Allege Microsoft’s Participation in the Operation
                              or Management of a RICO Enterprise
 6

 7           Separately, Plaintiffs’ 1962(c) claim fails as to Microsoft because the SAC does not allege

 8    facts establishing that Microsoft participated in the operation or management of a RICO enterprise,

 9    “not just [its] own affairs.” Ferrari v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, 2016 WL 7188030, at *2 (N.D.
10    Cal. Dec. 12, 2016) (citations omitted); see also Walter v. Drayson, 538 F.3d 1244, 1248-49 (9th

11    Cir. 2008) (dismissing claim for failure to allege that defendants had a part in directing the

12    enterprise’s affairs). None of the SAC’s minimal additions comes anywhere close to suggesting

13    that Microsoft participated in the operation or management of a RICO enterprise.

14           Only one paragraph attempts to allege Microsoft’s participation in the enterprise, and that

15    falls far short. See SAC ¶ 446. As shown above, see supra at p. 3-4, that paragraph makes only

16    vague, conclusory, and insufficient allegations that some combination of the For-Profit Entities

17    and Microsoft “knowingly provid[ed] the capital, corporate structure(s), and advice and

18    encouragement” and were “vital participants in siphoning off and exploiting OpenAI, Inc’s

19    valuable AI/AGI technology” SAC ¶ 446(a), (c). Inflammatory characterization aside, this simply
20    alleges that Microsoft provided OpenAI with needed investment capital (SAC ¶¶ 157, 216) and,

21    in return, OpenAI licensed its GenAI technology to Microsoft (SAC ¶ 131). That allegation

22    amounts to nothing more than Microsoft’s normal business activity, with no relation to the

23    purported “racketeering activity” alleged against Altman and Brockman. It therefore does not

24    suffice to allege participation in a RICO enterprise. See Reves v. Ernst & Young, 507 U.S. 170,

25    184 (1993); Ferrari, 2016 WL 7188030, at *3 (dismissing a claim where allegations were

26    insufficient to show that the defendants “conducted or participated in the conduct of a racketeering

27    enterprise, rather than just the business of [their company] separate and apart from any alleged

28    racketeering acts.”).

 1           As this Court has already observed, the RICO statute is designed to address a specific

 2    pattern of racketeering conduct. Dkt. 163 at 10. Applying the RICO laws to Microsoft’s arm’s

 3    length business contracts with OpenAI distorts the law’s purpose. See Reves, 507 U.S. at 184 (“[I]t

 4    is clear that Congress did not intend to extend RICO liability under § 1962(c) beyond those who

 5    participate in the operation or management of an enterprise through a pattern of racketeering

 6    activity.”). This supplies yet another reason requiring dismissing Count 20 as to Microsoft.

 7                  4.      The SAC Does Not Allege a Section 1962(d) Conspiracy
 8           Finally, the SAC’s Section 1962(d) claim fails as to Microsoft because a RICO conspiracy

 9    claim cannot survive when the underlying RICO claim fails. See Sanford v. MemberWorks, Inc.,
10    625 F.3d 550, 559 (9th Cir. 2010). Despite multiple attempts, Plaintiffs still plead no facts showing

11    that Microsoft knew about or agreed to facilitate the alleged RICO scheme. See Lewis v. Sporck,

12    612 F. Supp. 1316, 1325 (N.D. Cal. 1985). As a result, Count 21 should be dismissed with

13    prejudice.

14    V.    CONCLUSION

15           For the foregoing reasons, the Court should grant Microsoft’s Motion to Dismiss SAC

16    Counts 20 and 21 without leave to amend.
