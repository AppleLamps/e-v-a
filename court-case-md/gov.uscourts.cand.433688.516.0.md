 1            The OpenAI Defendants submit this memorandum in response to the Court’s request for
 2   further briefing regarding the applicability of Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17510.6 and 17510.8 to
 3   Musk’s charitable trust claim. As detailed below, there is no legal or factual basis for Musk’s
 4   argument that a charitable trust was created under those provisions of California’s charitable
 5   solicitations statute. Accordingly, the jury should not be instructed regarding this alternative basis
 6   for Musk’s claim.
 7                                              *          *      *
 8            Musk primarily argues that a charitable trust was created because he manifested an intention
 9   that OpenAI, Inc. use the funds he donated only for specified charitable purposes. See Dkt. 427.1
10   at 1 (Plaintiff’s Statement of Elements); Dkt. 433 at 22 (Plaintiff’s Proposed Jury Instruction 18-
11   P).
12            Musk separately claims that a charitable trust was created pursuant to Cal. Bus. & Prof.
13   Code § 17510.8 1 based on two alleged charitable solicitations: (1) an email Altman sent to Musk
14   in February 2016 regarding Musk’s quarterly $5 million payments, and (2) an email Chris Clark
15   sent to Jared Birchall in July 2020 regarding Musk’s final two monthly rent payments. Trial Tr.
16   (Apr. 29, 2026) at 639:8-25. Neither satisfies the statute’s requirements for creating a charitable
17   trust.
18            (1) Altman’s February 2016 email. Altman’s February 2016 email asking if Musk could
19   “do $20MM a year for [] each of the next 3 years,” PX-0069—for a total of $60 million out of the
20   $1 billion Musk had already pledged as a founding commitment (DX-0509)—did not create a
21   charitable trust under § 17510.8 because both Altman and Musk were “members” of OpenAI, Inc.
22   at the time of the alleged solicitation. § 17510.6.
23            Section 17510.6 provides that “[t]he provisions of [Article 1.3]”—which includes
24   § 17510.8—“shall not apply to solicitations … within the membership of a charitable organization

10           Musk argues that § 17510.6 does not apply on account of § 17510.8’s “notwithstanding”
11   clause. That is wrong. Section 17510.8’s “notwithstanding” clause cannot limit § 17510.6 because
12   the clause itself is within a “provision[] of this article [which] shall not apply.” § 17510.6. If the
13   article doesn’t apply, a section within it cannot either.
14           Two California decisions have addressed this precise question, and both have rejected
15   Plaintiff’s contention. The California Court of Appeal in Cohen v. Kabbalah Centre Int’l, Inc. held
16   squarely that § 17510.6 renders 17510.8 inapplicable to “‘solicitations’ within the membership of
17   a charitable organization.” 35 Cal. App. 5th 13, 23 (2019). The plaintiff in that case, just like Musk
18   here, argued that § 17510.8’s “notwithstanding” clause trumped § 17510.6’s member-exclusion.
19   The court rejected that argument: “This clause has no application here, however, because this
20   section is within the article that does not apply to ‘solicitations’ within the membership of a
21   charitable organization.” Id. (citing § 17510.6). The second decision, Buck v. Peace Apostolic
22   Church, Inc., likewise found that § 17510.8 did not apply because the plaintiff there was a member
23   of the defendant charitable organization. 2023 WL 9064440, at *6 (Cal. Super. Ct. Oct. 31, 2023)
24   (describing § 17510.6 as “an exception to” § 17510.8). 2

 1           Applying § 17510.6’s member-exclusion as written is entirely consistent with the purpose
 2   of the statute—which is to “protect[] the public against the predatory practices of charitable
 3   solicitors,” not a co-founder from his fellow members. People v. Orange Cnty. Charitable Servs.,
 4   73 Cal. App. 4th 1054, 1059 (1999) (applying § 17510.8 to 130 individuals and business entities
 5   engaged in commercial fundraising). 3 Section 17510.8 lies within Article 1.3 (“Charitable
 6   Solicitations”) of Chapter 1 (“Advertising”) of Part 3 (“Representations to the Public”) of the
 7   Business and Professions Code. The Article’s stated purpose “is to safeguard the public against
 8   fraud, deceit and imposition” and “restrict harmful solicitation methods.” § 17510(b). Numerous
 9   sections of the statute are directed at such public-facing practices—e.g., addressing solicitations in
10   “newspaper[s],” or “by public outcry or proclamation,” or those “involv[ing] persons standing in a
11   public roadway soliciting contributions from passing motorists” (§§ 17510.2(c), 17510.25)—and
12   highlight the statute’s inapplicability to the facts here. One provision requires a solicitor to “exhibit
13   to the prospective donor” a “‘Solicitation or Sale for Charitable Purposes Card,’” which should be
14   signed and have the name and address of the solicitor or the soliciting organization. § 17510.3(a).
15   Alternatively, the solicitor may present a brochure containing the same information. Id. None of
16   any of this would make sense if the intent was to apply § 17510.8 to a co-founder like Musk.

21   Clark’s request was not for a charitable contribution to OpenAI, Inc., but rather for sharing building
22   costs between OpenAI, Inc. and Neuralink—Musk’s for-profit company that Musk also housed in
