 1             Plaintiff’s proffered expert testimony from Dr. Stuart Russell should be excluded under
 2   Daubert and Rules 702 and 403. Russell, a computer scientist and prominent AI “doomer,” has
 3   made a career giving public lectures warning that AI may one day kill off humanity. In 2017,
 4   Russell went viral by releasing a short film on YouTube called “Slaughterbots” that dramatized
 5   speculative scenarios in which swarms of miniature AI-enabled drones—the “slaughterbots”—
 6   could attack large population centers and carry out targeted assassinations.
 7             Plaintiff wants Russell to present similar dystopian scenarios to the jury. If permitted to
 8   testify, Russell will tell the jury that AI might in the future drive mass unemployment; immiserate
 9   workers; develop novel weapons of mass destruction; foment nuclear war; kill millions of people;
10   and ultimately cause human extinction. Ex. A (Russell Rpt. ⁋⁋ 78-80,75,77,99-101,102-104).
11   Russell will do this without reference to the facts of this case (which he hasn’t reviewed), or the
12   legal claims at issue (with which he is unfamiliar). The sole purpose of his testimony is to encourage
13   the jury to believe that AI is likely to harm them personally. That is improper.
14                                             BACKGROUND
15             According to Musk, his claims are “not super complicated.” Ex. B (Musk Tr. 52:23). He
16   says he supported OpenAI years ago on the understanding that it would be “non-profit” and “open
17   source”—“that’s it.” Id. 52:14-19; 53:4-13; Dkt. 350 at 15. Musk identifies no specific safety
18   commitments made to him by OpenAI. And at deposition, Musk could identify no OpenAI practices
19   or products he believed had breached any purported safety commitments. Ex. B 261:13-21; 263:11-
20   16. Musk also chose not to pursue discovery into OpenAI’s safety practices: To shield himself from
21   reciprocal discovery probing xAI’s abysmal safety record, Musk largely abandoned his safety-
22   related document requests and withdrew his request that OpenAI’s 30(b)(6) witness testify on AI
23   safety.
24             Russell, for his part, is not offering opinions on “any alleged safety commitments made by
25   OpenAI to Mr. Musk” (Ex. C (Russell Tr. 44:11-15)); “whether OpenAI’s current or past safety
26   practices are consistent with any [such] alleged commitments” (id. 44:16-21); “whether any
27   historical OpenAI product is safe or unsafe” (id. 47:21-48:2); or “whether OpenAI’s safety
28   practices” are “better or worse than the safety practices of other AI companies.” Id. 45:6-11.

10          Russell agrees there are “serious AI experts” who dispute his doomsday views. Ex. C
11   190:13-16. He does not discuss these critiques, however, because his “job” was “to give arguments
12   for the propositions rather than against [his] own propositions.” Id. 190:17-21. Russell also agrees
13   there is no “methodologically rigorous” way to determine the risk of human extinction from AI,
14   that no “scientifically rigorous” studies have even tried, and that it is “reasonable” to consider
15   extinction probabilities “just guesses.” Id. 219:18-24; 217:20-218:1. Nevertheless, Russell intends
16   to tell the jury that AI poses a “roughly 1% to 3% per year” risk of human extinction. Ex. A ⁋110.
17                                             ARGUMENT
18          “[E]xpert testimony carries special dangers to the fact-finding process because it ‘can be
19   both powerful and quite misleading.’” Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc., 43 F.3d 1311, 1321
20   n.17 (9th Cir. 1995), quoting Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993). The
21   proponent of the testimony must therefore demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that it
22   satisfies the requirements of Rule 702. Engilis v. Monsanto Co., 151 F.4th 1040, 1049 (9th Cir.
23   2025). To be admitted under Rule 702(a), expert opinions must “help the trier of fact to understand
24   the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” FRE 702(a). This “fit” requirement is more stringent
25   than “the general relevancy requirement of Rule 402”: “Federal judges must . . . exclude proffered
26   scientific evidence under Rules 702 and 403 unless they are convinced that it speaks clearly and
27   directly to an issue in dispute in the case, and that it will not mislead the jury.” Daubert, 43 F.3d
28   1311 at 1321 n.17. Russell’s opinions do not satisfy this standard.

 1          Opinions 1-4 should be excluded. Whether and to what extent AI might one day pose “a
 2   very substantial risk to humanity” (Ex. A ⁋43) are not “fact[s] in issue” that the jury must
 3   “determine” to resolve Musk’s claims. Musk alleges that OpenAI abandoned purported
 4   commitments—that OpenAI remain “nonprofit” and “open source”—made to him more than ten
 5   years ago. Determining whether OpenAI made those commitments, or abided by them, does not
 6   turn on conjecture about whether AI may one day take away jobs, or eliminate wages, or kill
 7   millions of people. Nor will Russell’s opinions help the jury “understand the evidence” specific to
 8   OpenAI. Musk didn’t pursue discovery into OpenAI’s safety practices, and Russell does not purport
 9   to opine on any such practices now. Russell has no opinion on whether anything OpenAI has done,
10   is doing, or may do makes it more or less likely that his alarmist vision of the future will come to
11   pass. Instead, Russell appears to believe that the entire AI industry is irredeemable, and that
12   advanced AI can never be developed safely. Ex. C 51:8-11; Ex. A ⁋32.
13          Russell’s opinions likewise cannot be admitted “to educate the factfinder about general
14   principles.” FRE 702 (2000 Comm. Notes). Generalized opinions remain subject to Rule 702’s “fit”
15   requirement. Id.; see also Brown v. Google, LLC, 2022 WL 17961497, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 12,
16   2022) (“generalized testimony” must “logically advance a material aspect of the proposing party’s
17   case”) (cleaned up). In Brown, for example, this Court admitted generalized expert testimony on
18   data privacy, but only because the testimony “fit” the elements of plaintiffs’ data privacy claims,
19   which required a determination that Google’s data practices would be “highly offensive.” Id.
20   Because Russell’s opinions bear no similar relation to Musk’s claims, they should be excluded. See
21   Rovid v. Graco Children's Prods. Inc., 2018 WL 5906075, at *13 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 9, 2018)
22   (excluding expert testimony where “no attempt to tie [] general background to the facts of th[e]
23   action or to any relevant issue”); Rolls-Royce Corp. v. Heros, Inc., 2010 WL 184313, at *6 (N.D.
24   Tex. Jan. 14, 2010) (excluding testimony about safety of civil aviation industry).
25          Even if Russell’s opinions were minimally probative, they should still be excluded under
26   Rule 403. Russell’s testimony risks misleading the jurors and prompting them to act on fear for
27   their personal safety and livelihoods rather than on an assessment of the evidence. Courts have
28   rejected similar “reptile” tactics, Griffin v. Pet Sense, LLC, 2024 WL 1604605, at *4 (E.D. Ark.

 4          Opinion 3 should be excluded for additional reasons. Central to Russell’s thesis is his
 5   claim in Opinion 3 that AI companies and governments are in a “race” to develop AI that “may
 6   lead to safety being a secondary consideration.” Ex. A ¶45. In addition to being irrelevant, that
 7   opinion should be excluded because it is based on nothing more than Russell’s say-so. Russell’s
 8   only support for the proposition was an anonymous news article. Id. Russell disclaimed reliance on
 9   that article at deposition, before insisting that the “race” was “well known” and did not “need
10   detailed arguments in support,” but had been confirmed to him through “a lot of conversations with
11   a lot of people.” Ex. C 184:13-19;185:13-186:5. To the extent this purported “race” is so “well
12   known” as to need no support, Russell can’t testify to it. See United States v. Hanna, 293 F.3d
13   1080, 1086 (9th Cir. 2002) (experts cannot testify to “common knowledge”). Regardless, an
14   opinion backed by undisclosed “conversations”—and no methodology—is not “based on sufficient
15   facts or data” or “reliable principles and methods,” FRE 702(b)-(c), and runs afoul of FRCP
16   26(a)(2)(B).
17          Opinion 4 should be excluded for additional reasons. Russell’s opinions about potential
18   risks should also be excluded as speculative and unreliable. For example, citing nothing, Russell
19   speculates that AI could replace human labor, leaving humanity “enfeebled passengers in a cruise
20   ship run by machines.” Ex. A ⁋82. Russell likewise acknowledges his extinction risk scenarios are
21   speculative—he says one could “imagine” them, or that they could “conceivably” occur, citing no
22   source or methodology. Id. ⁋⁋104;102. Russell admits that there is no “methodologically rigorous”
23   way to determine extinction risk, and that it is “reasonable” to consider extinction probabilities
24   “just guesses.” Ex. C 219:18-24; 217:20-218:1. And Russell based his extinction probabilities
25   entirely on unsourced hearsay that he hasn’t analyzed himself. Ex. A ⁋110. Opinion 4 is advocacy,
26   not science. It should be excluded for that reason, too.
