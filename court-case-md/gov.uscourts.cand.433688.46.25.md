15                                            BACKGROUND
16          This action arises from allegations that Defendants have engaged in anticompetitive conduct,
17 breached a charitable trust, and engaged in self-dealing. Plaintiffs allege that Defendants have

18 transformed OpenAI, Inc. from its founding mission as a transparent, safety-focused non-profit into

19 a co-conspirator of Microsoft Corporation engaged in unlawful anticompetitive conduct.

20          Plaintiffs seek preliminary injunctive relief to prevent Defendants from: (1) making,
21 enforcing or furthering agreements with investors not to invest in OpenAI9s competitors; (2) holding

22 interlocking directorates or benefiting from competitively sensitive information obtained through

23 interlocking directorates; (3) converting or furthering the conversion of OpenAI from a non-profit

24 to a for-profit enterprise; and (4) engaging in self-dealing transactions.

 1                                          LEGAL STANDARD

 2          <A plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction must establish (1) that he is likely to succeed

 3 on the merits, (2) that he is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief, (3)

 4 that the balance of equities tips in his favor, and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest.=

 5 Winter v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008). Alternatively, under the Ninth

 6 Circuit9s <sliding scale= approach, serious questions going to the merits and a balance of hardships

 7 that tips sharply towards the plaintiff can support issuance of a preliminary injunction, so long as

 8 the plaintiff also shows that there is a likelihood of irreparable injury and that the injunction is in

 9 the public interest. Alliance for the Wild Rockies v. Cottrell, 632 F.3d 1127, 1135 (9th Cir. 2011).

10                                              DISCUSSION

11          The Court finds that Plaintiffs have satisfied both the Winter test and the Ninth Circuit9s

12 alternative sliding scale approach. Each element is analyzed in turn.

13    I.    Likelihood of Success on the Merits

14          A.      Sherman Act Section 1 Claim

15          Plaintiffs have demonstrated a strong likelihood of success on their claim that OpenAI and

16 Microsoft violated Section 1 of the Sherman Act. The evidence shows that during OpenAI9s October

17 2024 funding round, Defendants conditioned the opportunity to invest in OpenAI on agreements by

18 investors not to invest in OpenAI9s competitors, specifically naming xAI. This conduct constitutes

19 a per se violation of Section 1 as either a traditional horizontal group boycott, PLS.Com, LLC v.

20 Nat9l Ass9n of Realtors, 32 F.4th 824, 834 (9th Cir. 2022), or a hub-and-spoke conspiracy under

21 Klor9s, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc., 359 U.S. 207 (1959). See Honey Bum, LLC v. Fashion

22 Nova, Inc., 63 F.4th 813, 821 (9th Cir. 2023). Even if the conduct were not per se illegal, it would

23 still constitute a modified per se violation under Northwest Wholesale Stationers, Inc. v. Pacific

24 Stationery & Printing Co., 472 U.S. 284 (1985), as OpenAI and Microsoft possess a dominant

25 position in the relevant market for generative artificial intelligence (<AI=), controlling

26 approximately 69% of the market, and their conduct has restricted access to the capital financing

27 necessary for competitors to effectively compete. Injunctive relief is appropriate. 15 U.S.C. § 26.

 1          B.      Clayton Act Section 8 Claim

 2          Plaintiffs have also shown a likelihood of success on their claim that Defendants violated

 3 Section 8 of the Clayton Act through interlocking directorates. The undisputed evidence shows that

 4 Reid Hoffman served simultaneously on the boards of Microsoft and OpenAI, Inc. from March 2018

 5 until March 2023, and Deannah Templeton served as Microsoft9s representative on OpenAI, Inc.9s

 6 board from November 2023 until July 2024. Both Microsoft and OpenAI had capital, surplus, and

 7 undivided profits exceeding the statutory thresholds during the relevant period. See 89 Fed. Reg.

 8 3926 (Jan. 22, 2024).

 9          Microsoft9s admission that it competes with OpenAI in its SEC filings, coupled with the

10 similar pricing and features of their respective AI products (OpenAI9s ChatGPT and Microsoft9s

11 Copilot), establishes that they are competitors within the meaning of Section 8. The information

12 gained from these interlocking directorates, without regard to voting power, represents a grave and

13 continuing threat to competition, and Defendants9 violations are blatant and repeated. See TRW, Inc.

14 v. F.T.C., 647 F.2d 942, 946347 (9th Cir. 1981). Injunctive relief is appropriate. See United States v.

15 W. T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953).

16          C.      Charitable Trust Claim

17          Musk has demonstrated a likelihood of success on his claim that Defendants are violating

18 the terms of his charitable donations. Under California law, Musk has standing to enforce the

19 conditions of his gifts both as a donor with a contractual interest subject to a condition subsequent,

20 Cal. Corp. Code § 5142(a)(4), and as a settlor of a charitable trust in favor of OpenAI, Inc. or as

21 otherwise having a sufficiently special interest under Holt v. College of Osteopathic Physicians &

22 Surgeons, 61 Cal. 2d 750 (1964).

23          The evidence shows that Defendants are actively working to convert OpenAI into a for-profit

24 enterprise in direct contravention of the conditions on which Musk made his charitable donations,

25 expressed unambiguously no later than September 20, 2017. Injunctive relief is appropriate. Cal.

26 Corp. Code § 5142(a); Holt, 61 Cal. 2d at 757.

27          D.      Self-Dealing Claim

28          Finally, Plaintiffs have shown a likelihood of success on their claims regarding prohibited

14    II.   Irreparable Harm

15          Plaintiffs have demonstrated they will suffer irreparable harm absent preliminary relief.

16          A.     Harm to Competition

17          The term imposed by OpenAI and Microsoft on investors in OpenAI9s October 2024 funding

18 round directly impairs xAI9s access to capital markets during a crucial developmental period for

19 generative AI companies. Given the strong network effects in this market4where more users lead

20 to better products, attracting still more users4the loss of investment opportunities now will result

21 in competitive disadvantages that cannot be remedied by monetary damages alone. See Epic Games,

22 Inc. v. Apple Inc., 493 F. Supp. 3d 817, 851 (N.D. Cal. 2020).

23          The misuse of competitively sensitive information obtained by Microsoft/Templeton and

24 Hoffman through interlocking directorates compounds this harm. Courts in this District <presume

25 that Plaintiff will suffer irreparable harm if its proprietary information is misappropriated.= Comet

26 Techs. United States of Am. Inc. v. Beuerman, No. 18-CV-01441-LHK, 2018 WL 1990226, at *5

27 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 15, 2018). These harms create an ongoing competitive advantage that cannot be

28 undone through monetary relief.

 1          B.      Harm to OpenAI, Inc.9s Charitable Mission

 2          The imminent conversion of OpenAI, Inc. to a for-profit enterprise threatens irreparable

 3 harm to both Musk9s interests as a donor and the public interest in charitable enforcement. Unlike

 4 the For-Profit Entities9 current structure, which involves a limited number of sophisticated investors,

 5 full conversion to for-profit status would invite a greater number of investors, including retail

 6 investors, resulting in complex and formidable barriers to unwinding such transactions. OpenAI,

 7 Inc.9s full conversion to a for-profit structure therefore threatens permanent harm to the existence

 8 and charitable mission of the non-profit that Musk9s donations were intended to support. There is

 9 also substantial doubt whether, in light of the self-dealing alleged and OpenAI9s current rate of

10 spending, OpenAI or Altman would have sufficient resources to pay a judgment for damages.

11 III.     Balance of Equities

12          The balance of equities tips sharply in Plaintiffs9 favor. The requested preliminary injunction

13 merely requires Defendants to comply with existing legal obligations under federal antitrust law and

14 state corporate and charitable trust law. See Henry Schein, Inc. v. Cook, 191 F. Supp. 3d 1072, 1077

15 (N.D. Cal. 2016).

16          A.      Anticompetitive Conduct

17          Enjoining Defendants from enforcing their anticompetitive investment restriction imposes

18 no legitimate hardship on them, as they have no right to orchestrate group boycotts or otherwise

19 restrict the flow of capital to competitors. Similarly, preventing the misuse of information obtained

20 through unlawful interlocking directorates creates no cognizable burden on Defendants, who should

21 never have had access to such information in the first place.

22          B.      Charitable Mission and Self-Dealing

23          Maintaining OpenAI, Inc.9s charitable status pending final resolution and halting further

24 self-dealing transactions protects both the organization9s founding mission and the public interest in

25 proper administration of charities. While Defendants may experience some delay in their planned

26 corporate restructuring, any such private financial interests are substantially outweighed by the

27 public9s interest in preserving charitable assets and enforcing fiduciary duties.

 1   IV.    Public Interest

 2          The public interest strongly favors preliminary relief.

 3          A.      Competition and Innovation

 4          <[T]he central purpose of the antitrust laws, state and federal, is to preserve competition. It

 5 is competition . . . that these statutes recognize as vital to the public interest.= Glen Holly Ent., Inc.

 6 v. Tektronix Inc., 343 F.3d 1000, 1010 (9th Cir. 2003) (internal quotation marks omitted). The

 7 development of generative AI technology presents profound implications for society, making

 8 preservation of competitive markets in this sector particularly crucial to the public interest.

 9          B.      Charitable Enforcement

10          The public has a strong interest in ensuring that charitable assets are not diverted for private

11 gain. This interest is particularly acute here given the substantial tax benefits OpenAI, Inc. received

12 as a non-profit, the organization9s repeated public commitments to developing AI technology for the

13 benefit of humanity, and the serious safety concerns raised by former OpenAI employees regarding

14 the organization9s rush to market potentially dangerous products in pursuit of profit.

15    V.    Scope of Injunction

16          Having found that preliminary injunctive relief is warranted, the Court turns to crafting an

17 appropriately tailored order that will maintain the status quo pending final resolution. See Perfect

18 10, Inc. v. Google, Inc., 653 F.3d 976, 981 (9th Cir. 2011).

19          IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that, pending final resolution of this action:
