 1                      MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

 2   I.      INTRODUCTION

 3           Pursuant to Federal Rules of Evidence 702 and 403, Defendant Microsoft respectfully

 4   moves the Court for an order excluding Opinion 5 and related testimony of Plaintiff’s proffered

 5   expert on wrongful gains, Dr. Paul Wazzan. In addition to the fact that Wazzan’s overall

 6   methodology is not reliable under Daubert, his opinion as to Microsoft’s wrongful gains should

 7   be excluded for the additional reason that it does not fit the theory of liability against Microsoft.

 8           The only remaining claim against Microsoft in this case is alleged aiding and abetting

 9   OpenAI’s breach of a fiduciary duty stemming from the existence of a charitable trust. Yet,

10   Wazzan’s expert opinion purports to calculate “the amount by which . . . Microsoft wrongly

11   profited or w[as] unjustly enriched by operating OpenAI as a commercial venture despite

12   having received charitable contributions from Elon Musk on the understanding that OpenAI

13   would remain a nonprofit dedicated to the public good.” Ex. A (Wazzan Rpt. ¶ 8). Musk takes

14   the position that “all of Microsoft’s gains on its investments in OpenAI for-profit derive from

15   that entity’s unauthorized commercial operations” and, as such, Wazzan need only calculate

16   “what portion of those gains is attributable to Mr. Musk’s contributions.” (Dkt. 396 at 12-13).

17   Because Wazzan fails to present a methodology that calculates what amount of Microsoft’s

18   gains were the result of the allegedly wrongful conduct engaged in by Microsoft in substantial

19   assistance of OpenAI’s alleged breach, Opinion 5 of Wazzan’s report should be excluded under
20   Rule 702 and 403.

21   II.     BACKGROUND

22           Dr. Wazzan submitted an expert report on October 29, 2025. He filed a supplemental

23   report on December 2, 2025, following OpenAI’s announced recapitalization. Wazzan

24   calculates the wrongful gains attributable to Microsoft at $13.30 billion to $25.06 billion. Ex.

25   B (Wazzan Supp. Rpt. Ex. 20). At the time of Wazzan’s reports, there were two remaining

26   claims against Microsoft: (1) quasi-contract/unjust enrichment, and (2) aiding and abetting

27   breach of fiduciary duty. Despite there being two distinct claims, Wazzan developed a single

28   methodology to measure Microsoft’s wrongful gains—regardless of which might lead to

13   claim against Microsoft. (Dkt. 390). The Court found that there was no quasi-contract between

15   benefit directly’ from him.” (Id. at 27). Only aiding and abetting remains against Microsoft.

16            Microsoft and OpenAI filed a motion to exclude Wazzan’s opinions 3-6 pursuant to

17   Rule 702, for a variety of reasons including that Wazzan’s methodology does not tie the

18   purported enrichment to the alleged wrongdoing. (Dkt. 393). In that motion, Microsoft

19   indicated it would bring a related motion in limine to exclude testimony regarding Microsoft’s
20   enrichment, which is the subject of this motion.

21   III.     LEGAL STANDARD

22            Federal Rule of Evidence 702 requires that any expert testimony “will help the trier of

23   fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue,” Fed. R. Evid. 702(a). This

24   standard requires that where “a model purporting to serve as evidence of damages . . . must

25   measure only those damages attributable to th[e] theory [of liability].” Comcast Corp. v.

26   Behrend, 569 U.S. 27, 35 (2013).

27            “Federal judges must . . . exclude proffered scientific evidence under Rules 702 and

28   403 unless they are convinced that it speaks clearly and directly to an issue in dispute in the

 5   IV.     ARGUMENT

 6           Dr. Wazzan’s calculation of Microsoft’s wrongful gains does not fit the theory of

 7   liability in this case. Because Wazzan calculated the “wrongful gains that OpenAI and

 8   Microsoft reaped from their unauthorized commercial use of [Musk’s] charitable

 9   contributions” (Dkt. 396, Pl. Daubert Opp., at 1) (emphasis added), Wazzan is treating

10   Microsoft as if it had the same quasi-contractual relationship with Musk that OpenAI is alleged

11   to have had. But as this Court already has found, Microsoft was never in a quasi-contractual

12   relationship with Musk. By utilizing the exact same methodology for Microsoft as he has for

13   OpenAI—taking Microsoft’s total stake in OpenAI’s current value and calculating what

14   portion of that is attributable to Musk’s contributions—Wazzan has only presented a

15   methodology potentially relevant to the dismissed quasi-contract/unjust enrichment claim, not

16   to the aiding and abetting claim. At the same time, Wazzan has failed to analyze how the alleged

17   conduct by Microsoft substantially assisted OpenAI’s alleged breach of fiduciary duty—a

18   necessary step in determining Microsoft’s wrongful gain stemming from aiding and abetting.

19   As such, his testimony will not assist the trier of fact. Fed. R. Evid. 702(a). It also risks
20   confusing the issues, misleading the jury, and would result in unfair prejudice to Microsoft.

21   Fed. R. Evid. 403.

22           Contrary to Musk’s assertion that Microsoft is liable for gains “derived from the

23   primary violator’s misconduct” (Dkt. 396 at 12-13), an aiding-and-abetting defendant is liable

24   “only for harm to which the aiding and abetting made a contribution.” Restatement (Third) of

25   Torts: Liab. for Econ. Harm § 28 cmt. e (2020) (emphasis added). Musk asserts, however, that

26   Wazzan need not examine Microsoft’s own conduct in his analysis. (Dkt. 396 at 12-13). But

27   by measuring Microsoft’s disgorgement solely by reference to OpenAI’s conduct and Musk’s

28   contributions, Wazzan’s methodology does not and cannot measure what amount of

 1   Microsoft’s purportedly wrongful gains are the result of Microsoft’s conduct. As a result,

 2   Wazzan’s calculation of wrongful gains is “contrary to law” and thus “unreliable under FRE

 3   702 and Daubert.” Apple, Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., No. 11-CV-01846-LHK, 2012 WL

 4   2571332, at *6 (N.D. Cal. June 30, 2012) (excluding expert opinion where the damages

 5   calculations were inconsistent with the requirements of the law).

 6             The fact that Wazzan was directed by counsel to assume that all gains after formation

 7   of the OAI for-profit entity are unlawful does not save his analysis. Even when so instructed,

 8   Wazzan’s expert testimony “lacks relevance unless one can show that the damages resulted

 9   from the defendant’s wrongful acts.” See Ex. D (Weil, Roman L, et al. Litigation Services

10   Handbook: The Role of the Financial Expert, at 4.7, Hoboken, New Jersey, Wiley, 2017)

11   (discussing the standards for expert testimony on remedies including disgorgement) (emphasis

12   added).

13             As admitted during his deposition, none of the steps in Wazzan’s methodology look to

14   how the conduct Microsoft is alleged to have engaged in provided substantial assistance to

15   OpenAI’s purported breach of charitable trust. Thus, his methodology provides no assistance

16   to the jury in determining what portion of Microsoft’s gains are attributable to Microsoft’s

17   allegedly wrongful conduct, as opposed to some other cause. See Fed. R. Evid. 702(a). Worse,

18   presenting a Microsoft disgorgement figure pegged to a dismissed “unauthorized use” theory

19   would be highly prejudicial and further risks misleading and confusing the jury. See Fed. R.
20   Evid. 403.

21   V.        CONCLUSION

22             For the foregoing reasons, Microsoft respectfully moves this Court to exclude Opinion

23   5 of Wazzan’s report regarding Microsoft’s wrongful gains under Rules 702 and 403.
