10                                                   ANALYSIS

11              To state a claim, a complaint must contain “[f]actual allegations” that “raise a right to relief

12    above the speculative level.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). Under this

13    standard, “[t]hreadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory

14    statements, do not suffice.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). Claims sounding in fraud

15    must also satisfy Rule 9(b). The plaintiff must plead “the who, what, when, where, and how of the

16    misconduct charged,” “what [was] false or misleading about a [challenged] statement, and why it

17    [was] false.” Yetter v. Ford Motor Co., 428 F. Supp. 3d 210, 231 (N.D. Cal. 2019) (cleaned up).

18    Where, as here, “a unified course of fraudulent conduct” is alleged, any claim grounded in that

19    purported misconduct “must satisfy . . . Rule 9(b),” whether or not expressly pleaded as fraud. Vess

20    v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA, 317 F.3d 1097, 1103-04, 1106 (9th Cir. 2003).

21              All of Plaintiffs’ claims are due to be dismissed under these standards.

22       I.      CONTRACT CLAIMS

23              A.     Breach of express contract

24              The FAC fails to state a claim for breach of express contract (Count I). The terms of an

25    express contract must be “stated in words,” from which the “vital elements” of “mutual assent” and

26    “consideration” must be apparent. Pac. Bay Recovery, Inc. v. Cal. Physicians’ Servs., Inc., 12 Cal.

27    App. 5th 200, 215 (2017) (citing Cal. Civ. Code § 1620). The documents purportedly evidencing

28    Musk’s contract—consisting of an assortment of public filings and emails spanning years and

 1    covering various topics (Compl. ¶ 251)—show neither.

 2           Mutual assent requires a “meeting of the minds on all material points,” Am. Emps. Grp.,

 3    Inc. v. Emp. Dev. Dep’t, 154 Cal. App. 4th 836, 846-47 (2007), demonstrated by the parties’

 4    “outward conduct” showing a mutual “intent to be bound,” Burch v. Premier Homes, LLC, 199

 5    Cal. App. 4th 730, 746 (2011); see also Cal. Civ. Code § 1565. None of the assortment of writings

 6    identified by Musk show the parties’ “mutual assent” to the terms he claims have been breached.

 7    The “cobble[d] together” correspondence is inadequate as a matter of law to constitute a contract.

 8    See Haskins v. Symantec Corp., 2013 WL 6234610, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 2, 2013). Nor does

 9    Musk plead facts demonstrating that anyone intended to be bound by the purported terms he alleges.

10    Compl. ¶¶ 251-54. And none of Musk’s alleged terms is “sufficiently definite . . . to constitute an

11    enforceable contract” in any event. Netbula, LLC v. BindView Dev. Corp., 516 F. Supp. 2d 1137,

12    1155 (N.D. Cal. 2007).

13           Musk likewise fails to plausibly allege any bargained-for consideration. “Consideration”

14    requires the recipient of a promise to confer a “benefit” or suffer a “prejudice” as “an inducement

15    to the promisor.” Cal. Civ. Code § 1605. It is not enough to unilaterally “confer a benefit or suffer

16    prejudice.” Orcilla v. Big Sur, Inc., 244 Cal. App. 4th 982, 1006 (2016). Instead, “consideration”

17    must be “bargained for”—a quid pro quo. Jara v. Suprema Meats, Inc., 121 Cal. App. 4th 1238,

18    1249 (2004). The FAC’s conclusory allegations do not show that the parties engaged in bargaining

19    of any kind.

20           Finally, even if the FAC adequately pleaded the existence of a contract, Musk has not

21    plausibly alleged any breach—the FAC’s conclusory allegations are untethered to the breach of

22    any specific contractual terms.

26    than an express contract, must be founded upon an ascertained agreement of the parties to perform

27    it,” requiring the same showing of mutual assent and bargained-for consideration (cleaned up)).

28    Musk does not plausibly allege conduct evincing mutual assent or bargained-for consideration or

10      II.      FRAUD CLAIMS

11              Musk seeks to repackage his breach of contract claims as various claims of fraud. Rather

12    than assert that Defendants made and then broke enforceable promises, Musk says those same

13    promises were made to him with no present intention to fulfill them and therefore constituted fraud.

14    See Compl. ¶¶ 309-10. But a failed contract claim cannot be repurposed in this manner. See, e.g.,

15    JMP Secs. LLP v. Altair Nanotech. Inc., 880 F. Supp. 2d 1029, 1043-44 (N.D. Cal. 2012)

16    (dismissing fraud claim where plaintiff attempted to take “allegations underpinning a

17    straightforward claim for breach of a commercial contract and recast them as [a] tort[]”).

18              A.     Fraud and unjust enrichment

19              Musk’s fraud (Count VII) and duplicative unjust enrichment (Count IV) claims fail because

20    he does not identify any promise made to him by any Defendant, or any facts suggesting that any

21    such promise was made with fraudulent intent, let alone plead these facts with the particularity

22    required under Rule 9(b). See Behnke v. State Farm Gen. Ins. Co., 196 Cal. App. 4th 1443, 1453,

23    1459 (2011); Valencia v. Sharp Elecs. Corp., 561 F. App’x 591, 593-94 (9th Cir. 2014).

24              These claims are also time-barred under the three-year limitations period for fraud claims.

25    See Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 338(d). Musk alleges that Altman’s “promise” was breached in February

26    2018, when Defendants devised a “structure for an equity fundraise . . . [to] convert to a for-profit

27    structure.” Compl. ¶ 106. Musk alleges he was personally notified of this plan, and that it was

28    disclosed publicly, in March 2019 (id. ¶ 114; id., Ex. 19), more than three years before he filed suit

 2           B.      RICO

 3           Musk and xAI’s civil RICO claims under 18 U.S.C. § 1962 (Counts XXV and XXVI) must

 4    likewise be dismissed. The FAC attempts to plead a pattern of racketeering activity based on wire

 5    fraud, requiring Musk and xAI to plead with particularity: “(A) the formation of a scheme to

 6    defraud, (B) the use of the . . . wires in furtherance of that scheme, and (C) the specific intent to

 7    defraud.” Eclectic Props. E., LLC v. Marcus & Millichap Co., 751 F.3d 990, 997 (9th Cir. 2014).

 8    But Musk and xAI identify no particular representation by any Defendant that was false when made,

 9    nor do they allege facts establishing an intent to defraud. Musk and xAI therefore do not adequately

10    allege any cognizable predicate acts.

11           Each of the RICO theories fails for additional reasons.

12           First, to state a claim under § 1962(c), Plaintiffs must allege “the existence of two distinct

13    entities: (1) a person . . . and (2) an ‘enterprise.’” Ferrari v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, 2016 WL

14    7188030, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 12, 2016). Musk and xAI identify the For-Profit Entities (Compl.

15    ¶ 509) among the “persons” participating in the alleged enterprise—here, OpenAI, Inc. (id.

16    ¶ 508)—violating RICO’s distinctiveness requirement. See Ferrari, 2016 WL 7188030, at *2;

17    Chagby v. Target Corp., 358 F. App’x 805, 808 (9th Cir. 2009). Nor do Musk and xAI plausibly

18    allege that Altman and Brockman “conduct[ed]” OpenAI’s operations “through a pattern of

19    racketeering,” as is required to state a claim under § 1962(c). See § 1962(c).

20           Second, Musk and xAI fail to identify “an injury” proximately caused by “the use or

21    investment of racketeering income” that is “distinct from an injury caused by the predicate acts

22    themselves,” defeating their § 1962(a) theory. Gjovik v. Apple Inc., 2024 WL 2309100, at *7 (N.D.

23    Cal. May 20, 2024) (emphasis in original).

24           Finally, their claim under § 1962(b) fares no better, as Musk and xAI plead no “injury,”

25    Metro Servs. Grp. v. Travelers Cas. & Sur. Co. of Am., 2021 WL 2633416, at *6 (N.D. Cal. June

26    25, 2021), that is “proximately caused” by Defendants’ alleged “acquisition or control” of the

27    enterprise, In re Toyota Motor Corp., 785 F. Supp. 2d 883, 921 (C.D. Cal. 2011).

28           These deficiencies also necessarily require dismissal of Plaintiffs’ claim for RICO

 2     III.      CHARITABLE TRUST CLAIMS

 3              A.     Breach of charitable trust

 4              Musk lacks standing to assert his charitable trust claim (Count XX) because he is a donor

 5    with no “reversionary, contractual, or property interest in” the assets of OpenAI, Inc., nor is he an

 6    officer or director of OpenAI, Inc. Cal. Corp. Code § 5142(a)(1)-(4); see also Pinkert v. Schwab

 7    Charitable Fund, 2021 WL 2476869, at *5-6 (N.D. Cal. June 17, 2021) (listing classes of plaintiffs

 8    with standing to assert breach of charitable trust under California law and noting that “a donor . . . is

 9    not among them”). His admission that he lacks a “reversionary interest” in OpenAI, Inc.’s assets,

10    see ECF No. 73, at 18, is fatal to his claim of standing as a “settlor” of a charitable trust, see Patton

11    v. Sherwood, 152 Cal. App. 4th 339, 342 (2007), and to his asserted “special interest” standing, see

12    Holt v. Coll. of Osteopathic Phys. & Surgeons, 61 Cal. 2d 750, 753 (1964). Nor does Musk’s

13    alleged contract with Altman and OpenAI, Inc. support his standing—he has failed to identify an

14    enforceable contract, let alone a “conditional contract” governing the use of his donations and

15    specifying that “failure to comply . . . would result in a forfeiture,” L.B. Rsch. & Educ. Found. v.

16    UCLA Found., 130 Cal. App. 4th 171, 179-80 (2005).

17              Musk also fails to adequately allege that the supposed terms of his donations (Compl.

18    ¶¶ 254, 420) were breached. None of his allegations plausibly suggest OpenAI accepted his

19    donations subject to the commitments he now claims.

20              B.     Constructive fraud

21              Musk’s constructive fraud claim (Count VI) is duplicative of his charitable trust claim and

22    is dismissed for the same reasons. Musk cannot cure his lack of standing to assert breach of

23    charitable trust simply by renaming the claim. See Pinkert, 2021 WL 2476869, at *6. And the claim

24    fails for lack of particularized allegations of fraud in any event. See In re Verisign, Inc. Derivative

25    Litig., 531 F. Supp. 2d 1173, 1219 (N.D. Cal. 2007) (dismissing constructive fraud claim under

26    Rule 9(b)).

 1            A.      Standing

 2            Federal law (Rule 23.1)—not the California law Plaintiffs allege (Cal. Corp. Code

 3    § 5710(b))—supplies the “procedural” rule governing derivative actions in federal court. Kona

 4    Enters., Inc. v. Est. of Bishop, 179 F.3d 767, 769 (9th Cir. 1999). Under Rule 23.1, standing is

 5    limited to “shareholders or members of a corporation” who “fairly and adequately represent the

 6    interests of” the corporation’s other shareholders or members. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23.1. As interpreted

 7    by the courts, this requires putative derivative plaintiffs to be members or shareholders at the time

 8    they file suit, and throughout the litigation. See Quinn v. Anvil Corp., 620 F.3d 1005, 1012 (9th Cir.

 9    2010); Pagtakhan-So v. Cueto, 2016 WL 617429, at *4-5 & n.6 (E.D. Ky. Feb. 16, 2016) (former

10    trustees of non-profit could not provide “fair[] and adequate[] represent[ation]”); Conboy v. Black

11    Diamond Props., Inc., 2010 WL 2944374, at *7-8 (M.D. Fla. July 23, 2010) (plaintiff who resigned

12    membership was not a “shareholder[] or member[] . . . at the time [of] fil[ing] [] suit” and thus

13    lacked standing). Neither Musk nor Zilis is currently a “member” of OpenAI: they are former

14    members who resigned from OpenAI long ago. They therefore lack standing to sue.

15            Even if California law applied, the result would be the same. Cal. Corp. Code § 5710(b)

16    provides that a “member” of a non-profit may sue derivatively. California law does not grant former

17    members derivative standing. See Turner v. Victoria, 15 Cal. 5th 99, 115, 124 (2023) (“[t]he

18    director enforcement statutes clearly indicate they require an individual who brings a lawsuit to be

19    a director when that person institutes the action”); Grosset v. Wenaas, 42 Cal. 4th 1100, 1111

20    (2008) (interpreting Cal. Corp. Code § 800(b) to require would-be stockholder derivative plaintiffs

21    to hold shares at the time suit is filed).

22            Nor have Musk and Zilis adequately alleged demand or demand futility, as required under

23    Cal. Corp. Code § 5710(b)(2). Their alleged “phone calls, in-person discussions, public statements

24    via social media, and emails” (Compl. ¶¶ 446, 462), and their purported “notice to the Board” two

25    hours before filing this complaint (id. ¶¶ 448, 464), fall short of the “particularized” allegations

26    necessary to show they “exhausted all [] means” to secure remedial action from OpenAI’s board,

27    Bader v. Anderson, 179 Cal. App. 4th 775, 789 (2009). And by alleging they made a demand (albeit

28    a deficient one), Musk and Zilis cannot claim that their purported demand was futile: they have

 6             B.     Breach of fiduciary duty

 7             Musk and Zilis’s breach of fiduciary duty claim (Count XXII) is barred by the business

 8    judgment rule, see Berg & Berg Enters., LLC v. Boyle, 178 Cal. App. 4th 1020, 1045 (2009)

 9    (cleaned up), as their allegations of bad faith and lack of due care are purely conclusory, see Compl.

10    ¶ 442; Lee v. Interinsurance Exch., 50 Cal. App. 4th 694, 714-15 (1996) (“conclusory allegations”

11    that board “acted for improper motives and as a result of a conflict of interest” are insufficient to

12    rebut the business judgment rule).

13             C.     Self-dealing

14             Musk and Zilis have not adequately pleaded a claim for self-dealing (Count XXIII) under

15    Cal. Corp. Code § 5233, which requires them to allege “a transaction to which the corporation is a

16    party and in which one or more of its directors has a material financial interest and which does not

17    meet the requirements of . . . subdivision (d).” Cal. Corp. Code § 5233(a); see also Sargent

18    Fletcher, Inc. v. Able Corp., 110 Cal. App. 4th 1658, 1669 (2003) (discrete issues “embedded

19    within [a statutory] definition” are “part of [plaintiff’s] prima facie case”). Section 5223(d) in turn

20    requires that the transaction not be one that the “corporation entered into . . . for its own benefit,”

21    that is “fair and reasonable,” and that has been authorized by disinterested and informed directors.

22    Cal. Corp. Code § 5233(d)(2). Musk and Zilis have not alleged sufficient facts to negate § 5233(d).

23    See Compl. ¶¶ 137, 139-44, 465.

24     VI.      ANTITRUST CLAIMS

25             A.     Standing

26             To state a claim under federal or California antitrust law, Plaintiffs must satisfy the

27    requirements of “antitrust standing.” See Pac. Recov. Sols. v. United Behav. Health, 481 F. Supp.

 5            B.      Investor boycott

 6            Plaintiffs argue that OpenAI engaged in a prohibited “group boycott” under Section 1 of

 7    the Sherman Act (Count IX) by allegedly requiring investors in its October 2024 funding round to

 8    forgo investment in competitors (Compl. ¶ 331(a)). Their single conclusory allegation in support

 9    of this claim (id. ¶ 201) fails to satisfy either element of a Section 1 claim: (1) an “agreement” that

10    (2) “unreasonably restrain[s] trade.” In re Musical Instruments & Equip. Antitrust Litig., 798 F.3d

11    1186, 1191 (9th Cir. 2015). Plaintiffs plead no facts suggesting OpenAI reached an “agreement”

12    with anyone to boycott investment in its competitors. Nor have they plausibly alleged a restraint

13    that is unreasonable “per se” or under the “rule of reason.” Flaa v. Hollywood Foreign Press Ass’n,

14    55 F.4th 680, 688 (9th Cir. 2022).

15            An alleged group boycott is only subject to “per se” treatment in the presence of a

16    “horizontal agreement,” either among the “initiators of the boycott . . . or those pressured into

17    joining”—the latter of which constitutes a “hub-and-spoke boycott.” Honey Bum, LLC v. Fashion

18    Nova, Inc., 63 F. 4th 813, 821 (9th Cir. 2023). As to the former, Plaintiffs fail to allege the existence

19    of any agreement between OpenAI and Microsoft, and their suggestion that “OpenAI’s conduct

20    should be imputed to Microsoft” for purposes of this claim, ECF No. 73, at 8, has no grounding in

21    law, see D’Augusta v. Am. Petroleum Inst., 117 F.4th 1094, 1104 (9th Cir. 2024). Nor have

22    Plaintiffs adequately alleged agreements between OpenAI and each investor (vertical “spokes”)

23    and agreements among the investors to that same effect (a horizontal “rim”), as required to plead

24    the existence of a hub-and-spoke boycott. See Honey Bum, 63 F.4th at 821.

25            By pleading this claim solely as a per se violation (Compl. ¶ 331), Plaintiffs have abandoned

26    any theory under the “rule of reason.” See Texaco Inc. v. Dagher, 547 U.S. 1, 7 & n.2 (2006). Even

27    if they had not, Plaintiffs fail to offer sufficient allegations (1) that Defendants’ conduct was

28    “intended to harm or restrain trade” and “actually injure[d] competition,” Brantley v. NBC

 3           C.      Board interlocks

 4           Plaintiffs next claim that alleged interlocking directorates between Microsoft and OpenAI’s

 5    boards violated § 8 of the Clayton Act (Count XVI). Specifically, they challenge two purported

 6    “interlocks”: (1) Hoffman’s service as a director on Microsoft’s and OpenAI’s boards from March

 7    2018 until March 2023 (Compl. ¶ 163 & n.8); and (2) Templeton’s service as Microsoft’s non-

 8    voting observer of OpenAI’s board from late 2023 through July 2024 (id. ¶ 168). Id. ¶¶ 368-82.

 9           Plaintiffs lack standing to assert this claim: Hoffman’s and Templeton’s departure from the

10    board long before Plaintiffs commenced a § 8 claim means there is no “case or controversy” on

11    which to ground Article III standing, Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83,

12    109 (1998), and Plaintiffs allege no individualized antitrust injury from these purported interlocks

13    that could give rise to antitrust standing, see Bearden v. Ballad Health, 967 F.3d 513, 517-18 (6th

14    Cir. 2020).

15           Plaintiffs also fail to state a § 8 violation. They do not plausibly allege that Microsoft and

16    OpenAI were competitors during Hoffman’s tenure, as is necessary to implicate § 8. See 15 U.S.C.

17    § 19(a)(1)(B). Nor does Templeton’s prior service as an “observer” implicate § 8, which applies

18    only to “director[s] or officer[s]” of competing corporations—a person eligible to “act for such

19    corporation[s] in such capacit[ies].” See id. § 19(a)(1), (b). “When the term ‘director’ is used in

20    reference to a corporation”—as it is in § 8—“the term plainly means a person who is a member of

21    the governing board of the corporation and participates in corporate governance.” In re Kunz, 489

22    F.3d 1072, 1077-78 (10th Cir. 2007). Templeton was not a “member” of the “governing board” of

23    OpenAI, id., nor did she have the capability to “act for [the] corporation,” 15 U.S.C. § 19(b). She

24    therefore did not qualify as a “director” under the statute. Id.
