 1                                             INTRODUCTION

 2           Relying on the opinions of Dr. C. Paul Wazzan, Elon Musk seeks to recover between

 3    $65.50 billion and $109.43 billion from the nonprofit OpenAI Foundation plus an additional

 4    $13.30 billion to $25.06 billion from Microsoft. Wazzan opines that Musk is entitled to be paid

 5    these amounts—up to $135 billion total—because he donated approximately $38 million to the

 6    OpenAI nonprofit and made certain nonmonetary contributions to the organization in its early

 7    years. Wazzan’s methodology is made up; his results unverifiable; his approach admittedly

 8    unprecedented; and his proposed outcome—the transfer of billions of dollars from a nonprofit

 9    corporation to a donor-turned competitor—implausible on its face.

10           Wazzan’s analysis begins in familiar territory, with proffered valuations of the OpenAI for-

11    profit organization (step one) and of the nonprofit’s controlling stake in that organization (step

12    two). But then it departs from reliable expertise and deploys a “methodology” that Wazzan invented

13    for this case to justify the conclusion that the OpenAI nonprofit has been unjustly enriched by an

14    amount 1700x to 2900x greater than the total amount of Musk’s charitable donations, and that Musk

15    should receive that value instead.

16           Wazzan’s approach to unjust enrichment proceeds from the incoherent assumption that if

17    Musk prevails on any of his claims, he should be compensated as if he had an “economic interest”—

18    an equity stake—in the nonprofit. It is legally impossible for private individuals to hold economic

19    interests in nonprofits, and Wazzan conceded at deposition that he had no reason to believe Musk

20    “expected a financial return when he donated . . . to OpenAI nonprofit.” Ex. 4 (Wazzan Dep. Tr.)

21    at 75:1-9.1 Wazzan’s opinions about Musk’s purported “economic interest” therefore flunk the Rule

22    702(a) requirement that expert testimony “help the trier of fact . . . determine a fact in issue.”

18    OpenAI nonprofit using a three-step methodology. At step one, Wazzan seeks to estimate the value

21    including the nonprofit, based in part on Black-Scholes modeling. Id. ¶¶ 52-70. Defendants are not

22    challenging as unreliable under Rule 702 Wazzan’s methodologies or his opinions at step one or

23    step two, which comprise the bulk of his Opening Report and simply present valuations of the

24    OpenAI for-profit and of the nonprofit’s stake in the for-profit—points not at issue in this case.

11           Wazzan’s opinions thus rest on his counterfactual view that all OpenAI cofounders agreed

14    2025) (en banc), the Federal Circuit held that the district court abused its discretion by permitting

15    a damages expert in an infringement case to testify based on a “hypothetical negotiation”—

16    specifically, the royalty to which the litigants would have agreed had they successfully negotiated

17    an agreement before the challenged infringement—where the evidentiary record could only support

18    what one party may have been willing to accept. Here, Wazzan commits the same error: he treats

19    failed negotiations as probative not only of the equity stake Musk might have accepted, but also of

20    what all cofounders would have agreed to, without evidentiary support.
