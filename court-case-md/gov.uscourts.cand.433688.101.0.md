 1                              STATEMENT OF ISSUES TO BE DECIDED

 2

 3         1. Whether the Eleventh Amendment doctrine of sovereign immunity bars suits against

 4   Defendant (Involuntary Plaintiff), Rob Bonta, in his official capacity as Attorney General of

 5   California.

 6         2. Whether Plaintiffs have stated a claim for relief against the Attorney General in the First

 7   Amended Complaint.

 8                                             INTRODUCTION

 9           The Attorney General seeks an order dismissing him from Plaintiffs’ First Amended

10   Complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Eleventh Amendment, and for failure

11   to state a claim against the Attorney General.

12           Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint named the Attorney General as an involuntary

13   plaintiff, and as a defendant, pursuant to Federal Rules of Civil Procedure 19. The joinder of the

14   Attorney General is improper because, unlike individuals, the Attorney General has sovereign

15   immunity and cannot be coerced into a lawsuit. Because Plaintiffs’ claim against the Attorney

16   General cannot be salvaged, the Court should dismiss the Attorney General from the First

17   Amended Complaint with prejudice.

18                              FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY

19           On November 14, 2024, Plaintiffs Elon Musk, individually and derivatively on behalf of

20   OpenAI, Inc., Shivon Zilis, derivatively on behalf of OpenAI, Inc., and X.AI Corp. filed their

21   First Amended Complaint (ECF No. 52) in response to a Motion to Dismiss filed by Defendants

22   Samuel Altman, et al. (ECF No. 25). The First Amended Complaint includes a new cause of

23   action, Count XXIII, alleging self-dealing under section 5233 of the California Corporations

24   Code. Count XXIII is the only count in which the Attorney General, in his official capacity as the

25   Attorney General of California, is named, but there is no allegation against the Attorney General.

 1                                                 ARGUMENT

 2         I.      LEGAL STANDARDS

 3              A. Rule 12(b)(1) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure

 4                Dismissal is appropriate when the Court lacks subject matter jurisdiction over a claim. Fed.

 5   R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1); Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101-102 (1998). “If the

 6   court determines at any time that it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, the court must dismiss the

 7   action.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1). Rule 12(b)(1) allows a party to raise the defense that the court

 8   lacks “jurisdiction over the subject matter” of a claim. It is the plaintiff’s burden, the party

 9   asserting federal subject matter jurisdiction, to prove jurisdiction at the time the action is

10   commenced. Chandler v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 598 F.3d 1115, 1121-22 (9th Cir.

11   2010); Williams v. Facebook, Inc., 498 F. Supp. 3d 1189, 1194 (N.D. Cal. 2019).

12              B. Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure

13                A court should grant a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim under Federal Rule of

14   Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) if the complaint does not proffer “enough facts to state a claim to relief

15   that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007). “A claim has

16   facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the

17   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. In considering a

18   motion to dismiss, the Court “must accept as true all of the factual allegations contained in the

19   complaint.” Navarro v. Block, 250 F.3d 729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001). But dismissal can be based on

20   the lack of a cognizable legal theory or the absence of sufficient facts alleged to support that

21   theory. Balistreri v. Pacifica Police Dep’t, 901 F.2d 696, 699 (9th Cir. 1990).

22                Dismissal without leave to amend is appropriate when deficiencies in the complaint could

23   not possibly be cured by amendment. See Jackson v. Carey, 353 F.3d 750, 758 (9th Cir. 2003);

24   see also Watison v. Carter, 668 F.3d 1108, 1117 (9th Cir. 2012) (dismissal without leave to

25   amend is appropriate if the district court “determines that the pleading could not possibly be cured

26   by the allegation of other facts,” quoting Doe v. United States, 58 F.3d 494, 497 (9th Cir. 1995)).

 1    II.    LEGAL ARGUMENT

 2          A. This Court Lacks Jurisdiction Because the Attorney General of California Is

 3             Immune from Suit in Federal Court.

 4          The Eleventh Amendment prohibits suit against a state or its instrumentalities for legal or

 5   equitable relief, in the absence of consent or waiver by the state or an abrogation of that immunity

 6   by Congress. Papasan v. Allain, 478 U.S. 265, 276–77 (1986); Pennhurst State Sch. & Hosp. v.

 7   Halderman, 465 U.S. 89, 100 (1984). “A State’s constitutional interest in immunity encompasses

 8   not merely whether it may be sued, but where it may be sued.” Welch v. State Dep’t of Highways

 9   & Public Transp., 483 U.S. 468, 473 (1987). That immunity extends to state officials when “the

10   state is the real, substantial party in interest,” such as, for example, when the damages sought

11   would be paid by the state. Pennhurst, 465 U.S. at 101 & n.11; see also Edelman v. Jordan, 415

12   U.S. 651, 663 (1974) (lawsuit by a private party “seeking to impose a liability which must be paid

13   from public funds in the state treasury is barred by the Eleventh Amendment.”)

14          The Office of the Attorney General is undoubtedly an arm of the state entitled to immunity

15   in federal court. Deanco Healthcare, LLC v. Becerra, 365 F.Supp.3d 1029, 1036 (C.D. Cal.

16   2019). Plaintiffs’ claim against Defendant Rob Bonta in his official capacity as “Attorney General

17   of California,” is against the Attorney General’s Office, and thus is no different from suing the

18   state itself. Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 491 U.S. 58, 71 (1989). Plaintiffs named the

19   Attorney General as a defendant, which they believe to be “consistent with Fed. R. Civ. P.

20   19(a)(2).” (FAC Introduction ¶ n.2). But Plaintiffs have not alleged that the Attorney General has

21   waived sovereign immunity and consented to be sued in federal court. Indeed, Plaintiffs agree

22   that there was no such consent. Id. In the absence of a state voluntarily availing itself of federal

23   court jurisdiction, or an express waiver of sovereign immunity, Rule 19 must yield to the state’s

24   assertion of sovereign immunity. Gensetix, Inc. v. Bd. of Regents of Univ. of Tex. Sys., 966 F.3d

25   1316, 1324 (Fed. Cir. 2020). Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ attempt to join the Attorney General in any

26   capacity—whether characterized as an Involuntary Plaintiff or Defendant under Rule 19—fails.

27          When a federal claim is dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, the court has no

28   discretion to retain supplemental jurisdiction over the plaintiff’s state law claims. Scott v.

 5          B. The California Corporations Code Does Not Confer Federal Jurisdiction

 6          California Corporations Code section 5233(c) states in relevant part that the “Attorney

 7    General or, if the Attorney General is joined as an indispensable party, any of the following may

 8    bring an action in the superior court of the proper county for the remedies specified in

 9    subdivision (h).” Cal. Corp. Code, § 5233 (c) (emphasis added). Finding no procedure for naming

10    indispensable parties in federal courts, Plaintiffs instead invoked Federal Rule of Civil Procedure

11    19(a)(2) and section 382 of the California Code of Civil Procedure as authority for naming the

12    Attorney General as an Involuntary Plaintiff and for seeking relief against the Attorney General.

13    (FAC ¶¶ 459-460). However, neither statute confers subject matter jurisdiction over the Attorney

14    General as an indispensable party in federal district court, or provides an exception to the doctrine

15    of sovereign immunity. Thus, the Attorney General must be dismissed with prejudice from the

16    First Amended Complaint

17          C. Count XXIII Fails to State a Claim for Relief Against the Attorney General

18              Under California’s Nonprofit Corporation Law.

19          A motion to dismiss is proper under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure

20    if a plaintiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief may be granted. In considering a motion

21    to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), the court must accept all material facts alleged in complaint as

22    true and construe them in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. Erickson v. Pardus,

23    551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007); Knievel v. ESPN, 393 F.3d 1068, 1072 (9th Cir. 2005). But the court need

24    not accept conclusory allegations and unwarranted inferences. Adams v. Johnson, 355 F.3d 1179,

25    1183 (9th Cir. 2004) (citation omitted). And “[d]ismissal is proper when the complaint does not

26    make out a cognizable legal theory or does not allege sufficient facts to support a cognizable legal

27    theory.” Cervantes v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., 656 F.3d 1034, 1041 (9th Cir. 2011).

 1          Plaintiffs Musk and Zilis summarily allege that joining the Attorney General in Count

 2    XXIII, and in the prayer for relief, confers standing. (FAC ¶¶ 459-460). But critically, Plaintiffs

 3    do not allege fault or wrongdoing by the Attorney General. Given Plaintiffs’ failure to state a

 4    claim for relief, the Attorney General must be dismissed from the First Amended Complaint.

 5    Moss v. U.S. Secret Service, 572 F.3d 962, 969 (9th Cir. 2009) (“In sum, for a complaint to

 6    survive a motion to dismiss, the non-conclusory ‘factual content,’ and reasonable inferences from

 7    that content, must be plausibly suggestive of a claim entitling the plaintiff to relief.”)

 8                                               CONCLUSION

 9          For the foregoing reasons, the Attorney General respectfully requests that the Court grant

10    this motion to dismiss the Attorney General from the First Amended Complaint, with prejudice.
