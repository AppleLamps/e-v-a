 1                                           INTRODUCTION

 2          Plaintiff Elon Musk contributed $38 million to OpenAI – roughly 60% of the nonprofit’s

 3 early funding.     He also contributed countless non-monetary benefits, including prestige and

 4 reputation that gave the nonprofit instant credibility with recruits and business partners. Musk made

 5 those contributions on the understanding that OpenAI was and would remain a nonprofit dedicated

 6 to the public good. But once Musk left the nonprofit, his co-founders reinvented OpenAI as a get

 7 rich quick scheme, selling out the nonprofit for personal gain.

 8          OpenAI now seeks to deny Musk any remedy for that misconduct based on technicalities. It

 9 asserts that Musk lacks standing to sue for breach of charitable trust or constructive fraud because

10 Musk made most of his contributions indirectly – some through his personal donor advised funds

11 (“DAFs”) and others through the fiscal sponsor YC.org that OpenAI designated to receive

12 contributions on its behalf while it waited for the IRS to approve its own tax exemption.

13          Those arguments are meritless. This Court has already ruled that the “settlor” of a trust has

14 standing to sue. Dkt. 121 at 15 n.11. Musk was the settlor of his contributions to OpenAI, whether

15 he made them directly or through intermediaries. Moreover, YC.org acted as OpenAI’s agent – and

16 the DAFs acted as Musk’s agents – when they processed charitable contributions. OpenAI’s

17 insistence that those intermediaries exercised independent discretion over donations is a fiction

18 based on generic terms in written policies and program guidelines that do not track the actual role

19 those intermediaries played. Longstanding California case law makes clear that language in

20 contracts or other documents that purports to describe the parties’ relationship is not grounds for

21 summary judgment when other evidence shows that the true facts are otherwise.

22          OpenAI fares no better challenging the unjust enrichment and fraud claims. Musk can

23 pursue his unjust enrichment claim in the alternative to his tort claims. And OpenAI’s “expectation

24 of compensation” requirement purports to impose an arbitrary limitation on a cause of action that

25 California law frames in conspicuously broad and flexible terms. Finally, OpenAI’s challenge to

26 the fraud claim rests on factual disputes that are plainly questions for the jury.

27          OpenAI’s motion should be denied. This case should proceed to trial.

 2          A.      Musk and Altman’s Formation of OpenAI as a Nonprofit

 3          In 2015, Musk harbored deep concerns about artificial intelligence. For-profit companies

 4 like Google, he worried, were not “taking AI safety seriously.” Ex. 1 (Musk Tr.) at 22:5-23:3. Musk

 5 believed that AI should be pursued instead by a nonprofit that would develop AI safely for the

 6 benefit of humanity as a whole. Id.

 7          Musk thought he had found kindred spirits in Sam Altman and Greg Brockman. Over

 8 several months, the three of them discussed setting up a new nonprofit to pursue AI research for the

 9 public good. Throughout those discussions, Altman and Brockman reassured Musk that they shared

10 his priorities. In May 2015, for example, Altman proposed that they “structure [the entity] so that

11 the tech belongs to the world via some sort of nonprofit.” Ex. 2. He later added: “The technology

12 would be owned by the foundation and used ‘for the good of the world,’ ” and “safety should be a

13 first-class requirement.” Ex. 3. Musk “[a]gree[d] on all.” Id.; Ex. 1 (Musk Tr.) at 23:9-27:19.

14 Brockman similarly wrote that the “organization needed to be a non-profit, without any competing

15 incentives to dilute its mission,” “to ensure that AI was beneficial.” Ex. 4 at 4-5.

16          Altman proposed that Musk be the nonprofit’s primary financial supporter. Ex. 5. Musk

17 responded that “governance” was “critical,” because he “[did]n’t want to fund something that goes

18 in what turns out to be the wrong direction.” Id. Musk proposed “[d]oing this as an independent,

19 pure play 501c3, but with a crystal clear focus on the positive advent of strong AI distributed widely

20 to humanity.” Ex. 6 at 2. Altman agreed that “an independent 501c3 is correct.” Id.

                    1.      Musk’s Common Law Settlor Standing Does Not Depend on Whether
 8                          Musk Made His Contributions Through an Intermediary

 9          The “settlor” of a trust is the “one who furnishes the consideration for [the] trust.” Ruocco

10 v. Bateman, Eichler, Hill, Richards, Inc., 903 F.2d 1232, 1239 (9th Cir. 1990). OpenAI claims that

11 Musk does not qualify under that definition because the DAFs and YC.org contributed his funds to

12 OpenAI. OpenAI Mem. at 5-8. That argument ignores that the “settlor” is the person who supplies

13 the consideration to create the trust, even if the funds flow through an intermediary.

14          In Security-First National Bank of Los Angeles v. Wright, 47 Cal. App. 2d 787 (1941), for

15 example, the court held that a decedent wife was the settlor of a trust because she supplied the trust

16 funds, even though “the husband transferred [the funds] to the . . . trustee.” Id. at 789, 793. “[T]he

17 wife furnished the consideration for the declaration of trust, and not the husband. Hence she, rather

18 than the husband, must be deemed the settlor.” Id. at 793.

19          Similarly, in Lehman v. Commissioner, 109 F.2d 99 (2d Cir. 1940), a case the Ninth Circuit

20 relied on in Ruocco, 903 F.2d at 1239, the Second Circuit held that a decedent taxpayer was the

21 settlor of a trust even though he contributed the funds through his brother. “A person who furnishes

22 the consideration for the creation of a trust is the settlor, even though in form the trust is created by

23 another.” 109 F.2d at 100 (quoting Scott on Trusts § 156.3) (emphasis added).

24          Other cases reach similar results. See, e.g., In re Brooks, 844 F.2d 258, 263 (5th Cir. 1988)

25 (“The mold in which the transaction is cast does not determine who is the settlor of a trust. The

26 person who provides the consideration for a trust is the settlor even if another person or entity

27 nominally creates the trust.”); Guar. Tr. Co. of N.Y. v. N.Y. Tr. Co., 74 N.E.2d 232, 234 (N.Y. 1947)

28 (“[T]he trust was created at the behest of Sullivan’s undisclosed client, and its corpus formed from
