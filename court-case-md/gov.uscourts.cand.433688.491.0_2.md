 1          Trial begins in five days but Plaintiff still refuses to state plainly what claims he will pursue
 2   and what remedies he will seek. These evasive tactics appear designed to obscure that Plaintiff has
 3   abandoned every remedy available to him and has no cognizable theory of relief remaining. The
 4   OpenAI Defendants would be pleased to present further briefing to the Court at its convenience to
 5   establish the irretrievable deficiencies in Plaintiff’s retooled lawsuit. At a minimum, to avoid
 6   prejudice at trial, Plaintiff’s gamesmanship calls for immediate redress in the form of an order
 7   (1) precluding Plaintiff from pursuing disgorgement; and (2) directing Plaintiff to file compliant
 8   notices (a) dismissing (or not) his fraud and constructive fraud claims and (b) unequivocally
 9   waiving all remedies in which Plaintiff has any interest.
10                                            BACKGROUND
11          Months ago, Elon Musk avoided summary judgment by persuading the Court he could
12   adduce evidence showing he (1) retained control rights over contributions made to donor advised
13   funds (“DAFs”) and fiscal sponsors that in turn donated to OpenAI, Inc., see Dkt. No. 350 at 14;
14   Dkt. No. 390 at 15; and (2) was personally defrauded, see Dkt. No. 350 at 22-23, Dkt. No. 390 at
15   17-19. In his Notice of Remedies, also filed months ago, the sole articulated remedy Musk
16   identified on any of the claims set for trial was a disgorgement award to him personally, tied to his
17   personal contributions to OpenAI. Dkt. 392. The evidentiary and expert record had been developed
18   on the basis of this asserted remedy, and this asserted remedy only.
19          Two and a half weeks before trial, Musk filed an “Amended” Notice of Remedies that bore
20   no resemblance to the initial notice and very little connection to the evidentiary record. Dkt. 459.
21   Musk abandoned the personal harm theories he had pressed to keep this case alive, abandoned the
22   only remedy he had been pursuing to date (disgorgement to him personally, from the OpenAI
23   nonprofit), and sought to cloak himself as representative of the public interest. Never mind that the
24   Office of the California Attorney General deemed him unfit for the role over a year ago. Dkt. 157-1.
25   In Musk’s new telling, it is not he but the OpenAI nonprofit (still a defendant on all claims) or the
26   DAFs (never named in this suit) who have been harmed and should receive a disgorgement award.
27   Dkt. 484. Because Musk no longer seeks to enforce duties allegedly owed to him by virtue of his

 6                                              ARGUMENT
 7           Musk’s remedies shell game should not be countenanced: He (now) asserts no personal
 8   injury to be redressed, and has no standing to sue on behalf of the public, the OpenAI nonprofit, or
 9   the DAFs. A plaintiff cannot come to court just to air a grievance. If Musk is permitted to proceed
10   at all, he should at a minimum be precluded from pursuing any disgorgement remedy. That remedy
11   has no legal basis however framed, and Musk’s ever-shifting articulations of its framing—to whom
12   does it go? from whom is it paid? on what factual basis? on what legal authority?—put Defendants
13   in an impossible position on the eve of trial.
14          The OpenAI Defendants further request an order (1) directing Musk to immediately file a
15   compliant notice regarding his fraud claims—one that either voluntarily dismisses the claims or
16   does not; and (2) directing Musk to supplement his waiver of personal remedies to comply with the
17   Court’s April 17, 2026 order, Dkt. 477.
18          I.      Musk should at a minimum be precluded from seeking disgorgement.
19          In their April 10 response to Plaintiff’s “Amended Notice of Remedies,” the OpenAI
20   Defendants argued that Musk could not lawfully pursue either a non-personal derivative
21   disgorgement remedy—on behalf of a defendant whom he does not represent—or the exorbitant
22   and derivative injunctive relief described in the amended notice. Dkt. 460. Now, Musk has moved
23   the goal posts once more, suggesting that disgorgement could flow not to him or the OpenAI
24   nonprofit but to the non-party DAFs that actually made the contributions. Dkt. 484 at 1.
25          None of the relief Musk is pursuing now is available to him—and Musk’s latest efforts to
26   marshal authority only underscore the point. Musk invokes the cy pres doctrine, but cy pres
27   generally applies only “when a charity is originally or later becomes impossible, inexpedient, or

 5          As for Musk’s new ask for a “return” of any wrongful gains to the DAFs, he cites no
 6   authority that would allow him to make that ask on behalf of parties that have never been in this
 7   case. The DAFs—entities owned and controlled by Vanguard and Fidelity—have not appeared in

 9   seek a remedy. Even more fundamentally, the request undermines Musk’s very basis for
10   maintaining his underlying claims. The Court denied summary judgment on Musk’s charitable trust
11   claim based on his allegations that he imposed restrictions on the donations he made through DAFs
12   (and fiscal sponsors), and retained the continuing right to enforce those restrictions. See Dkt. 390
13   at 15. By suggesting instead that the DAFs have the relevant property rights—that they are the
14   owners of the claim and they are the rightful beneficiaries of any remedy—Musk appears to close
15   off the pathway to standing the Court recognized in its summary judgment ruling.
16          The OpenAI Defendants are mindful of the Court’s intention to “address the availability of
17   Musk’s proposed equitable remedies at a later date.” Dkt. 477 at 3. But Musk’s shifting, illogical,
18   and untenable positions on remedy raise serious questions whether he has any claim here at all. At
19   a minimum, he should not be permitted to seek any disgorgement remedy. He has already
20   abandoned the theory of disgorgement he pursued for nearly the entirety of this case and has no
21   standing to pursue his new theory. Plaintiff’s other recently-articulated remedies fail for reasons
22   noted above and summarized in Dkt. 460. As set out in that filing, there is no case left to try. The
23   OpenAI Defendants are prepared to submit briefing further establishing why at any time convenient
24   to the Court.

26          During the April 17 conference, Plaintiff’s counsel announced that Musk had “decided to
27   dismiss” his claims for fraud and constructive fraud. 4/17/26 Hearing Tr. at 5-6. After further

 9          No Federal Rule allows a litigant to bargain with the Court like this. If Musk wants to

12          III.    Musk should be directed to supplement his waiver.
13          On April 17, the Court directed Musk to verify that none of he, xAI, or any other Musk-
14   controlled fund would “seek an award . . . as to all remaining causes of action.” Dkt. 477 at 4. The
15   “remaining causes” include the antitrust claims that xAI filed and that are slated for Phase II
16   proceedings. Musk’s verified waiver makes no mention of these and instead seeks to limit its
17   compliance with the Court’s order to “his claims set for trial,” Dkt. 484 at 1. Musk also reserves
18   the right to seek nominal damages in his favor “if the Court concludes that a claim for nominal
19   damages is necessary to support Musk’s standing, to establish any cause of action, or to overcome
20   any other impediment to effective relief.” Dkt. 484 at 1.
21          While Musk thus confirms abandonment of any claim for disgorgement in his favor, he
22   refuses to comply with the Court’s order. Musk should be directed to supplement his waiver or else
23   forgo the right to make the arguments identified in the Court’s April 17 order.
