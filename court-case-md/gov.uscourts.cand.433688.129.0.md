 1                                             INTRODUCTION1

 2           Defendant Rob Bonta, in his official capacity as Attorney General of California (the

 3 “Attorney General”), moves to dismiss his involvement in this litigation. The Attorney General

 4 argues (1) sovereign immunity under the Eleventh Amendment, (2) that the California Corporations
 5 Code does not confer federal jurisdiction, and (3) Plaintiffs fail to state a claim for relief against
 6 him. The Attorney General’s Motion to Dismiss should be denied because joining the Attorney
 7 General as an indispensable party under these circumstances falls within the well-established Ex
 8 parte Young exception to sovereign immunity, is necessary to Plaintiffs’ derivative action, and is
 9 permitted by federal courts addressing similar state statutory schemes.
10                                            LEGAL STANDARD

11           Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1), a defendant may move to dismiss a claim

12 for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, such as on the basis of sovereign immunity. “Once
13 challenged, the party asserting subject matter jurisdiction has the burden of proving its existence.”
14 Rattlesnake Coalition v. EPA, 509 F.3d 1095, 1102 n.1 (9th Cir. 2007). However, in considering a
15 Rule 12(b)(1) motion, courts must “take as true all material allegations in the complaint” and
16 “construe the complaint in favor of the plaintiff.” Arizona v. Yellen, 34 F.4th 841, 849 (9th Cir.
17 2022) (citing Warth v. Seldin, 422 U.S. 490, 501 (1975)).
18           Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), dismissal is appropriate when the complaint

19 lacks “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atl. Corp. v.
20 Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007). To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain
21 sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”
22 Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009).

 2     I.   PLAINTIFFS’ JOINDER OF THE ATTORNEY GENERAL AS AN INVOLUNTARY
            PLAINTIFF DOES NOT IMPLICATE SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY.
 3

 4          The Attorney General attempts to characterize his joinder as an impermissible “coercion”

 5 into federal court. However, the Attorney General misunderstands the nature of his joinder.
 6 Plaintiffs do not seek any form of relief from the Attorney General, nor do they allege any
 7 wrongdoing by him. This is the reason he is an involuntary plaintiff rather than a true defendant.
 8          Moreover, modern sovereign immunity jurisprudence has two touchstones: the effect of the

 9 plaintiff’s request for relief on the state treasury and “‘the indignity of subjecting a [s]tate to the
10 coercive process of judicial tribunals at the instance of private parties.’” Kohn v. State Bar of
11 California, 87 F.4th 1021, 1027 (9th Cir. 2023), cert. denied, 144 S. Ct. 1465, 218 L. Ed. 2d 694
12 (2024) (internal citation omitted). With respect to this, the California Legislature has already
13 determined there is no dignitary interest in avoiding coercive process under Section 5233 by
14 specifically providing for the Attorney General’s joinder by private plaintiffs, and doing so in
15 language evidencing such joinder would not be at the Attorney General’s initiative. Cal. Corp. Code
16 § 5233(c) (“The Attorney General or, if the Attorney General is joined as an indispensable party
17 ….” (emphasis added)). Further, even if Plaintiffs were seeking affirmative relief from the Attorney
18 General, which they are not, the relief they seek is equitable, not monetary damages from the state
19 treasury.
20    II.   EVEN IF THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S JOINDER DID IMPLICATE
21          SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY, THE EX PARTE YOUNG EXCEPTION APPLIES.

22          While the Attorney General correctly notes that the Eleventh Amendment generally

23 prohibits federal courts from hearing suits against states and their instrumentalities, this principle is
24 subject to several well-established exceptions. Relevant here, under the doctrine established in Ex
25 parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), federal courts may exercise jurisdiction over state officials in
26 their official capacities for claims seeking prospective injunctive relief to prevent ongoing
27 violations of federal law. Idaho v. Coeur d’Alene Tribe of Idaho, 521 U.S. 261, 269 (1997).

 1          While Ex parte Young is traditionally understood as permitting federal courts to hear claims

 2 against state officials for violations of federal law, the doctrine also extends to state law claims that
 3 are properly before the federal court under supplemental jurisdiction. In Pennhurst State School &

 4 Hospital v. Halderman, 465 U.S. 89 (1984), the Supreme Court held that the Eleventh Amendment
 5 bars federal courts from ordering state officials to conform their conduct to state law. However,
 6 again, Plaintiffs do not seek to compel the Attorney General to do anything, whether under state law
 7 or otherwise. Even if they did, nothing in Pennhurst precludes the joinder of state officials where
 8 necessary to provide complete relief on federal claims or closely related state claims. See Edelman
 9 v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651, 653 n.1 (1974).
10          Here, Count XXIII for self-dealing under California Corporations Code Section 5233 is

11 closely related to Plaintiffs’ federal claims. The alleged self-dealing by defendants Altman and
12 Brockman forms part of the same pattern of conduct that underlies Plaintiffs’ federal antitrust,
13 RICO, and Lanham Act claims, as well as the UCL claim over which there is federal jurisdiction.
14 The diversion of OpenAI, Inc.’s charitable assets for private gain is central to the overall scheme
15 alleged in the FAC. Because the Attorney General’s presence is necessary to adjudicate claims that
16 arise from the same nucleus of operative facts as Plaintiffs’ federal claims, but Plaintiffs seek no
17 relief from the Attorney General, the Ex parte Young exception applies.
18          The Attorney General is named as an indispensable party solely because California

19 Corporations Code Section 5233(c) requires his joinder for Plaintiffs to pursue their derivative
20 claim for self-dealing. This statutory requirement reflects the Attorney General’s unique role in
21 supervising charitable organizations under California law, and the California Legislature determined
22 that his joinder is necessary to provide complete relief to persons like Plaintiffs.
23          The Ninth Circuit has consistently recognized that state officials may be joined in federal

24 court actions when they have responsibility for enforcing a statute, as indeed the Attorney General
25 has regarding the misuse of the charitable form for self-dealing. See, e.g., Nat’l Audubon Soc’y, Inc.
26 v. Davis, 307 F.3d 835, 847 (9th Cir. 2002) (holding that state officials could be joined in federal
27 court action); Los Angeles Cnty. Bar Ass’n v. Eu, 979 F.2d 697, 704 (9th Cir. 1992) (finding that

     III.   FEDERAL COURTS HAVE JURISDICTION OVER STATE LAW CLAIMS
 4          THAT REQUIRE JOINDER OF THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.

 5          The Attorney General argues that California Corporations Code Section 5233 does not
 6 confer federal jurisdiction. But, of course, there would be no way for a state statute to confer federal
 7 jurisdiction. Plaintiffs do not assert that Section 5233 itself confers federal jurisdiction. Rather,
 8 federal jurisdiction over this action is based on 15 U.S.C. § 4, 15 U.S.C § 1121, 18 U.S.C. § 1964,
 9 and 28 U.S.C. § 1331, as clearly stated in Paragraph 41 of the FAC.
10          The Court has supplemental jurisdiction over the state law claims, including Count XXIII,
11 pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1367, because these claims form part of the same case or controversy under
12 Article III. The Attorney General’s joinder is as a necessary party to a state law claim that falls
13 within the Court’s supplemental jurisdiction. Indeed, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 19 explicitly
14 contemplates the joinder of parties necessary for the adjudication of claims properly before the
15 Court.
16          Furthermore, federal courts clearly have the power to exercise supplemental jurisdiction
17 over state officials when doing so, as here, does not offend principles of sovereign immunity. In
18 Edelman v. Jordan, 415 U.S. 651 (1974), the Supreme Court held that “the District Court was
19 correct in exercising pendent jurisdiction over [plaintiffs’] statutory claim,” id. 653, n.1, but then
20 concluded that the Eleventh Amendment barred an award of retroactive relief on that pendent claim.
21 Id. at 678. As discussed above, Plaintiffs in this case seek no retroactive or other relief against the
22 Attorney General prohibited by sovereign immunity.
23          The fact that California Corporations Code Section 5233(c) refers to actions being brought
24 “in the superior court of the proper county” does not limit federal supplemental jurisdiction. It is an
25 ancient principle of federal jurisprudence that state statutes cannot restrict federal jurisdiction where
26 it otherwise exists. See Railway Co. v. Whitton, 80 U.S. 270, 286 (1871) (“[W]herever a general
27 right is conferred by the laws of a State, and a general remedy for its violation exists in the courts of
28 the State, that right may be enforced and that remedy pursued in the Federal courts as well as in the
29

30                                                                        PLS.’ OPPOSITION TO CAL. AG MTD
 1 courts of the State. It cannot be withdrawn from the cognizance of such Federal court by any

 2 provision of State legislation that it shall only be enforced in a State court.”). The principle is not
 3 only ancient, but enduring. E.g., U.S. ex rel. Galmines v. Novartis Pharms. Corp., No. CIV.A. 06-

 4 3213, 2013 WL 5924962 (E.D. Pa. Nov. 5, 2013) (“This rule, that 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a) controls,
 5 rather than state law, is not merely an implication from [City of Chicago v. Int'l Coll. of Surgeons,
 6 522 U.S. 156, 164–65 (1997) (“ICS”)]. Rather, although the ICS Court did not cite to Railway Co.
 7 v. Whitton’s Administrator, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 270 (1872), it clearly relied on a foundational
 8 principle found in that case [namely, the quoted portion above].”); Elgard Corp. v. Brennan Const.
 9 Co., 157 F.R.D. 1, 2 (D. Conn. 1994) (“While a state statute may mandate that a bond suit be filed
10 only in a particular state court, Johnson Acoustics, Inc. v. P.J. Carlin Construction et al., 29
11 Conn.Supp. 457, 292 A.2d 273, 276 (1971), a state statute cannot divest a federal District Court of
12 diversity jurisdiction. Railway Company v. Whitton's Administrator, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 270, 286, 20
13 L.Ed. 571 (1872); Grand Bahama Petroleum Co. v. Asiatic Petroleum Corp., 550 F.2d 1320, 1324–
14 25 (2d Cir. 1977).”).
15          The proper interpretation of Section 5233(c) is that it identifies the appropriate venue within

16 California’s state court system, not that it excludes federal court jurisdiction.
17   IV.    CALIFORNIA CORPORATIONS CODE SECTION 5233 REQUIRES THE
            ATTORNEY GENERAL’S JOINDER BUT DOES NOT REQUIRE CLAIMS
18          AGAINST HIM.
19          The Attorney General argues that Plaintiffs fail to state a claim against him under Rule
20 12(b)(6). This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the Attorney General’s role in
21 this litigation. California Corporations Code Section 5233(c) requires the Attorney General’s
22 joinder not as a defendant against whom relief is sought, but as an indispensable party whose
23 presence is necessary to adjudicate claims against other defendants.
24          Section 5233(c) clearly states that “the Attorney General or, if the Attorney General is joined
25 as an indispensable party, any of the following may bring an action in the superior court of the
26 proper county for the remedies specified in subdivision (h).” (Emphasis added.) The statute then
27 lists the parties who may bring such an action if the Attorney General is joined, including “[t]he

 1 corporation, or a member asserting the right in the name of the corporation pursuant to Section

 2 5710.” Cal. Corp. Code § 5233(c)(3).
 3          This statutory structure does not require Plaintiffs to assert specific claims “against” the

 4 Attorney General. Rather, it requires his joinder as an indispensable party to ensure that the public
 5 interest in charitable assets is represented. As the California Supreme Court has recognized, “the
 6 Attorney General has broad powers under common law and California statutory law to carry out his
 7 mission to protect and oversee charities and public trusts.” Holt v. College of Osteopathic
 8 Physicians & Surgeons, 61 Cal. 2d 750, 754 (1964).
 9          The absence of specific allegations of wrongdoing against the Attorney General is thus not a

10 deficiency in the FAC, but rather a reflection of the unique role the Attorney General plays in
11 actions involving charitable corporations. The Attorney General’s presence ensures that the
12 remedies sought by Plaintiffs—including the imposition of a constructive trust over Defendants and
13 their ill-gotten gains, and the voiding of contracts contrary to the non-profit’s charitable purposes—
14 are consistent with the public interest.
15    V.    DISMISSAL WOULD IMPROPERLY FRAGMENT THIS LITIGATION.

16          Dismissing the Attorney General would improperly fragment this litigation and potentially

17 prevent Plaintiffs from pursuing their self-dealing claim altogether. If the Court dismisses the
18 Attorney General, Plaintiffs would be forced to either abandon their self-dealing claim under
19 Section 5233, leaving a critical aspect of Defendants’ wrongdoing unaddressed, or file a separate
20 action in state court against the same defendants for the same conduct.
21          Such fragmentation would be particularly inappropriate here, where the self-dealing claim is

22 inextricably intertwined with Plaintiffs’ federal claims. The same factual allegations that support
23 Plaintiffs’ federal antitrust, RICO, and Lanham Act claims, as well as the UCL claim over which
24 there is federal jurisdiction, also support their Section 5233 claim. Requiring Plaintiffs to litigate
25 related claims in different forums would undermine judicial economy and risk inconsistent
26 outcomes.
27          Moreover, the Attorney General has failed to demonstrate that his presence in this litigation

28 would impose any actual burden on the state. As noted above, Plaintiffs do not seek damages from

 6                                              CONCLUSION

 7           For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs respectfully request that the Court deny the Attorney

 8 General’s Motion to Dismiss. The Attorney General’s joinder as an indispensable party under
 9 California Corporations Code Section 5233 falls within the Ex parte Young exception to sovereign
10 immunity, is necessary for Plaintiffs to pursue their derivative action, and is consistent with federal
11 courts’ exercise of supplemental jurisdiction over state law claims. The absence of specific
12 allegations of wrongdoing against the Attorney General reflects the California Legislature’s unique
13 system for bringing claims related to charitable self-dealing, not a failure to state a claim.
14 Maintaining the Attorney General as an indispensable party is necessary to provide complete relief
15 to Plaintiffs and avoid fragmenting this litigation.
