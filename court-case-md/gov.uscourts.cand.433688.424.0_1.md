 1                                            INTRODUCTION
 2          Musk intends to turn this trial into a referendum on the circumstances of Sam Altman’s
 3   removal and reinstatement as director and CEO of OpenAI in November 2023. Those events are a
 4   sideshow. Musk’s claims concern alleged promises made to him nearly a decade earlier, which he
 5   alleges were breached when OpenAI accepted investments from Microsoft long before Altman’s
 6   removal and reinstatement. Musk’s only theory of relevance is his contention that OpenAI’s
 7   nonprofit directors reinstated Altman because of commercial pressure to do so.
 8          Having injected this collateral issue into the case, Musk now seeks to bar the jury from
 9   hearing the full story. As multiple witnesses will testify, in the days following Altman’s removal,
10   OpenAI’s employees revolted and threatened to resign en masse unless the board reversed course.
11   Two of the directors who voted to remove Altman (whom Musk intends to call as witnesses) then
12   voted to reinstate Altman as CEO, resign, and appoint new independent directors in their stead. The
13   new directors formed a special committee and hired a leading law firm, WilmerHale, to conduct an
14   independent review of the circumstances surrounding Altman’s removal. Only after that review
15   concluded did the directors authorize Altman’s return to the board and ratify the prior board’s
16   decision to reinstate him as CEO.
17          Musk now seeks to exclude all testimony and evidence of WilmerHale’s independent
18   review. There is no basis to do so. The existence of the review is not privileged; it’s a fact directly
19   relevant to the issue Musk seeks to litigate: whether the board reinstated Altman, and kept him on
20   as CEO, to placate Microsoft. Testimony and evidence offered to establish the fact of the review is
21   thus admissible. If Musk doesn’t want the jury to hear this evidence, he need not raise the collateral
22   issue of Altman’s removal and reinstatement at trial.
23          WilmerHale’s conclusions are also admissible for the limited purpose of establishing the
24   effect they had on the directors’ state of mind and decision-making. Musk contends that Altman’s
25   reinstatement was the product of improper influence on OpenAI’s directors. OpenAI’s witnesses
26   are entitled to rebut this narrative by referencing the nonprivileged information that factored into
27   their decision-making. Any risk that the jury may improperly rely on those conclusions for a
28   different purpose can be abated by an appropriate limiting instruction.

 1                                              ARGUMENT
 2          Evidence establishing the fact of the independent review is relevant and admissible.
 3   OpenAI’s board authorized an independent review of the circumstances of Altman’s removal
 4   before reinstating him to the board and determining to retain him as CEO. That fact is indisputably
 5   relevant to the case Musk intends to litigate, as he has placed the board’s motives at issue. The
 6   directors Musk attacks cannot explain their decisions at trial without acknowledging their
 7   deliberative process, which ordered an independent review into the circumstances of Altman’s
 8   dismissal. Two of Musk’s own witnesses—directors who voted to remove and then reinstate
 9   Altman as CEO—testified that they insisted on an independent review, and Musk has designated
10   their testimony for use in his case-in-chief. See Ex. A (McCauley Tr.) at 123:4-124:22. Musk’s
11   sweeping order would prohibit these witnesses even from testifying as to what they say they wanted
12   as a condition to reinstating Altman, or whether and how their claimed demands were met.
13          Depriving the jury of these facts would allow Musk to present a misleading and one-sided
14   narrative to the jury, to OpenAI’s prejudice. Musk seeks to conduct a mini-trial on an issue
15   untethered to his claims (which center on events from years earlier); not driven by product safety
16   concerns (as his own witnesses will testify); and offered primarily to impugn the credibility of
17   OpenAI’s CEO and the motives of its board. At the same time, he seeks to bar OpenAI’s witnesses
18   from responding to his accusations by explaining why they did what they did. None of Musk’s
19   arguments support that unjust result.
20          1.      Musk’s hearsay objection (MIL at 4) is meritless. In each case he cites, a party
21   sought to admit the results of an investigation—not its existence—for their truth. See Paddack v.
22   Dave Christensen, Inc., 745 F.2d 1254,1258-59 (9th Cir. 1984) (proponent sought to admit pension
23   fund audit “as evidence of the amount of the [contribution] deficiencies”); United States v. Reyes,
24   239 F.R.D. 591, 600 (N.D. Cal. 2006) (proponent sought to establish “lack of culpability” via
25   investigation that purportedly “exonerate[d]” him); In re Homestore.com, Inc., 2011 WL 291176,
26   at *6-7 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 25, 2011) (plaintiff sought to use investigative report “to prove” defendant’s
27   “past fraudulent practices”). That is not the case here. The fact that an independent review was

 3           2.      Rule 403 does not justify exclusion. Musk does not deny that the challenged
 4   evidence is relevant—because of his litigating position—and therefore admissible under Rule 401.
 5   Musk argues instead (MIL at 2) that evidence of WilmerHale’s review may invite the jury to
 6   substitute WilmerHale’s findings for its own, a risk he says justifies wholesale exclusion of the
 7   evidence. That is not the law. Any risk of unfair prejudice can be abated by an appropriate limiting
 8   instruction. See Aghmane v. Bank of Am., 2018 WL 558886, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 25, 2018)
 9   (admitting statements offered to show knowledge and state of mind); United States v. Gieniec, 993
10   F.2d 885 (9th Cir. 1993) (affirming court’s admission of statements offered for their “effect on the
11   hearer”). Musk’s cases again provide no support for his argument, as each involved the risk of a
12   jury adopting an investigator’s findings on an ultimate issue in the case. See MIL at 2-3. That isn’t
13   a concern here because Musk’s probe of Altman’s reinstatement is (at most) a collateral issue.
14           3.      Musk’s contention (MIL at 3-4) that OpenAI seeks to use the independent review
15   as both “a sword and a shield” is also unfounded. First, the mere fact of the review implicates no
16   claim of privilege. Second, as Musk’s own authority confirms, the doctrine applies only when a
17   party attempts to use privileged information (which the review’s existence is not) as a “sword”—
18   that is, by affirmatively placing the information “at issue.” Bittaker v. Woodford, 331 F.3d 715,
19   718-19 (9th Cir. 2003). Here, it is Musk who wields the figurative sword, because it is Musk who
20   seeks to attack the circumstances of Altman’s removal instead of focusing on the actual claims and
21   defenses before the jury. The law does not require OpenAI to waive privilege or accede to Musk’s
22   one-sided narrative. See Lyons v. NBCUniversal Media, 2021 WL 4777143, at *4 (C.D. Cal. July
23   1, 2021) (“[I]t is Plaintiffs, not Defendant, who put the adequacy of the investigation at issue. . . . It
24   would be extraordinary if a party, by interjecting an issue in a case that the opposing party is not
25   asserting as a defense, could obtain a waiver for any response at all by Defendant[.]”).
26           Finally, Musk has never before contended that the challenged evidence constitutes
27   “selective disclosure” of privileged information, as he does now (MIL at 3-4). To the contrary,
28   Musk accepted OpenAI’s comprehensive response to his interrogatory asking for details on the

 7   motion in limine where party did not advance sword/shield arguments in discovery).
 8          The independent review’s conclusions are relevant and admissible. Musk argues that the
 9   investigative conclusions are also inadmissible hearsay. MIL at 4. That is incorrect. OpenAI is not
10   offering these conclusions for their truth. Rather, testimony and evidence are being offered to
11   explain the effect of the conclusions on the directors’ state of mind and decision-making, an issue
12   that Musk has injected into this case. See Sanders v. Arnold, 2017 WL 3168521, at *14-15
13   (N.D. Cal. July 26, 2017) (concluding state appellate court did not err in admitting evidence
14   containing hearsay where such evidence was offered to show “why the witness did what he did”);
15   Montoya v. Orange Cnty. Sheriff's Dep’t, 987 F. Supp. 2d 981, 993 (C.D. Cal. 2013) (denying
16   motion to exclude prior investigations because they were “not offered to prove the truth of the
17   matter asserted, but rather . . . to demonstrate the effect the investigations had on [defendant]”).
18          Following an independent review, WilmerHale concluded that Altman’s conduct “did not
19   mandate removal” and that the prior board’s actions “did not arise out of concerns regarding product
20   safety or security, the pace of development, OpenAI’s finances, or its statements to investors,
21   customers, or business partners.” Musk Ex. 2 at -733. The OpenAI board considered those
22   conclusions when deciding whether to endorse the previous board’s decision to rehire Altman. Id.
23   Regardless of whether those conclusions were correct, that the reconstituted board considered them
24   directly rebuts Musk’s attacks on the board’s motivations and deliberative process. And here again,
25   there is no “sword/shield” concern for the reasons above—OpenAI has not “selectively disclosed”
26   any privileged advice, has not put any privileged advice at issue, and is not wielding the conclusions
27   as proof that Altman did or did not do any of the things Musk will accuse of him of. Any risk of
28   unfair prejudice does not substantially outweigh the highly probative nature of this evidence.
