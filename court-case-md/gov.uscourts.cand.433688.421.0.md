 1                                          INTRODUCTION

 2          Defendant Microsoft Corp. seeks to preclude Plaintiff ’s expert Dr. C. Paul Wazzan from

 3 testifying as to the amount of wrongful gains Microsoft derived from Musk’s contributions. The

 4 Court should deny that motion. Microsoft’s motion repeats the exact same arguments Defendants

 5 already made in their pending Daubert motion. Dkts. 394-2, 397. Those arguments are no more

 6 persuasive the second time around. Microsoft simply ignores that aiding and abetting is a derivative

 7 claim that renders the aider and abettor liable for the primary violator’s misconduct. The Court

 8 should therefore deny both Microsoft’s latest motion in limine and Defendants’ pending Daubert

 9 motion.

10                                             ARGUMENT

11 I.       MICROSOFT’S MOTION IS DUPLICATIVE

12          Microsoft’s motion in limine rehashes – at times verbatim – the same argument that

13 Defendants already made in their pending Daubert motion. In that prior motion, Defendants argued

14 that “Wazzan’s methodology does not tie the purported enrichment to the alleged wrongdoing”

15 because “[t]he Restatement of Torts makes clear that an aiding and abetting defendant can be

16 accountable ‘only for harm to which the aiding and abetting made a contribution.’ ” Dkt. 394-2 at

17 12. Defendants complained that Dr. Wazzan’s “calculations do not tie any purported enrichment to

18 specific Microsoft acts, much less analyze the timing and incremental impact (if any) of such acts.”

19 Id. Defendants then belabored that point in their Daubert reply, urging that “[a]n aiding-and-

20 abetting defendant is liable ‘only for harm to which the aiding and abetting made a contribution.’ ”

21 Dkt. 397 at 6 (citing same Restatement provision). Microsoft now repeats those exact same

22 arguments in its motion in limine, urging that “an aiding-and-abetting defendant is liable ‘only for

23 harm to which the aiding and abetting made a contribution’ ” and that “Wazzan’s methodology

24 does not and cannot measure what amount of Microsoft’s purportedly wrongful gains are the result

25 of Microsoft’s conduct.” Mot. at 3-4.

26          Microsoft’s motion in limine is thus an improper attempt to rehash arguments that Microsoft

27 already made in a prior motion. That alone justifies denial. See Apodaca v. Eaton Corp., No. 2:20-

28 CV-01064-TL, 2023 WL 2242146, at *2 (W.D. Wash. Feb. 27, 2023) (denying motion in limine

 2 II.       DR. WAZZAN’S OPINIONS ARE ADMISSIBLE

 3           Microsoft’s motion is not only redundant but wrong. Dr. Wazzan’s disgorgement analysis

 4 properly holds Microsoft responsible for the wrongful gains that Microsoft derived from OpenAI’s

 5 breaches of charitable trust. Dr. Wazzan was not required to separate out the portion of wrongful

 6 gains that derived specifically from Microsoft’s assistance to OpenAI’s breaches.

 7           Microsoft simply ignores that aiding and abetting is a secondary liability claim. “Liability

 8 based on an aiding and abetting . . . theory is . . . ‘derivative,’ i.e., liability is imposed on one person

 9 for the direct acts of another.” Richard B. LeVine, Inc. v. Higashi, 131 Cal. App. 4th 566, 579

10 (2005). The aider and abettor “st[ands] in [the] shoes” of the primary violator. Pham v. Nguyen,

11 No. H047433, 2022 WL 1101750, at *4 (Cal. Ct. App. Apr. 13, 2022). For that reason, aiders and

12 abettors are jointly and severally liable for the consequences of the wrongful conduct they assist.

13 See Dan B. Dobbs et al., The Law of Torts § 435 (2d ed.) (“One who knowingly provides substantial

14 aid or encouragement to another’s commission of a tort is . . . jointly and severally liable for it along

15 with the person who actually carries out the tortious acts.”); Pham, 2022 WL 1101750, at *4

16 (“Olivia, as an aider and abettor, was responsible for the conduct of [the primary violator] . . . and

17 as such, was jointly and severally liable.”); Frame v. PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP, 36 Cal. Rptr.

18 3d 209, 223 (Ct. App. 2005) (“As an aider and abettor, PwC would be jointly and severally liable to

19 the victims of the fraud . . . .”).

20           Microsoft’s motion ignores those principles. If the jury finds that Microsoft aided and

21 abetted OpenAI’s breach of charitable trust, Microsoft is derivatively liable for the consequence of

22 OpenAI’s breach. For disgorgement purposes, that means Microsoft must disgorge the wrongful

23 gains that Microsoft derived from OpenAI’s breach, not merely the wrongful gains Microsoft

24 derived from Microsoft’s assistance to OpenAI’s breach.                  Microsoft’s contrary argument

25 completely ignores the secondary and derivative nature of aiding and abetting claims.

26           Microsoft’s citation to the Restatement does not help it in the least. The Restatement

27 confirms that aiding and abetting is “a form of secondary liability” in which “a defendant is held

28 liable for the tort that was aided and abetted” and “is subject to ‘secondary’ liability for what the

 1 primary wrongdoer has done.” Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liab. for Econ. Harm § 28 cmt. a

 2 (2020). The comment Microsoft quotes explains that an aider and abettor “can be held to account

 3 . . . only for harm to which the aiding and abetting made a contribution.” Id. cmt. e (emphasis

 4 added). It does not say that the aider and abettor may be held liable only for harm caused by the

 5 contribution itself. The Restatement is thus fully consistent with the settled secondary liability

 6 principles explained above.

 7          Dr. Wazzan’s calculation of Microsoft’s wrongful gains faithfully applies those principles.

 8 First, Dr. Wazzan calculates the amount Microsoft gained from its investments in OpenAI’s for-

 9 profit entity by measuring the increase in value of Microsoft’s investments minus the cost of the

10 investments. Mot. Ex. A ¶¶ 104-105. Dr. Wazzan then calculates the portion of those gains that are

11 attributable to Musk’s contributions to the nonprofit (and thus attributable to OpenAI’s breach of

12 charitable trust). Id. That calculation yields $13.30 to $25.06 billion – the portion of Microsoft’s

13 gains that are attributable to OpenAI’s misconduct. Mot. Ex. B.

14          Microsoft faults Dr. Wazzan for not analyzing “how the conduct Microsoft is alleged to have

15 engaged in provided substantial assistance to OpenAI’s purported breach of charitable trust.” Mot.

16 at 3. But that is the jury’s job, not Dr. Wazzan’s. Once the jury finds that Microsoft aided and

17 abetted OpenAI’s wrongful conduct, Microsoft is secondarily liable for OpenAI’s wrongdoing.

18 Dr. Wazzan’s only task at that point is to determine how much Microsoft wrongfully gained from

19 the misconduct by OpenAI that it aided and abetted.1

20                                            CONCLUSION

21          The Court should deny Microsoft’s motion.
