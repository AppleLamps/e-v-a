 1                                          INTRODUCTION

 2          After OpenAI’s nonprofit board fired CEO Sam Altman for repeatedly lying to them about

 3 safety process failures and other misconduct related to OpenAI’s charitable mission, Altman and

 4 Microsoft conspired to get Altman reinstated and replace most of the independent board members

 5 who had voted to fire him. OpenAI then commissioned WilmerHale to conduct an investigation

 6 into the circumstances of Altman’s firing in an effort to justify his reinstatement. OpenAI never

 7 released the results of that investigation. Instead, it published a blog post with selective and

 8 one-sided characterizations of WilmerHale’s findings.

 9          OpenAI now seeks to introduce that blog post and other evidence of WilmerHale’s

10 investigation and findings at trial. That evidence would improperly invite the jury to substitute

11 WilmerHale’s purported findings for its own. Having asserted privilege over the details of the

12 investigation, moreover, OpenAI should not now be permitted to show the jury only its own self-

13 serving characterizations. The Court should exclude the evidence from trial.

14                                           BACKGROUND
15          In November 2023, OpenAI’s nonprofit board fired Altman for a pattern of dishonesty that
16 stretched back years. Days later, Microsoft announced that it would hire Altman and Brockman to

17 run Microsoft’s own AI division. Microsoft also offered to hire away OpenAI’s entire workforce

18 and match their existing compensation – an offer worth billions of dollars. In response, OpenAI’s

19 board agreed to reinstate Altman, and three of its four independent directors were forced to resign.

20          As soon as Altman was reinstated as CEO, the reconstituted board formed a Special
21 Committee to investigate the circumstances of his firing. At Altman’s insistence, the Committee

22 comprised two new directors whom Altman had just vetted and approved. See Ex. 1. That

23 Committee then hired WilmerHale to “review events relating to [Altman’s] termination.” Id.

24 WilmerHale’s investigation concluded in March 2024 when the Committee reported its findings to

25 the board. Id.

26          To date, almost no details are public. OpenAI disclosed nothing regarding the specific topics
27 WilmerHale investigated, the specific documents reviewed, or even the relevant time period under

28 investigation. OpenAI never disclosed how WilmerHale reached its findings or any conflicting

     I.     THE COURT SHOULD EXCLUDE WILMERHALE’S INVESTIGATION AND
23          FINDINGS UNDER RULE 403

24          Under Federal Rule of Evidence 403, the Court has broad discretion to exclude evidence that

25 poses a danger of “unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, [or] misleading the jury.” Fed. R. Evid.

26 403. WilmerHale’s investigation and findings pose all three risks, and those dangers substantially

27 outweigh any marginal relevance the evidence may have.

28          Brandishing an investigation by a prominent law firm like WilmerHale inevitably “invites

 1 the jury to improperly substitute the [investigation’s] findings . . . for its own.” United States v.

 2 Pac. Gas & Elec. Co., 178 F. Supp. 3d 927, 947-48 (N.D. Cal. 2016) (excluding conclusions of

 3 NTSB investigation conducted under “different evidentiary and legal standards” and “for a different

 4 purpose”). For that reason, courts routinely exclude such investigations under Rule 403. See, e.g.,

 5 Lewis v. Chicago, 563 F. Supp. 2d 905, 919-20 (N.D. Ill. 2008) (excluding internal investigation

 6 based on “undue risk . . . that the jury might simply adopt the result of the internal investigation”);

 7 Free v. Fed. Express Corp., No. 2:15-cv-2404, 2019 WL 332810, at *2 (W.D. Tenn. Jan. 25, 2019)

 8 (excluding investigation because it “risk[ed] biasing a jury’s perception of the evidence” and “could

 9 lead jurors to second guess their independent assessment of the proof ”); EEOC v. Evans Fruit Co.,

10 No. 10-cv-3033, 2013 WL 3817372, at *4 (E.D. Wash. July 22, 2013) (similar).

11          Those risks are particularly serious here because WilmerHale’s investigation covered critical

12 issues in this case. WilmerHale’s purported findings addressed whether the board’s actions – as

13 opposed to actions by Microsoft, Altman, or others – “destabilize[d]” OpenAI; whether the board’s

14 decision to fire Altman related to AI safety concerns; whether the board’s process for deciding to

15 fire Altman was adequate; and whether Altman’s misconduct warranted removal. Ex. 2. Those

16 issues all relate to key elements of Musk’s claims. Musk will present evidence at trial on those same

17 topics – including testimony of independent board members who voted to remove Altman. The risk

18 that the jurors might uncritically adopt WilmerHale’s findings as their own is acute.

19          Those risks substantially outweigh any scant probative value the evidence may have.

20 OpenAI’s self-serving descriptions of the investigation’s findings provide only a selective, one-

21 sided version of events as part of a curated public relations campaign. Ex. 3. Given that slight

22 relevance and the serious potential for misleading the jury, the Court should exclude the evidence.

23 II.      OPENAI’S SELECTIVE DISCLOSURE WARRANTS EXCLUSION

24          The selective nature of OpenAI’s blog post independently warrants exclusion. Basic fairness

25 “prevent[s] a party from using the privilege as both a shield and a sword.” Bittaker v. Woodford,

26 331 F.3d 715, 719 (9th Cir. 2003). A party cannot introduce advantageous portions of legal advice

27 while shielding the harmful portions.         See Columbia Pictures Television, Inc. v. Krypton

28 Broadcasting of Birmingham, Inc., 259 F.3d 1186, 1196 (9th Cir. 2001) (upholding exclusion where

 3          OpenAI has maintained privilege over the details of the WilmerHale investigation. Ex. 4.

 4 Musk thus has had no opportunity to test the thoroughness or independence of the investigation.

 5 Permitting OpenAI to reference the investigation despite having withheld those details would

 6 improperly “suggest to the jury that the investigation was adequate, notwithstanding that Plaintiffs

 7 have had no opportunity to probe its accuracy.” Evans Fruit, 2013 WL 3817372, at *4.

 8 III.     THE INVESTIGATION AND FINDINGS ARE HEARSAY

 9          OpenAI’s blog post and other documents or testimony purporting to summarize the

10 investigation or its findings are also hearsay. See Paddack v. Dave Christensen, Inc., 745 F.2d 1254,

11 1258-59 & n.7 (9th Cir. 1984) (holding that “special audit” investigation materials and findings

12 were inadmissible hearsay); United States v. Reyes, 239 F.R.D. 591, 600 (N.D. Cal. 2006)

13 (Breyer, J.) (holding internal investigation materials inadmissible); In re Homestore.com, Inc., No.

14 01-cv-11115, 2011 WL 291176, at *6-7 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 25, 2011) (similar).

15          OpenAI’s blog post, for example, is a public relations document that purports to summarize

16 what the Special Committee was told by WilmerHale about what the law firm learned from unknown

17 documents or interviews. OpenAI offers no basis for overcoming even a single layer of that

18 hearsay, much less all of them.

19                                           CONCLUSION

20          The Court should exclude all evidence and argument concerning WilmerHale’s investigation

21 and purported findings.
