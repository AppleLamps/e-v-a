 1                     MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

 2   I.     INTRODUCTION

 3          Pursuant to Federal Rules of Evidence 401, 402, 403, and 608(b), Defendant Microsoft

 4   respectfully moves the Court for an order excluding evidence or argument at trial concerning

 5   the personal, family law litigation history of Microsoft’s damages expert, Jonathan I. Arnold,

 6   Ph.D. Those cases have nothing to do with the parties or facts of this litigation—nor do they

 7   have anything to do with the expert’s qualifications or opinions. Microsoft files the instant

 8   motion to prevent introduction or discussion of this irrelevant and highly prejudicial evidence.

 9   II.    BACKGROUND

10          Microsoft has retained Dr. Arnold, an experienced economist, who has testified over

11   100 times, as a rebuttal expert to Plaintiff’s damages expert Dr. Paul Wazzan, Ph.D. Plaintiff

12   has indicated that he intends to reference certain court orders arising from Dr. Arnold’s

13   unrelated personal lawsuits stemming from his prior romantic relationship with his former

14   partner. While these matters are deeply personal and irrelevant to the claims at issue, a brief

15   summary is necessary.

16          In 2004, Dr. Arnold and his former partner had a marriage ceremony but later learned

17   they were never legally married due to the officiant’s failure to comply with California

18   requirements. See Arnold v. Villarreal, No. 09 C 7399, 2014 WL 2922810, at *2 (N.D. Ill. June

19   27, 2014). In 2007, they each petitioned to dissolve their marriage. Id. In 2008, they “stipulated

20   that they were not married and dismissed their dissolution petitions.” Id. Then came additional

21   litigation: in 2009, Dr. Arnold sued his former partner, alleging she falsely represented the

22   marriage was legal to obtain gifts from him. See id. The court granted her motion for summary

23   judgment and denied Dr. Arnold’s motion to alter the judgment under Federal Rule of Civil

24   Procedure 59(e). Arnold v. Villarreal, 853 F.3d 384, 387 (7th Cir. 2017). Dr. Arnold appealed

25   and his former partner moved for sanctions, claiming the appeal was frivolous. See id. The

26   Seventh Circuit affirmed and ordered sanctions against Dr. Arnold. Id. Dr. Arnold’s former

27   partner then filed two additional suits against him, claiming abuse of process and malicious

28   prosecution. See Villarreal v. Arnold, No. 17 CV 3141, 2017 WL 11576483, at *1 (N.D. Ill.

17   follows.

18   III.    LEGAL STANDARD

19           Evidence to be offered at trial is relevant if “it has any tendency to make a fact more or

20   less probable than it would be without the evidence” and “the fact is of consequence in

21   determining the action.” Fed. R. Evid. 401. “Irrelevant evidence is not admissible.” Fed. R.

22   Evid. 402. And, even if relevant, evidence is inadmissible “if its probative value is substantially

23   outweighed by a danger of . . . unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue

24   delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence.” Fed. R. Evid. 403.

25   IV.     ARGUMENT

26           Dr. Arnold’s personal lawsuits, and the courts’ findings in those cases, are irrelevant

27   because they do not make any fact of consequence in this matter any more or less probable.

28   See Fed. R. Evid. 401. Dr. Arnold is a rebuttal expert to Plaintiff’s damages expert. Thus, he

 1   would testify solely from his experience as an economist. Nothing in his lawsuits involving his

 2   personal relationships is remotely related to his damages testimony. It should be excluded

 3   under Federal Rules of Evidence 401 and 402. See Fed. R. Evid. 402.

 4          Nor does the sanctions order fall within Rule 608(b)’s exception for evidence of

 5   truthfulness. The sanctions order related to Dr. Arnold’s pursuit of a frivolous appeal—not for

 6   making a false statement. Dr. Arnold testified that his decision to pursue an appeal was not

 7   based on dishonesty but on the advice he received from counsel. See Ex. A (Arnold Dep. at

 8   53:1-5) (“Yes, on the advice of counsel, I continued to pursue [the case].”).

 9          But even if these personal lawsuits were admissible under any rule of evidence (they

10   are not), they are independently inadmissible under Federal Rule of Evidence 403 because any

11   probative value “is substantially outweighed by a danger of . . . unfair prejudice, confusing the

12   issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, [and] wasting time.” Fed. R. Evid. 403.

13          Russell v. Walmart Inc., No. CV-19-5495, 2023 WL 2628699 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 12, 2023),

14   is on point. In that case, a party wished to question the opposing party’s expert concerning

15   judicial findings from a personal employment lawsuit to “prove [the expert’s] credibility as an

16   expert witness.” Id. at *10. The court excluded such questioning. Id. at *12. As the court

17   explained, personal lawsuits create a substantial risk of jury confusion and unfair prejudice

18   because jurors could give evidence relating to judicial decisions undue weight “by virtue of

19   their having been made by a judge.” Id. at *11 (quoting United States v. Sine, 493 F.3d 1021,

20   1034 (9th Cir. 2007)).

21          This questioning should also be excluded because attacks on an expert’s credibility

22   based on his decisions in a personal lawsuit present “collateral matters that are likely to confuse

23   the issues.” Id. (excluding references to expert’s personal lawsuit because “[a]llowing any

24   inference as to Mr. Blum’s reliability as an expert, based on the Stout court’s conclusions

25   regarding Mr. Blum’s representations to his employer in the context of a discrimination lawsuit

26   would present ‘collateral matters that are likely to confuse the issues’” (citation omitted)). This

27   principle is especially salient here—probing the unrelated personal lawsuits would only

28   distract and confuse the jury.

 1          Plaintiff’s proposal during the meet-and-confer process to “narrow” this issue by only

 2   mentioning the sanctions ruling, without referring to the nature of the litigation, makes matters

 3   worse. Evidence of sanctions against an expert witness is inadmissible under Federal Rules of

 4   Evidence 608(b) and 403. For example, in Barten v. State Farm Mutual Auto. Ins. Co., No.

 5   CV-12-399, 2015 WL 11111309 (D. Ariz. Apr. 8, 2015), the court granted a motion in limine

 6   prohibiting a party from introducing a sanctions order involving the other side’s expert under

 7   Federal Rule of Evidence 608(b) and excluded any questioning regarding the order as

 8   irrelevant and unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. Id. at *4-5. The court held that “if the

 9   evidence of these orders is introduced, Plaintiff would have to be permitted to explain the

10   particulars, which would cause unnecessary delay and possible confusion of issues.” Id. at *4

11   (citing Fed. R. Evid. 403).

12          The same principle applies here. Telling the jury only about the sanctions is prejudicial

13   as “the jury would be left to speculate about [their] mysterious cause . . . no doubt

14   understanding it must be bad if opposing counsel raised it.” Shenwick v. Twitter, Inc., No. 16-

15   cv-5314, 2021 WL 1232451, at *7 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 31, 2021) (excluding references to expert’s

16   personal tweets because they would cause “juror confusion or the undue consumption of

17   time”). To avoid that prejudice, Dr. Arnold would need to provide the full context—causing all

18   the same distracting and confusing issues discussed above.

19   V.     CONCLUSION

20          For the foregoing reasons, Microsoft respectfully moves this Court to exclude any

21   evidence or argument relating to Dr. Arnold’s personal litigations, and associated judicial

22   findings, under Federal Rules of Evidence 401, 402, 403, and 608(b).
