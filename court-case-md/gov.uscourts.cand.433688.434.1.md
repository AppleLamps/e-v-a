 4 PLAINTIFF’S OBJECTIONS TO DEFENDANTS’ PROPOSED VERDICT FORM

 5          Plaintiff objects to Defendants’ use of two separate verdict forms for statute of limitations

 6 and liability. Although this Court indicated its intent to have the jury return a special verdict on

 7 whether Plaintiff ’s claims are barred by the statute of limitations, Plaintiff maintains that it would

 8 not make sense for the jury to deliberate separately on statute of limitations and liability, because

 9 those issues are so closely intertwined. For example, to determine whether any of Musk’s claims is

10 barred by the statute of limitations, the jury would need to determine whether, even if Musk had

11 earlier knowledge of his claims, the claims were nonetheless timely under the continuous accrual

12 doctrine because Defendants continued to engage in the wrongful conduct after the relevant

13 limitations date for that claim. See Aryeh v. Canon Bus. Sols., Inc., 55 Cal. 4th 1185, 1199 (2013)

14 (“[C]ontinuous accrual applies whenever there is a continuing or recurring obligation: ‘When an

15 obligation or liability arises on a recurring basis, a cause of action accrues each time a wrongful act

16 occurs, triggering a new limitations period.’ Because each new breach of such an obligation

17 provides all the elements of a claim – wrongdoing, harm, and causation – each may be treated as an

18 independently actionable wrong with its own time limit for recovery.’”); Water Audit Cal. v. Merced

19 Irrig. Dist., 111 Cal. App. 5th 1147, 1192 (2025) (continuous accrual doctrine applies where “a

20 continuing duty is breached on a recurring or ongoing basis”). In those circumstances, requiring the

21 jury to deliberate over the statute of limitations before it even considers liability would gravely

22 confuse the jury and deprive Plaintiff of a fair opportunity to obtain a determination of his

23 continuous accrual arguments before the jury resolves the statute of limitations defenses.

24          Plaintiff ’s preference would be simply to submit general verdict forms on liability. But at a

25 minimum, if the Court is inclined to adhere to its approach of requesting specific jury findings on

26 the statute of limitations, the Court should at least permit the jury to deliberate over liability at the

27 same time as it deliberates over the statute of limitations.

 1          Plaintiff objects to the laches question on Defendants’ verdict form on the ground that

 2 Plaintiff seeks only legal remedies and accordingly the laches defense does not apply. See County

 3 of Los Angeles v. City of Alhambra, 27 Cal. 3d 184, 195 (1980) (“The defense of laches, however,

 4 is not available at law, only in equity.”).

 5          Plaintiff objects to the unclean hands question on Defendants’ verdict form to the extent it

 6 requires proof only by a preponderance of the evidence. The standard of proof for unclean hands is

 7 clear and convincing evidence, not preponderance of the evidence. See Pinkette Clothing, Inc. v.

 8 Cosmetic Warriors Ltd., 894 F.3d 1015, 1029 (9th Cir. 2018) (“[O]nly a showing of wrongfulness,

 9 willfulness, bad faith, or gross negligence, proved by clear and convincing evidence, will establish

10 sufficient culpability for invocation of the doctrine of unclean hands.”). In addition, no unclean

11 hands question should be submitted with respect to the breach of charitable trust or aiding and

12 abetting claims because “[e]quitable defenses such as unclean hands may not . . . be used to wholly

13 defeat a claim based on a public policy expressed by the Legislature in a statute.” Salas v. Sierra

14 Chem. Co., 59 Cal. 4th 407, 432 (2014); see Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.8.

11 PLAINTIFF’S OBJECTIONS TO DEFENDANTS’ PROPOSED VERDICT FORM

12          Plaintiff proposes to submit specific questions to the jury that will enable the Court to enter

13 judgment for the appropriate amount of disgorgement based on those findings. Plaintiff submits

14 that this approach will help avoid jury confusion and better ensure that the verdict has a firm

15 grounding in the evidence. See Floyd v. Laws, 929 F.2d 1390, 1395 (9th Cir. 1991) (“As a general

16 rule, the court has complete discretion over whether to have the jury return a special verdict or a

17 general verdict.”); LA Int’l Corp. v. Prestige Brands Holdings, Inc., No. 24-3776, 2026 WL 504763,

18 at *6 (9th Cir. Feb. 24, 2026) (“District courts have ‘broad discretion in deciding whether to send

19 the case to the jury for a special or general verdict.’ ”). In Unicolors, Inc. v. H&M Hennes &

20 Mauritz, L.P., 52 F.4th 1054 (9th Cir. 2022), for example, the jury was directed to calculate the

21 defendant’s wrongful profits from copyright infringement by calculating both (1) the defendant’s

22 profits per infringing product sold by product type; and (2) the number of each infringing product

23 sold, and then calculating total disgorgement based on those figures. Id. at 1087-88. The specificity

24 of that verdict allowed the court to “preserv[e] the jury’s findings to the greatest extent possible”

25 when a portion of the award was ruled untenable after trial. Id. at 1087.
