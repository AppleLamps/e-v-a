 1                                          INTRODUCTION

 2          The OpenAI Defendants (“OpenAI”) seek to exclude evidence that they contend bears on

 3 “collateral matters with little or no relevance to the issues to be tried.” Mot. at 1. Not so. The

 4 evidence OpenAI wants to keep out relates to central, contested issues in the case: whether and how

 5 OpenAI subordinated its nonprofit mission to commercial ends and when that transformation

 6 became publicly known. Nor will Musk use the three exhibits for impermissible hearsay purposes.

 7 Instead, Musk will introduce the 2024 departure announcement of one of OpenAI’s key safety

 8 executives to show when OpenAI’s prioritization of commercial ends over its mission became

 9 publicly known. Mot. Ex. C. And Musk does not intend to introduce the other two exhibits into

10 evidence at all. Mot. Exs. A & B. Instead, those articles will be referenced only for the permissible

11 purpose of providing necessary context for deposition testimony.

12                                             ARGUMENT

13 I.       JAN LEIKE’S TWEETS ARE ADMISSIBLE TO SHOW PUBLIC NOTICE

14          In May 2024, OpenAI safety executive Jan Leike announced his departure from OpenAI and

15 tweeted that he “ha[d] been disagreeing with OpenAI leadership about the company’s core priorities

16 for some time, until we finally reached a breaking point.” Mot. Ex. C at -13710. He went on to

17 write that, “over the past years, safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny

18 products.” Id. at -13711. OpenAI’s argument that those tweets are inadmissible hearsay is

19 misplaced, because Musk does not offer them for their truth.

20          Rather, the timing of Leike’s 2024 departure and accompanying public statements are

21 relevant to proving that Musk brought his claims within the applicable statutes of limitations. See

22 Burgess v. Premier Corp., 727 F.2d 826, 835 (9th Cir. 1984) (affirming admission of newspaper

23 articles to show notice where timeliness of claims was disputed). Musk contends that he first

24 discovered OpenAI’s breaches of charitable trust in 2023, after Microsoft announced its whopping

25 $10 billion investment in OpenAI.        OpenAI will contend that Musk knew about OpenAI’s

26 commercial transformation much sooner, in part based on public disclosures. The timing of Leike’s

27 statements in 2024 provides crucial support to Musk’s position. That an OpenAI insider – a key

28 safety executive – first publicly shared his concerns about OpenAI’s prioritization of profits over

 7 II.      TESTIMONY REFERENCING THE NEWS ARTICLES IS ADMISSIBLE

 8          OpenAI also seeks to exclude two articles from the Wall Street Journal and The Economist

 9 that describe the events surrounding Altman’s firing and reinstatement by OpenAI’s former

10 nonprofit board in November 2023. Mot. Exs. A & B. But Plaintiff no longer seeks to admit those

11 articles at all. Instead, Plaintiff seeks to admit only testimony from two former board members

12 about the events described in the articles. That testimony references those articles only because the

13 articles provided context for questioning at the witnesses’ depositions.

14          A.      Evidence About Altman’s and Brockman’s Firing Is Highly Relevant

15          OpenAI’s contention that evidence related to the firing and reinstatement has “little or no

16 relevance” to the case strains credulity. Mot. at 1. The firing and reinstatement are directly

17 probative of Musk’s allegation that OpenAI transformed into a commercial venture with Microsoft’s

18 help – at the expense of OpenAI’s mission and commitment to safety. Altman repeatedly concealed

19 safety-process failures from the board in order to advance OpenAI’s commercial objectives. Ex. 1

20 (Toner Tr.) at 45:11-65:9, 156:19-157:13. That lack of candor – and the threat to AI safety that it

21 created – led board members to fire Altman and Brockman. Id. at 102:25-106:11. Microsoft

22 immediately sprang into action, offering to hire Altman and Brockman to lead a new AI division at

23 Microsoft and making a public offer to hire all of OpenAI’s employees. Id. at 146:18-147:22. The

24 pressure campaign was successful. Faced with the prospect that OpenAI would disintegrate,

25 OpenAI’s board had no choice but to reinstate Altman and Brockman, and safety-focused

26 independent board members like Helen Toner and Tasha McCauley were forced to resign. Id.

27 at 147:23-149:17.

 4          B.     References to the News Articles Will Not Be Offered for Their Truth

 5          Plaintiff never sought to introduce the Wall Street Journal and The Economist articles for

 6 their truth. And Plaintiff no longer seeks to introduce the articles as exhibits at all. Instead, in a

 7 good-faith effort to narrow the dispute, Plaintiff now seeks to introduce only testimony from Helen

 8 Toner and Tasha McCauley that incidentally references the articles in the course of questioning.

 9          That is a permissible non-hearsay use of the documents. Out-of-court statements may be

10 admitted when they aren’t offered for their truth, but rather “only to give context to other

11 statements.” United States v. Barragan, 871 F.3d 689, 704 (9th Cir. 2017); see also United States

12 v. Valerio, 441 F.3d 837, 844 (9th Cir. 2006) (similar). That is logical, “because it doesn’t make

13 sense to play conversations and take one side out of it.” Barragan, 871 F.3d at 704 (internal

14 quotation marks omitted).

15          At trial, Plaintiff intends to present portions of Toner’s and McCauley’s deposition

16 testimony. When asking Toner and McCauley about the events surrounding Altman’s firing and

17 reinstatement, Plaintiff ’s counsel sometimes referred to or quoted from the Wall Street Journal and

18 The Economist articles to provide context for the questions. Toner and McCauley responded with

19 admissible first-hand testimony discussing their own views and experiences. See Mot. Ex. G at

20 155:5-156:18; Mot. Ex. E at 81:21-82:25. Attorney questions, of course, are not evidence at all.

21 The references to the articles in those questions merely contextualize Toner’s and McCauley’s

22 testimony about the firing and reinstatement.

23          OpenAI has not asserted that mere references to the articles in attorney questions would be

24 unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. That fact distinguishes this case from United States v. Sine, 493

25 F.3d 1021 (9th Cir. 2007), where the Ninth Circuit concluded that a prosecutor’s use of a court order

26 during cross-examination was unfairly prejudicial to a criminal defendant. Id. at 1031. By contrast,

27 omitting the questions as OpenAI suggests would confuse the jury by removing the context

28 necessary to understand Toner’s or McCauley’s responses – effectively presenting only one side of
