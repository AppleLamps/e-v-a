 1                                         LEGAL STANDARD

 2          The Ninth Circuit has “‘carved out an exception to the presumption of access’ to judicial

 3   records” that governs “judicial records . . . ‘attached to a non-dispositive motion.’” In re Midland

 4   Nat’l Life Ins. Co. Annuity Sales Prac. Litig., 686 F.3d 1115, 1119 (9th Cir. 2012) (quoting Foltz

 5   v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co., 331 F.3d 1122, 1135 (9th Cir. 2003)). As relevant here, this

 6   exception applies to “sealed materials attached to a discovery motion unrelated to the merits of a

 7   case.” Ctr. for Auto Safety v. Chrysler Grp., LLC, 809 F.3d 1092, 1097 (9th Cir. 2016).

 8          In these circumstances, “the usual presumption of the public’s right to access is rebutted,”

 9   Phillips ex rel. Est. of Byrd v. Gen. Motors Corp., 307 F.3d 1206, 1213 (9th Cir. 2002), and a party

10   “need only satisfy the less exacting ‘good cause’ standard,” Ctr. for Auto Safety, 809 F.3d at 1097.

11   “Good cause” exists where continued sealing would “protect a party or person from annoyance,

12   embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense.” Kamakana v. City & Cnty. of Honolulu,

13   447 F.3d 1172, 1180 (9th Cir. 2006) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(c)).

14                                             ARGUMENT

15          There is good cause for the Court to maintain the confidentiality of the limited portions of

16   Mr. Sutskever’s deposition transcript that are identified above.

17          First, the OpenAI Defendants have a strong private interest in maintaining the

18   confidentiality of the deposition questions and answers at issue. Those questions and answers

19   explicitly reference or otherwise allude to the implied value of another OpenAI executive’s

20   economic interests in OpenAI’s for-profit affiliates. As courts have recognized, parties have a

21   “legitimate interest in ensuring the privacy of personal [financial] information,” and that interest

22   often “outweighs the public’s interest in access to court filings.” Richter v. Oracle Am., Inc., 2023

23   WL 5663217, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 30, 2023). For this reason, courts in this District have allowed

24   litigants to seal court records that reflect personal financial information—including under the

25   stricter “compelling reasons” standard that is not applicable here. See, e.g., Gomo v. NetApp, Inc.,

26   2019 WL 1170775, at *2-3 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 13, 2019) (“compelling reasons” to seal “details

27   regarding [company’s] compensation structure and personal information about [company]

 3            Second, unsealing the deposition excerpts at issue would harm the relevant OpenAI

 4   executive by revealing that individual’s personal financial information to the general public.

 5   Disclosing the information publicly could also complicate OpenAI’s ability to negotiate future

 6   compensation arrangements with existing or prospective employees in the highly competitive

 7   market for artificial intelligence engineers and executives.

 8            Third, the OpenAI Defendants have proposed the least restrictive alternative that is

 9   available to preserve the confidentiality of the information at issue. The OpenAI Defendants have

10   “narrowly tailored” their request by seeking to maintain under seal only the 18 lines of testimony

11   (from the 56-page excerpt of Mr. Sutskever’s deposition transcript that Plaintiff filed as an exhibit)

12   that reveal private financial information. See Laatz v. Zazzle, Inc., 2025 WL 460061, at *2 (N.D.

13   Cal. Jan. 21, 2025) (granting party’s request to “seal about 10 lines” of a deposition transcript).

14            These 18 lines of testimony are also irrelevant to the issues Musk raised in the discovery

15   motion to which the excerpted deposition transcript was attached. The Court did not reference any

16   of the transcript lines at issue in its decision, and Musk cited only three of them as part of a string

17   cite referencing multiple portions of the transcript. Musk’s counsel in fact withdrew one of the

18   questions the OpenAI Defendants seek to redact after being told that it violated the Protective

19   Order.

20                                              CONCLUSION

21            For these reasons, the OpenAI Defendants respectfully request that the Court issue an order

22   directing Plaintiff to redact at least the following lines from any publicly filed version of Exhibit 3:

23   Sutskever Tr. 351:8-18; Sutskever Tr. 352:15-17; and Sutskever Tr. 359:19-22.
