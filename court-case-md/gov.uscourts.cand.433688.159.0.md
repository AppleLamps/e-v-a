 1   I.     INTRODUCTION

 2          This Court has already rejected Plaintiffs’ Clayton Act § 8 claim. Plaintiffs’ Supplemental

 3   Brief regarding Defendants Hoffman and Templeton misinterprets the Court’s Preliminary

 4   Injunction Order and overlooks the requirements of Clayton Act § 8 and Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §

 5   17200. There is no basis to keep Hoffman or Templeton in this case.1

 6          Section 8: The Court dismissed Count 16 on the basis that Plaintiffs lack standing because

 7   there was no board interlock when the FAC was filed. Plaintiffs seek to cabin in this ruling by

 8   claiming it focuses only on prospective injunctive relief and otherwise seek to disregard it by again

 9   focusing on mootness. But nothing in the FAC plausibly alleges that the claimed interlocks caused
10   any injury-in-fact or antitrust injury—essential elements for a private antitrust claim. Additionally,

11   other vaguely referenced Hoffman interlocks are irrelevant and not claimed to have harmed

12   Plaintiffs or competition in any way.

13          Section 17200: Plaintiffs rehash the same deficient boilerplate standing arguments

14   advanced in their Opposition Brief (compare Supplemental Brief [Dkt. No. 155] at 6 and

15   Opposition [Dkt. No. 127] at 17). The lack of standing, however, also precludes the Section 17200

16   claim against Hoffman and Templeton. Plaintiffs admit the Section 17200 claim arises from a

17   “common factual nucleus” to the Section 8 claim. Supplemental Brief at 1. Section 17200 claims

18   require both Article III and UCL standing. Further, Section 17200’s only monetary remedy is

19   restitutionary disgorgement, but the FAC does not allege that Hoffman or Templeton took anything
20   from Plaintiffs. Therefore, no basis exists for any claim against Hoffman and Templeton under

21   Section 17200.

 1   II.    ARGUMENT

 2          A.      The FAC Does Not State a Clayton Act Section 8 Claim Against Hoffman or
                    Templeton (Count 16)
 3

 4           The Court has already found that the FAC fails to properly allege a Section 8 claim against

 5   Hoffman or Templeton. See March 4 Order Denying Motion for a Preliminary Injunction (“PI

 6   Order”) [Dkt. No. 121] at 8-11. Plaintiffs assert that the Court’s ruling applies only to their request

 7   for prospective injunctive relief. This argument is wrong for three reasons: (1) mootness is a

 8   separate issue from standing; (2) the FAC neither plausibly alleges injury-in-fact nor antitrust

 9   injury resulting from the claimed interlocks; and (3) Hoffman’s involvement with other companies
10   ended prior to the filing of the FAC and so is also insufficient to support standing, is not alleged

11   to have caused any injury, or is not alleged to exceed the competitive sales thresholds that would

12   trigger the statute.2

13           First, the Court’s PI Order held that Plaintiffs could not show that any Section 8 injury

14   existed when the FAC was filed, and therefore, Plaintiffs lacked standing. See id. at 11:8-9 and

15   11:22-23. The Court then granted the motion to dismiss Count 16. See PI Order at 11 n.8.

16   Additionally, the Court noted during the April 4 CMC that its PI Order was not limited to mootness

17   but focused on the threshold issue of standing: “There is no standing, therefore there can be no

18   claim, period. Count 16 is out.” Transcript of April 4 CMC at 6:22-7:2. Plaintiffs’ argument that

19   the PI Order did not “moot” xAI’s request for Section 8 damages, only its forward-looking request
20   for injunctive relief, see Pl. Suppl. Br. [Dkt. No. 155] at 3, is therefore incorrect. In short, the

21   Court has already ruled based on the FAC and the PI motion record that Plaintiffs lack standing

22   to bring any Section 8 claim because any alleged injury ended before the FAC was filed. There

23   is no basis for Plaintiffs’ request for further briefing on xAI’s Section 8 damages request, see Pl.

24   Supp. Br. at 3 n.2, and the Court should reject it.

 1          Second, xAI also lacks antitrust standing to bring the Section 8 claim because, as a private

 2   plaintiff, it must demonstrate “that it is a proper party to pursue [an antitrust] claim.” City of

 3   Oakland v. Oakland Raiders, 20 F.4th 441, 448 (9th Cir. 2021). This means xAI must allege not

 4   only its own injury but a direct injury to competition generally. See Hip Hop Beverage Corp. v.

 5   Monster Energy Co., 733 Fed. App’x 380, 381 (9th Cir. 2018). The injury must not be “derivative

 6   and indirect” or “secondary, consequential, or remote.” Oakland Raiders, 20 F.4th at 458 (citation

 7   omitted). Because a Section 8 violation does not itself cause any anticompetitive effects, it is

 8   unsurprising that when a court has considered the question, it has found that a Section 8 violation

 9   alone does not suffice to establish a concrete injury to a competitor for standing purposes.3 See
10   Bearden v. Ballad Health, 967 F.3d 513, 517 (6th Cir. 2020) (private plaintiff must allege a

11   concrete injury in fact and harm to competition more generally). Tellingly, Plaintiffs do not cite a

12   single case awarding damages to a competitor under Section 8 and Defendants are aware of none.

13          Here, the FAC fails to allege that the mere existence of an interlock caused xAI any specific

14   injury or harmed competition. In their Supplemental Brief, Plaintiffs point only to FAC ¶ 227’s

15   conclusory allegations about unspecified exchanges of “competitively sensitive information”

16   between OpenAI and Microsoft. Pl. Supp. Br. at 5:26-27. The statute’s plain language does not

17   provide a general cause of action against information sharing; it is narrowly tailored to prevent

18   certain board interlocks. And for good reason, since claims regarding unlawful information

19   sharing can be brought under Sherman Act § 1. See, e.g., United States v. Container Corp. of Am.,
20   393 U.S. 333, 335, 337 (1969) (holding information exchange unlawful when it “chill[s] the vigor

21   of price competition”). The FAC explains neither how the alleged unspecified exchanges

22   negatively affected xAI nor price competition in general. See In re Baby Food Antitrust Litig., 166

23   F.3d 112, 125 (3d Cir. 1999) (only specific exchanges that cause a price impact are actionable—

24   under Sherman Act § 1, not Clayton Act § 8); see also Microsoft Defendants’ MTD [Dkt. No.

13         B.      The FAC Does Not State a Section 17200 Claim Against Hoffman or
                   Templeton (Count 17)
14

15          Section 17200 prohibits unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business practices. Plaintiffs’

16   argument that the alleged interlocks were unlawful or unfair fails for two reasons: (1) Section

17   17200 by its own terms requires injury-in-fact, and like any other claim requires Article III

18   standing; and (2) monetary damages are not recoverable under the UCL and Plaintiffs have not

19   (and cannot) plead a claim for restitution.
20          First, contrary to Plaintiffs’ suggestion, Section 17200’s standing requirements do not

21   fundamentally differ. A plaintiff invoking federal jurisdiction—whether for a federal or state cause

22   of action—must establish Article III standing, including injury in fact fairly traceable to the

23   defendant’s conduct. See, e.g., Sud v. Costco Wholesale Corp., 229 F. Supp. 3d 1075, 1082 (N.D.

24   Cal. 2017) (dismissing UCL claim for lack of Article III standing), aff'd, 731 F. App’x 719 (9th

25   Cir. 2018); see also Baton v. Ledger SAS, 740 F. Supp. 3d 847, 872 (N.D. Cal. 2024) (evaluating

 1   Article III standing for Section 17200 unfair and unlawful prong claims). Section 17200’s injury

 2   requirement is similar. See Sud, 229 F. Supp. 3d at 1081; Cal. Bus. and Prof. Code § 17204 (UCL

 3   claim requires “injury in fact and [loss of] money or property.”). For the same reasons that xAI

 4   lacks standing to assert a Section 8 claim, xAI lacks Article III or UCL standing to assert a Section

 5   17200 claim against Hoffman and Templeton.

 6          Second, xAI cannot recover damages under Section 17200 because the UCL is equitable

 7   in nature. The only monetary remedy available to a private, non-governmental plaintiff under

 8   Section 17200 is restitutionary disgorgement. See Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.,

 9   29 Cal. 4th 1134, 1144 (2003). The FAC does not (and cannot) allege that Hoffman or Templeton
10   took anything from the Plaintiffs because of the alleged board interlocks, so there is nothing to

11   restore. Accordingly, the UCL does not provide Plaintiffs a monetary remedy. See Korea Supply,

12   29 Cal. 4th at 1140.5

13   III.   CONCLUSION

14          There is no reason for the Court to revisit its prior dismissal of Count 16, and the Court

15   should also dismiss Count 17 as to Defendants Hoffman and Templeton with prejudice. Plaintiffs’

16   suggestion to defer ruling on this issue is a waste of time. The issues are ripe now, there is no basis

17   for the claims, and there is no reason to burden Hoffman and Templeton with party status only to

18   dismiss them down the road.
