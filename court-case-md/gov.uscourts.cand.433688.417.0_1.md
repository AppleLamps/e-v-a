 1                                           INTRODUCTION

 2          The OpenAI Defendants seek to exclude the testimony of Professor Stuart Russell,

 3 Plaintiff ’s expert on AI safety, arguing that he does not address the specific facts of this case and

 4 has “no opinion on . . . anything OpenAI has done.” Mot. at 3. But generalized expert testimony is

 5 permissible, as is clear from the Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 702 and this Court’s prior

 6 decisions. OpenAI’s other bases for exclusion fail, too. The Court should deny the motion.

 7                                            BACKGROUND

 8          Prof. Russell, a professor of computer science at the University of California, Berkeley, has

 9 studied AI for nearly five decades. Mot. Ex. A (“Russell Rpt.”) ¶ 1. He authored the leading

10 textbook on AI, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach. Id. ¶ 2. For over ten years, Prof.

11 Russell’s research has focused on “ensur[ing] that AI systems are beneficial to humans rather than

12 harmful.” Id. ¶ 3. In 2019, Prof. Russell published Human Compatible: AI and the Problem of

13 Control, which describes an approach for developing AI safely. Id. Prof. Russell has testified before

14 Congress about AI safety, and he has advised the UN Secretary-General and international leaders

15 on AI policy and safety. Id. ¶ 4.

16          Drawing on his expertise, Prof. Russell will offer four opinions at trial: (1) AI is a powerful

17 and transformative technology, Russell Rpt. ¶¶ 8-58; (2) AI presents substantial risks, id. ¶¶ 59-104;

18 (3) AI companies have strong incentives to pursue artificial general intelligence notwithstanding

19 those risks, id. ¶¶ 44-58; and (4) those risks could be potentially catastrophic if not sufficiently

20 addressed, see, e.g., id. ¶¶ 105-111.

21                                             ARGUMENT

22 I.       OPINIONS 1-4 ARE PERMISSIBLE AND NOT UNFAIRLY PREJUDICIAL

23          “Rule 702 permits general principles testimony without substantive connection to the facts

24 of a case.” Emblaze Ltd. v. Apple Inc., 52 F. Supp. 3d 949, 961 (N.D. Cal. 2014). “[I]n complex

25 cases, having an expert provide general information can be helpful for the jury,” In re HIV Antitrust

26 Litig., No. 19-cv-2573, 2023 WL 3090619, at *14 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 13, 2023), and courts “regularly

27 admit” that “kind of general background information,” Sci. Applications & Res. Assocs., Inc. v.

28 Zipline Int’l, Inc., No. 22-cv-4480, 2025 WL 4108875, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 11, 2025). Prof.

 1 Russell’s opinions conform to that “venerable practice.” Fed. R. Evid. 702 adv. comm. note (2000);

 2 see Brown v. Google, LLC, No. 20-cv-3664, 2022 WL 17961497, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 12, 2022)

 3 (Gonzalez Rogers, J.) (declining to exclude testimony). Such generalized opinion testimony must

 4 “address a subject matter on which the factfinder can be assisted by an expert,” “be reliable,” and

 5 “ ‘fit’ the facts of the case.” Fed. R. Evid. 702 adv. comm. note (2000).

 6          Prof. Russell’s testimony meets Rule 702’s requirements, as it will reliably assist the jury in

 7 understanding the goals of AI systems, types of AI, and the associated safety risks. OpenAI claims

 8 Prof. Russell’s opinions do not “fit” the case because determining whether OpenAI breached its

 9 charitable commitments does not turn on issues of AI safety and because Plaintiff did not “pursue

10 discovery into OpenAI’s safety practices.” Mot. at 3. But AI safety is central to this case.

11          Musk’s commitment to AI safety was what motivated him to found OpenAI. Musk testified

12 that safety was the very reason he was driven to found OpenAI as an “open source nonprofit” – to

13 “counterbalance . . . Google,” which “did not seem to be taking AI safety seriously.” Ex. 1 (Musk

14 Tr.) at 22:5-23:3. “[T]he overall idea of [the] mission to create a general AI maximizing safety was

15 [Musk’s] idea.” Id. at 26:11-13. Prof. Russell’s opinions will help the jury understand why Musk

16 considered it so important that AI be developed safely through a nonprofit dedicated to the public

17 good rather than through a for-profit corporation.

18          The OpenAI Defendants purported to agree that “safety should be a first-class requirement

19 of OpenAI Inc.’s research and development of AI.” Ex. 1 (Musk Tr.) at 260:6-11; Ex. 2 (OpenAI

20 Charter). But OpenAI then breached those commitments by “putting profit before safety.” Ex. 1

21 (Musk Tr.) at 261:13-25; see also id. at 263:11-265:1. During discovery, numerous witnesses

22 testified about how OpenAI pursued profits at the expense of safety. See, e.g., Ex. 3 (Toner Tr.) at

23 38:16-39:1, 40:16-41:13; Ex. 4 (McCauley Tr.) at 24:20-25:18, 131:16-132:20. Prof. Russell’s

24 opinions will provide crucial context for why OpenAI’s subordination of its mission to for-profit

25 goals was such a sharp departure from Musk’s founding intentions.

26          Prof. Russell’s opinions thus provide important “background and context information” about

27 AI systems, describe AI’s safety risks, and explain why AI presents those risks. Brown, 2022 WL

28 17961497, at *10. Prof. Russell’s opinions will help the jury understand the incentive structure that

10 II.      OPINION 3 SATISIFIES RULE 702

11          Contrary to OpenAI’s assertions, Prof. Russell’s third opinion – that AI companies and

12 governments have strong incentives to pursue artificial general intelligence (“AGI”)

13 notwithstanding the safety risks – satisfies Rule 702. Prof. Russell’s “knowledge and experience

14 obtained from work” in the artificial intelligence field are sufficient to support that opinion.

15 Proofpoint, Inc. v. Vade Secure, Inc., No. 19-cv-4238, 2021 WL 2588974, at *6 (citing Fortune

16 Dynamic, Inc. v. Victoria’s Secret Stores Brand Mgmt., Inc., 618 F. 3d 1025, 1034 (9th Cir. 2010)).

17          Far from relying only on his own “say-so” and an “anonymous” article from The Economist

18 (Mot. at 4), Prof. Russell offers sufficient support and methodology, satisfying Rule 702. He will

19 describe the potential economic value of AGI, Russell Rpt. ¶ 44, and explain how that value

20 incentivizes AI companies “to push forward as fast as possible,” including because “the self-

21 reinforcing nature of progress in AI capabilities” imparts a “very substantial” first-mover advantage,

22 id. ¶ 45; see also id. ¶¶ 57-58. As evidence of those incentives, Prof. Russell points to the

23 astronomical investments AI companies have made pursuing AGI. Id. ¶ 46; see also Russell Tr.

24 (Mot. Ex. C) at 187:16-22 (noting Meta’s $100-million signing bonuses for AI researchers). Prof.

25 Russell also relies on countless conversations with others in the AI industry, including AI company

26 CEOs. Russell Tr. 185:24-186:15.

 1 III.     OPINION 4 IS NOT IMPERISSIBLY SPECULATIVE

 2          Finally, OpenAI seeks to exclude Prof. Russell’s fourth opinion concerning the risks related

 3 to AI as “speculative and unreliable” because Prof. Russell purportedly cannot calculate the exact

 4 probability that such risks will occur. Mot. at 4. That is not the point of his testimony. Prof. Russell

 5 is being called to testify simply that the risks exist, not to quantify the likelihood they will

 6 materialize. “Lack of certainty is not, for a qualified expert, the same thing as guesswork.”

 7 Primiano v. Cook, 598 F.3d 558, 565 (9th Cir. 2010).

 8          An expert opinion is “reliable if the knowledge underlying it has a reliable basis in the

 9 knowledge and experience of the relevant discipline.” Primiano, 598 F.3d at 565. Prof. Russell has

10 a reliable basis for his opinions. That conclusion is underscored by the fact that OpenAI’s own AI

11 Safety expert, Prof. Somesh Jha, agrees with Prof. Russell. Prof. Jha conceded that “some of the

12 risks that Professor Russell discusses . . . are possible.” Ex. 5 (Jha Rpt.) ¶ 58 (emphasis added). He

13 agrees that possible risks include disinformation and cybersecurity, Ex. 6 (Jha Tr.) at 196:7-9, 199:6-

14 23; racial, gender, and other forms of discrimination, id. at 198:12-22; dual-use risks, id. at 103:7-

15 15; misalignment risk, id. at 113:2-14; and weapons of mass destruction risks, id. at 105:4-18. Prof.

16 Jha even agrees with Prof. Russell that AI presents a risk of human extinction and that mitigating

17 that risk should be a global priority. Id. at 95:1-16. OpenAI cannot seek to exclude Prof. Russell’s

18 opinions when its own expert has agreed with them. See Garcia v. Columbia Med. Ctr. of Sherman,

19 996 F. Supp. 617, 623 (E.D. Tex. 1998) (denying Daubert motion where moving party’s “own

20 expert . . . used the same methodology”); Groobert v. Pres. & Dirs. of Georgetown Coll., 219 F.

21 Supp. 2d 1, 9 (D.D.C. 2002). Prof. Russell should be permitted to offer Opinion 4 at trial.

22                                             CONCLUSION

23          The Court should deny OpenAI’s motion.
