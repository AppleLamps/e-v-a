 2          1.      Whether OpenAI’s counterclaims for unfair and fraudulent business acts or

 3   practices in violation of California’s Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17200 et

 5   (Second Counterclaim), both of which are predicated on Plaintiffs’ sham bid and its attendant media

 6   hype, fall outside the scope of the Noerr-Pennington doctrine because the predicate conduct is

 7   neither petitioning activity nor incidental to any petitioning activity.

 9   privilege because the communications at issue were neither made in judicial or quasi-judicial

10   proceedings nor constituted a necessary or useful step in any litigation or regulatory process.

11          3.      Whether OpenAI’s First Counterclaim adequately alleges (i) unfair business acts or

12   practices based on the threatened anticompetitive effects of Plaintiffs’ sham bid and its attendant

13   media hype and (ii) fraudulent business acts or practices based on the likelihood of Plaintiffs’ sham

14   bid and its attendant media hype deceiving reasonable consumers into falsely believing that

15   Plaintiffs intended to acquire OpenAI Inc.’s assets.

16          4.      Whether OpenAI’s Second Counterclaim adequately alleges (i) an independently

17   wrongful act based on Plaintiffs’ unfair and fraudulent business acts or practices in violation of

18   California’s Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17200 et seq., (ii) economic

19   relationships with investors, employees, and customers that had a probability of future economic

20   benefit to OpenAI, and (iii) actual disruption and economic harm resulting from Plaintiffs’ sham

21   bid and its attendant media hype making OpenAI’s performance of those economic relationships

22   more costly or burdensome.

23          5.      Whether OpenAI’s counterclaims share a common nucleus of operative facts with,

24   and therefore should be tried alongside, Plaintiffs’ claims designated for Phase One of this

25   litigation, such that a stay of OpenAI’s counterclaims is not warranted.

 2           The “seldom-used” Noerr-Pennington doctrine “protects ‘those who petition any

 3    department of the government for redress . . . from statutory liability for their petitioning conduct.’”

 4    In re Apple Inc. Sec. Litig., 2020 WL 2857397, at *18 (N.D. Cal. June 2, 2020) (quoting Sosa v.

 5    DIRECTV, Inc., 437 F.3d 923, 929 (9th Cir. 2006)) (emphasis added). The doctrine protects only

 6    “(1) petitioning activity itself—for instance, ‘[a] complaint, an answer, a counterclaim’—and

 7    (2) ‘conduct incidental to a petition.’” Cascades Comp. Innov. LLC v. RPX Corp., 2013 WL

 8    6247594, at *16 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 3, 2013) (quoting Freeman v. Lasky, Haas & Cohler, 410 F.3d

 9    1180, 1183-84 (9th Cir. 2005)). And “conduct incidental to a petition” is captured only where

10    “‘sufficiently related to petitioning activity,’” id. (quoting Sosa, 437 F.3d at 935), and where

11    protecting the challenged activity is necessary “‘to preserve the breathing space required for the

12    exercise of the rights [the Petition Clause] protects,’” Arista Networks, Inc. v. Cisco Sys. Inc., 2018

 1           Like those of Plaintiffs’ claims designated for Phase One of this litigation, OpenAI’s

 2    counterclaims focus on the course of conduct between OpenAI, its founders, and Musk from

 3    OpenAI’s founding until the present (see, e.g., ¶¶ 1-9; cf. FAC ¶¶ 1-9)—including early

 4    understandings of OpenAI’s charitable purpose (¶¶ 19-27; cf. FAC ¶¶ 82-90), its need for funding

 5    from outside investors (¶¶ 28-30; cf. FAC ¶¶ 91-97), and its creation of a capped-profit subsidiary

 6    to facilitate that funding (¶¶ 45-48; cf. FAC ¶¶ 102-16). They also concern Musk’s visions of

 7    OpenAI’s future, his attempts to control the company, and his abandonment, and subsequent

 8    targeting, of it when those attempts failed (¶¶ 31-39, 55-64, 70-72, 112; cf. FAC ¶¶ 102-104)—

 9    factual allegations that squarely challenge the basis of Musk’s Phase One claims. OpenAI’s factual

10    allegations pertaining to Plaintiffs’ sham bid—the culmination of Plaintiffs’ years-long campaign

11    of harassment—also undermine the premise of Musk’s Phase One claims, see ¶¶ 83-91,

12    demonstrating that Musk objects not to OpenAI’s contemplated restructuring or to its funding

13    efforts as inconsistent with its charitable purpose (see ¶ 95 (alleging that the sham bid “did not so

14    much as acknowledge OpenAI’s mission”); cf. FAC ¶¶ 1-9, 181-99), but to its leadership—and

15    more specifically, the fact that he is not in charge. There is thus a close relationship between the

16    facts to be tried on Musk’s Phase One claims and the facts to be tried on OpenAI’s counterclaims.

17    Staying these counterclaims “until Phase Two of this case” as Plaintiffs suggest, MTD 23, would

18    require the Court to adjudicate claims resting on an identical factual predicate twice, contrary to

19    the Court’s guidance and to judicial efficiency.

20           And while OpenAI’s counterclaim under the UCL’s “unfair” prong is predicated on

21    anticompetitive effects stemming from Plaintiffs’ conduct underlying the Phase One claims, it

22    shares no “common nucleus of operative facts” with the antitrust claims slated for Phase Two of

23    the trial—which are based on agreements and arrangements between OpenAI and Microsoft, rather

24    than a campaign of harassment and misinformation designed to tilt the competitive playing field

25    away from OpenAI and toward xAI.

26           Viewed through the proper lens, no stay is warranted. OpenAI’s counterclaims should

27    proceed now.
