 1          The OpenAI Defendants file this memorandum of law to request that the Court issue a

 2   curative instruction correcting a misstatement of law made by Plaintiff at the invitation of his

 3   counsel during the second question of his direct examination.

 4          In response to a question to “describe for the jury what this lawsuit is about,” Tr. 316:19-

 5   20, Plaintiff told the jury: “If the Defendants are found not guilty, if Microsoft, Altman, and

 6   Brockman are found not guilty, this case will become case law, it will become precedent, and it

 7   will give license, in my opinion, to losing every charity in America. So the consequences of

 8   this case go far beyond me or the particular people here, but if it–it [is] ok–if the verdict comes

 9   up that effectively makes it okay to loot a charity, the entire foundation of charitable giving

10   in America will be destroyed. That’s my concern.” Tr. 316:26–317:9.

11          Those statements, which Plaintiff’s counsel must have known would be elicited by the

12   second question of the examination, are legally incorrect and unfairly prejudicial. Counsel could

13   and should have cured the prejudicial error by explaining the law to his client—that a jury verdict

14   in this case by this jury sets no precedent for any other case. As counsel is aware, a jury verdict

15   does not create binding legal precedent. Under stare decisis, binding precedent flows only from

16   appellate decisions on questions of law. Hart v. Massanari, 266 F.3d 1155, 1174 (9th Cir. 2001).

17   A jury verdict determines the facts and resolves a dispute between the parties. It does not create

18   any rule of law that binds any future case.

19          The prejudice from this statement could be substantial. Plaintiff’s statement invites the

20   jury to decide the case based on perceived societal consequences rather than the trial record and

21   the Court’s instructions—or at least to wonder if that is really the law. That would be improper.

22   The Ninth Circuit has made clear that counsel may not urge jurors to consider “the potential

23   social ramifications of the jury’s reaching a verdict” or make arguments “designed to appeal to

24   the passions, fears and vulnerabilities of the jury.” United States v. Sanchez, 659 F.3d 1252,

25   1256-57 (9th Cir. 2011); United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F.3d 1142, 1149 (9th Cir. 2005).

26   Jurors must decide the case based solely on the evidence submitted at trial and the Court’s

27   instructions on the law. Any argument that their verdict will create broader societal harm—i.e.,

28   destroy the foundation of charitable giving in America—is clearly inappropriate and prejudicial.

 1   To prevent any prejudice, the Ninth Circuit has endorsed the use of a curative instruction.

 2   Navellier v. Sletten, 262 F.3d 923, 943 (9th Cir. 2001).

 3           Absent a curative instruction, Plaintiff’s testimony risks leaving the jury with the

 4   misunderstanding that exercising its role as factfinder will establish a binding legal rule affecting

 5   non-parties or future cases. The Court gave a proper instruction to the jurors in the moment that:

 6   “He is not a lawyer. It’s not allowed for any legal value whatsoever. And if that’s his opinion,

 7   that’s his opinion, and you can give it whatever weight you want. Overruled.” Tr. 317:14-16.

 8   While the Court’s instruction was appropriate, the OpenAI Defendants believe that since the

 9   testimony was legally incorrect and legally inappropriate—but the jurors may not know that—the

10   Court can and should provide a very brief curative instruction.

11           Accordingly, the OpenAI Defendants respectfully request the Court provide the following

12   curative instruction to the jury:

13           As jurors, you must decide this case based solely on the evidence admitted at trial
             and the Court’s instructions on the law. Your verdict does not create legal precedent,
14           establish legal rules, or bind the outcome of any other case, including any case
             involving charitable giving.
