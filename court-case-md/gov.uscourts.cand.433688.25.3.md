 1                                             INTRODUCTION
 2             For the reasons set forth in Defendants’ demurrer, Plaintiff Elon Musk has not pleaded a

 3   cognizable cause of action. His suit rests on a make-believe “Founding Agreement” as to which he

 4   pleads no offer, no acceptance, no bargained-for consideration, and no mutual assent. The words

 5   “Founding Agreement” appear in no document anywhere. They are not alleged to have been uttered

 6   in any conversation ever. No contract is pleaded. And Musk’s various alternative claims asserted

 7   on the back of the non-existent “Founding Agreement” likewise fail.

 8             In addition, the complaint suffers from further infirmities properly addressed in this motion

 9   to strike:

10             Musk’s prayer for relief asks the Court to enter an exorbitant and impractical order of

11   specific performance. Because the imaginary “Founding Agreement” is not even alleged to contain

12   precise and definite terms, the prayer for mandatory injunctive relief is impermissible as a matter

13   of statute, case law, and common sense. It should be stricken.

14             Musk’s prayer for relief also asks the Court to declare that a technology called “Q*”

15   constitutes “Artificial General Intelligence” and must therefore be excluded from OpenAI’s

16   licensing agreement with Microsoft. The complaint concedes that Q* is presently “in development”

17   and even admits that “it is not clear what Q* is.” (¶ 92.) 1 Hypothetical facts about potential

18   technologies cannot present an actual controversy fit for declaratory relief. The prayer for

19   declaratory relief should be stricken.

20             Finally, Musk’s assertion that he is entitled to punitive damages falls short of meeting the

21   fundamental requirements of California law. The complaint pleads no facts suggesting the malice,

22   oppression, or fraud required to support a request for punitive damages. The prayer for punitive

23   damages should be stricken.

 2   requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint[.]” (Cal. Code Civ. Proc.
 3   § 431.10(b)(3).) “The appropriate procedural device for challenging a portion of a cause of action
 4   seeking an improper remedy is a motion to strike.” (Brown v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2018) 28
 5   Cal.App.5th 824, 844 (citation omitted).)
 6          Courts routinely strike prayers for relief not supported by the allegations in a complaint.
 7   (See, e.g., Turman v. Turning Point of Central Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63-64
 8   [punitive damages claim properly stricken where plaintiff failed to allege malice, oppression, or
 9   fraud, as required by statute]; Pac. Gas & Electric Co. v. Super. Ct. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 19,
10   22-24, 27 [trial court abused its discretion in denying motion to strike prayer for recovery of
11   nonparty’s deductible where proposed remedy was unsupported in the pleadings]; Phoenix
12   Enterprises, Inc. v. GG H16 Apartments, LLC (Super. Ct. Sac. June 2, 2021, No. 34-2020-
13   00286487-CU-BC-GDS) 2021 Cal. Super. LEXIS 80665, at *9 [Dkt. #50] [striking remedy of
14   specific performance absent enforceable contract].)
15          Musk’s allegations do not support his prayer for specific performance, for judicial
16   declarations about OpenAI’s technologies, or for punitive damages. The Court should strike these
17   prayers from the complaint.
18    I.    THE COURT SHOULD                 STRIKE         MUSK’S     PRAYER      FOR     SPECIFIC
            PERFORMANCE.

20          The Court should strike Musk’s prayer for specific performance and corresponding request
21   for an injunction for two reasons:
22          First, as described in Defendants’ demurrer, there is no contract, and so there are no
23   contractual obligations to specifically enforce. (Demurrer at pp. 9-14.) The complaint seeks to
24   enforce an indefinite purported agreement with unverifiable terms and no showing of mutual assent
25   or bargained-for consideration. Musk does not identify any document or conversation in which any
26   Defendant agreed to any contractual undertaking, and Musk’s made-for-litigation “Founding
27   Agreement” is not identified anywhere in the complaint. There are also no allegations that Musk
28   was induced to contribute money to OpenAI in exchange for a promise as to how his contributions

 1   would be deployed; or any allegations that OpenAI acted in accordance with the purported
 2   “Founding Agreement” in consideration of Musk’s contributions. Thus, because the allegations do
 3   not support the prayer for relief, the Court should strike Musk’s request for specific performance.
 4   (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 431.10(b)(3); id. § 436.)
 5           Second, even if there were a contract, the allegations would still not support the prayer,
 6   because the obligations pleaded are too uncertain to be enforced through equity. The Civil Code
 7   prohibits specific performance where contractual terms “are not sufficiently certain to make the
 8   precise act which is to be done clearly ascertainable.” (Cal. Civ. Code § 3390(e).) Accordingly,
 9   “[s]pecific performance cannot be granted unless the terms of the contract are sufficiently definite
10   for the court to know what to enforce.” (13 Witkin, Summary of Cal. Law 11th Equity § 44 (2023)
11   [collecting cases].) Applying this settled rule, courts have repeatedly refused to order specific
12   performance where the words of the relevant contractual provision were uncertain, where no
13   contractual term required the performance sought, or where the parties reserved agreement on
14   important terms. (Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 127 [plaintiff “failed to
15   identify any provision of the Terms of Service that she seeks to have a court enforce”]; Bonk v.
16   Boyajian (1954) 128 Cal.App.2d 153, 156-57 [specific performance unavailable where the
17   “instrument on its face [was] incomplete, uncertain and indefinite” and the parties had reserved
18   agreement on key terms].)
19           Musk seeks two orders of specific performance. Neither remotely satisfies the test for clear
20   ascertainability.

23   standing practice of making AI research and technology developed at OpenAI available to the
24   public.” (Compl. at 34:5-7; Prayer A.i (the “Mandatory Open Source Injunction”).) According to
25   the complaint, this injunction is justified by the following contractual obligations:
26       •   OpenAI should “be open-source, balancing only countervailing safety considerations,”
27           ¶ 24 (emphasis added);

 8   would be made available only “when applicable.” And the complaint supplies no basis to determine
 9   whether public disclosure is “applicable” or “inapplicable.” The complaint also acknowledges that
10   OpenAI’s leaders were to engage in “an ongoing conversation about what work should be open-
11   sourced and what shouldn’t.” (Ibid.) The proposed injunction would impermissibly overrun that
12   part of the supposed bargain. (See Bonk, 128 Cal.App.2d at pp. 156-57 [no specific performance

18   injunction, subject to pain of contempt for guessing wrong about its meaning. To avoid such
19   inadministrable and unfair outcomes is precisely why specific performance is prohibited unless the
20   mandatory terms of a contract are certain enough “to make the precise act which is to be done
21   clearly ascertainable.” (See Cal. Civ. Code § 3390(e); Lawrence v. Shutt (1969) 269 Cal.App.2d
22   749, 763-64 [“uncertainty of the release clause preclude[d] specific performance of the provision”];
23   Lewis, 244 Cal.App.4th at p. 127 [specific performance unavailable where plaintiff failed to
24   identify contractual term for the court to enforce].) The terms alleged here are not ascertainable at
25   all. The prayer for a Mandatory Open Source Injunction should be stricken.

 3    II.    THE COURT SHOULD STRIKE THE REQUEST FOR A JUDICIAL
             DETERMINATION AS TO OPENAI’S “NEXT GENERATION LARGE
 4           LANGUAGE MODELS IN DEVELOPMENT.”
 5           Musk’s prayer for a “judicial determination” that “Q* and/or other OpenAI next generation
 6    large language models in development constitute(s) Artificial General Intelligence and is/are
 7    outside the scope of OpenAI’s license to Microsoft” should be stricken from the complaint as an
 8    improper request for declaratory relief. (Compl. at 34:13-15, Prayer C (emphasis added); id. at
 9    34:16, Prayer D.)
10           California law limits declaratory relief to “cases of actual controversy[.]” (Cal. Code Civ.
11    Proc. § 1060.) An “actual controversy” is “one which admits of definitive and conclusive relief by
12    judgment within the field of judicial administration, as distinguished from an advisory opinion upon
13    a particular or hypothetical state of facts.” (Selby Realty Co. v. City of San Buenaventura (1973)
14    10 Cal.3d 110, 117.) For that reason, there is no “actual controversy” where a plaintiff seeks a
15    “judicial determination” of facts that do not yet exist. (See, e.g., id. at pp. 117-18 [no “actual
16    controversy” where county had merely issued a “tentative” plan of proposed streets that “if
17    ultimately constructed, will cross plaintiff’s property”].)
18           Musk’s prayer admits that it seeks a declaration based on a “hypothetical state of facts.” Id.
19    at 117. Musk alleges that OpenAI “is developing” Q* and that “it is not clear what Q* is[.]” (¶ 92.)
20    So Musk does not even know what Q* is. But Musk nevertheless wants the Court to decide that Q*
21    and other OpenAI technology likewise “in development” is AGI and outside the scope of OpenAI’s
22    license to Microsoft. (Compl. at 34:13-15, Prayer C (emphasis added).) That is not a proper basis
23    for declaratory relief. (See Selby Realty, 10 Cal.3d at pp. 117-18.) This Court cannot determine the
24    nature of technologies that are still “in development” or are not yet known to exist. Musk’s Prayer
25    C and the corresponding request for an injunction should be stricken from the complaint. (Cal.
26    Code Civ. Proc. § 431.10(b)(3); id. § 436.)

 1 III.      THE COURT SHOULD STRIKE THE PRAYER FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES.
 2           The Court should also strike Musk’s prayer for punitive damages because the complaint

 3    does not allege that Defendants are chargeable with oppression, fraud, or malice. (Compl. at 34:24-

 4    25, Prayer G.)

 5           A prayer for punitive damages “must set forth the elements as stated in the general punitive

 6    damage statute, Civil Code section 3294.” (Turman, 191 Cal.App.4th at p. 63.) Those elements

 7    include “that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Cal. Civ. Code

 8    § 3294(a).) To satisfy the statutory definition, the allegations must show “‘intentional,’ ‘willful,’

 9    or ‘conscious’ wrongdoing of a ‘despicable’ or ‘injur[ious]’ nature.” 2 (College Hospital Inc. v.

10    Super. Ct. (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721 (citation omitted).) Conclusory assertions are not enough—a

11    plaintiff seeking punitive damages must allege specific facts supporting their demand. (See

12    Brosseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 872 [“conclusory characterization of defendant’s

13    conduct as intentional, wilful and fraudulent” was “patently insufficient” to satisfy section 3294];

14    Rutter Group, Cal. Prac. Guide Civ. Pro. Before Trial § 6:158 [plaintiff “must allege specific facts

15    showing that defendant’s conduct was oppressive, fraudulent or malicious”].)

16           Musk does not even make a conclusory allegation of oppression, fraud, or malice here, still

17    less allegations of specific facts showing such conduct. Nothing is alleged to suggest that

18    Defendants “intended” to injure Musk or that Defendants acted in “conscious disregard” of Musk’s

19    rights. Musk has not alleged anything coming close to “despicable conduct.” (Cal. Civ. Code

20    § 3294(c)(1), (2).) Nor has Musk alleged any fraud by Defendants. Musk’s allegations that

21    Defendants “induce[d]” him to make contributions to OpenAI fail to allege the necessary intent to

22    deceive or show the “concealment of a material fact known” to Defendants. (See ¶¶ 129, 141; Cal.

23

24    2
        The statute defines “malice” as “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the
      plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious
25    disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Cal. Civ. Code § 3294(c)(1).) “Oppression” is
26    “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of
      that person’s rights.” (Id. § 3294(c)(2).) And “fraud” is “an intentional misrepresentation, deceit,
27    or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the
      defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.”
28    (Id. § 3294(c)(3).)

 6    a result, the Court should strike his prayer for punitive damages. 191 Cal.App.4th at
