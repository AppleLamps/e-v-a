 1                         MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

 2          Pursuant to Federal Rule of Evidence 702(a), Defendant Microsoft respectfully moves

 3   the Court for an order excluding proposed testimony from Plaintiff’s expert David Schizer

 4   related to Microsoft’s conduct.

 5   I.     INTRODUCTION

 6          Plaintiff retained David Schizer, a Columbia University law professor and former dean,

 7   as an expert in nonprofit organizations to testify about the “usual and customary practices for

 8   nonprofit organizations, with a particular focus on nonprofits’ use of for-profit affiliates.” Ex.

 9   A (Schizer Rep. ¶¶ 1-2) (emphasis added). To that end, Dean Schizer’s expert reports purport

10   to provide a framework of customs and practices associated with nonprofits. He then evaluates

11   OpenAI’s conduct—not Microsoft’s—within the context of that framework to opine on

12   whether OpenAI complied with those standard nonprofit practices. (Id. ¶ 3). Dean Schizer

13   was not asked to assess Microsoft’s conduct in its dealings with OpenAI. (Id. ¶¶ 2-3).

14          Despite that, at his deposition Dean Schizer went well outside the scope of his

15   assignment to also render opinions characterizing Microsoft’s conduct in its business dealings

16   with OpenAI. In particular, he suggested that Microsoft, which of course is not a nonprofit,

17   took advantage of OpenAI’s alleged misconduct and acted improperly by, e.g.: (1) offering to

18   hire OpenAI employees after Mr. Altman’s November 2023 firing; (2) suggesting or “vetoing”

19   board candidates for OpenAI; and (3) negotiating a really good deal with OpenAI (collectively,
20   Schizer’s “Microsoft Opinions”). Ex. B (Schizer Dep. at 299:12-20; 304:14-24; 306:21-

21   307:18). He also broadened the scope of his assignment, insisting that it “would include

22   observations about the choices, conduct of the for-profit partner,” despite his very limited for-

23   profit experience and no support in his reports. (Id. at 280:8-20). These opinions, which focus

24   on the propriety of Microsoft’s “for-profit” conduct, are impermissible under Rule 702(a)

25   because they do not fit within Dean Schizer’s nonprofit-focused background, qualifications, or

26   methodology.

10   Such testimony does not fit Dean Schizer’s nonprofit-focused analysis and is thus unhelpful to

11   the jury, highly prejudicial to Microsoft, and should be excluded.

12   II.    LEGAL STANDARD

13          The proponent of expert testimony must demonstrate by a preponderance of the

14   evidence that “the expert’s scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge will help the

15   trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” Fed. R. Evid. 702(a);

16   Engilis v. Monsanto Co., 151 F.4th 1040, 1049 (9th Cir. 2025). “[T]here is no presumption in

17   favor of admission.” Engilis, 151 F.4th at 1049. In evaluating whether an expert’s testimony

18   will help the trier of fact, courts consider whether the testimony “fits the facts of the case and

19   is relevant to the task at hand.” In re Nat’l Collegiate Athletic Ass’n Athletic Grant-in-Aid Cap
20   Antitrust Litig., No. 14-CV-02758, 2018 WL 1948593, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 25, 2018)

21   (internal quotation marks omitted). Expert testimony should be excluded when the expert’s

22   qualifications do not “fit” the topic of their opinion. Huang v. TriNet HR III, Inc., 20-CV-

23   2293, 2023 WL 3092626, at *7 (M.D. Fla. Apr. 26, 2023); Doe v. Rollins Coll., No. 18-CV-

24   1069, 2020 WL 8408417, at *4 (M.D. Fla. Jan. 7, 2020) aff’d, 77 F.4th 1340 (11th Cir. 2023).

25   Additionally, “an expert opinion must be based on reliable methodology and must reliably flow

26   from that methodology.” Heller v. Shaw Indus., Inc., 167 F.3d 146, 152 (3d Cir. 1999).

 1   III.    ARGUMENT

 2           Dean Schizer’s admittedly limited experience with for-profit entities and non-profit

 3   centric analysis are inadequate to support his opinions that seek to judge the propriety of

 4   Microsoft’s conduct, a for-profit company, in its business relationship with OpenAI.

 5           Dean Schizer’s assignment was clear: opine on the usual and customary practices for

 6   nonprofit organizations, with a focus on nonprofits’ use of for-profit affiliates, and whether

 7   OpenAI has complied with those nonprofit practices. Ex. A (Schizer Rep. ¶ 1). This scope

 8   makes sense given his nonprofit experience and admittedly limited for-profit work experience

 9   as an associate in a law firm nearly thirty years ago. (Id. ¶¶ 1-2); see also Ex. B (Schizer Dep.

10   at 272:13-19). Consistent with this, his reports include a nonprofit-focused methodology and

11   provide no guidelines for for-profit conduct. Ex. A (Schizer Rep. ¶¶ 1-3). Dean Schizer

12   confirmed as much during his deposition, testifying that he did not focus on “independent or

13   separate standards or customs and practices regarding the for-profit’s conduct in such

14   collaborations.” Ex. B (Schizer Dep. at 282:1-8).

15           Yet, at the same deposition, Dean Schizer departed from his assignment, experience,

16   and nonprofit methodology by offering opinions about Microsoft’s conduct in its interactions

17   with OpenAI. His testimony characterized Microsoft as a wrongdoer when it, e.g.: (1) offered

18   to hire OpenAI employees after Mr. Altman’s November 2023 firing; (2) suggested or vetoed

19   board candidates for OpenAI; and (3) negotiated a really good deal with OpenAI. (Id. at
20   299:12-20; 304:14-24; 306:21-307:18).        And when asked whether Microsoft had done

21   anything wrong, he insisted that it was within the scope of his assignment. (Id. at 280:8-20;

22   299:12-20; 304:14-24; 306:21-307:18).

23           These opinions are a significant departure from Dean Schizer’s stated focus on the

24   customs and practices of nonprofits and OpenAI’s adherence to them. This testimony reads

25   more like layperson speculation than expert opinion and finds no support in his reports, which

26   fail to provide any guidelines or standards for for-profit conduct, any explanation of the

27   methodology or the standard he applies to characterize Microsoft’s conduct, or any analysis on

 3          His testimony regarding Microsoft’s negotiations surrounding certain provisions of the

 7   framework for assessing what a “really good deal” looks like for a for-profit company in such

 8   circumstances.    And while his statement naturally implies that Microsoft obtained a

 9   significantly better deal than other for-profit companies would have, he never provides any

10   relative analysis for what terms another similarly situated for-profit would have tried to

11   negotiate. (Id. at 299:12-300:19). Plaintiff’s counsel insists that such testimony is relevant to

12   OpenAI’s conduct, but there is a critical difference between opining that OpenAI did not

13   negotiate for certain necessary protections and characterizing Microsoft’s negotiation tactics

14   as somehow taking advantage of OpenAI.

15          Because Dean Schizer’s characterizations of Microsoft’s actions are not grounded in

16   any custom, practice, or readily identifiable framework regarding for-profit entities, his

17   methodology does not fit his Microsoft Opinions and should be excluded. See Fed. R. Evid.

18   702(a). And, allowing Dean Schizer to testify about the propriety of actions of for-profit

19   entities, which he is not remotely qualified to do, would be highly prejudicial and risk
20   misleading the jury. See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc., 43 F.3d 1311, 1321 n.17 (9th

21   Cir. 1995) (“[E]xpert testimony carries special dangers to the fact-finding process because it

22   ‘can be both powerful and quite misleading.’”) (quoting Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms.,

23   Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993)).

24   IV.    CONCLUSION

25          The Court should grant Microsoft’s Motion to Exclude Dean Schizer’s testimony

26   related to Microsoft’s conduct.
