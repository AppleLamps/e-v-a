 1                                         [PROPOSED] ORDER

 2           Before the Court is Defendant Microsoft Corporation’s (“Microsoft” or “Defendant”)

 3    Motion for Summary Judgment (“Motion”). Having considered the Motion and all documents and

 4    arguments related thereto, the Court hereby ORDERS that the Motion is GRANTED.

 5                                             BACKGROUND

 6           There are only two claims currently at issue against Microsoft: Count 19 (aiding and

 7    abetting breach of fiduciary duty to Musk) and Count 4 (breach of quasi contract / unjust

 8    enrichment).1 Having reviewed the evidence submitted by the parties, the Court finds that the

 9    following material facts are undisputed: 1) Microsoft was not involved with the founding of
10    OpenAI, Inc. or the incorporation of OpenAI’s for-profit subsidiary; 2) no one at Microsoft was

11    ever told about any purported obligations or duties owed to Musk, or any purported restrictions

12    relating to Musk’s donations to OpenAI; 3) OpenAI and its for-profit subsidiary affirmatively

13    represented to Microsoft that it had the authority to enter into agreements with Microsoft, and that

14    the agreements did not violate any third-party agreements or rights; and 4) OpenAI’s nonprofit

15    board independently approved Microsoft’s agreements to invest in and collaborate with OpenAI.

16                                                  ANALYSIS

17           Summary judgment is proper when there is “no genuine issue as to any material fact and

18    that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S.

19    317, 323-24 (1986) (citing then-Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a)). “The mere existence of a scintilla of
20    evidence in support of the plaintiff’s position will be insufficient; there must be evidence on which

21    the jury could reasonably find for the plaintiff.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242,

 1     248-49 (1986). Thus, the Court must ask whether there is evidence upon which a jury can properly

 2     proceed to find a verdict for the party producing it, upon whom the onus of proof is imposed. Id.

 3              Once the moving party meets its initial burden—including by arguing that there is an

 4     absence of evidence of an element where the non-movant bears the burden of proving the element

 5     at trial, Fairbank v. Wunderman Cato Johnson, 212 F.3d 528, 531 (9th Cir. 2000)—the non-

 6     moving party must go beyond the pleadings and, by its own evidence, “set forth specific facts

 7     showing that there is a genuine issue for trial.” Anderson at 248 (citing then-Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(e)).

 8     If the non-moving party fails to point to evidence precluding summary judgment, the moving party

 9     is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 323; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(e)(3).
10         I.     Count 19 (Aiding and Abetting Breach of Fiduciary Duty to Musk)

11              To establish a claim for aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty, Musk must prove:

12     (a) knowledge that another’s conduct constitutes breach of fiduciary duty; and (b) substantial

13     assistance or encouragement to the other to so act. Casey v. United States Bank Nat’l Ass’n, 127

14     Cal. App. 4th 1138, 1144, 1148 (2005).2

15                a. Actual Knowledge

16              If an aiding and abetting defendant does not have “actual knowledge of the specific

17     primary wrong the defendant substantially assisted,” the claim must fail. AngioScore, Inc. v.

18     TriReme Med., LLC, 70 F. Supp. 3d 951, 957 (N.D. Cal. 2014) (cit. omit.). The undisputed

19     evidence submitted by the parties shows that Microsoft had no knowledge of any purported duties
20     or obligations that OpenAI, Altman, or Brockman owed to Musk, or any breach of those duties.

21     Numerous witnesses affiliated with the Plaintiff, OpenAI, and Microsoft testified that no one ever

22     told anyone at Microsoft about any purported duties or obligations OpenAI, Altman, or Brockman

23     owed to Musk. See Microsoft Motion at Section IV(A)(1)(b). Nor is there any evidence that

24     suggests otherwise. Id. Microsoft also points to due diligence it conducted, which did not reveal

25     or even suggest that any duties or obligations were owed to Musk. Id.

 1             In response, Musk points only to evidence suggesting that Microsoft should have known

 2     that OpenAI owed certain legal duties to Musk. But that is insufficient under the law. AngioScore,

 3     70 F. Supp. 3d at 957; In re Columbia Pipeline Group, Inc. Merger Litig., 2025 WL 1693491, at

 4     *23 (Del. June 17, 2025) (aider and abettor must have actual, not just constructive, knowledge of

 5     fiduciary duty breach). Microsoft met its burden, and Musk has not pointed to any evidence that

 6     creates a genuine issue as to whether Microsoft had actual knowledge. Microsoft is thus entitled

 7     to summary judgment because a reasonable jury could not find that Microsoft had actual

 8     knowledge of any duty or obligation to Musk, which is a necessary element of Musk’s aiding and

 9     abetting claim.
10               b. Intent

11             Although Microsoft is entitled to summary judgment on Count 19 based on the actual

12     knowledge element, the Court reaches the same conclusion based on the separate element of intent

13     for aiding and abetting claims. A defendant can be held liable as a tortfeasor for acting in concert

14     only if he or she knew that a tort had been, or was to be, committed, and acted “with the intent of

15     facilitating the commission of that tort.” Casey, 127 Cal. App. 4th at 1146 (emphasis in original,

16     cit. omit.). There is no evidence that Microsoft intended to aid and abet the breach of fiduciary

17     duty to Musk. Indeed, the only evidence, as noted above, shows that: 1) Microsoft entered its

18     agreements with OpenAI and its for-profit subsidiary after OpenAI’s nonprofit board

19     independently approved each of the agreements, and 2) OpenAI entities affirmatively represented
20     to Microsoft that they had the authority to enter into the agreements with Microsoft and the

21     agreements would not violate any third-party rights. See Microsoft Motion at Section IV(A)(2)(b).

22     These facts are undisputed by Musk. On these facts, Microsoft “had no reason to know” about any

23     potential breach of duties to Musk, In re Radnor Holdings Corp., 353 B.R. 820, 844 (Bankr. D.

24     Del. 2006) and thus could not have intended to assist in their breach. Casey, 127 Cal. App. 4th at

25     1146.

27             A plaintiff’s claim for unjust enrichment fails when it is premised on the same factual

28     allegations as claims that are dismissed. Gudgel v. Clorox Co., 514 F.Supp.3d 1177, 1188 (N.D.

 1     Cal. 2021). Since the aiding and abetting claim fails as a matter of law, Musk’s unjust enrichment

 2     claim necessarily fails as well since it is predicated on the same conduct. See Microsoft Motion at

 3     Section IV(B)(1).

 4            In addition to alleging the same underlying conduct, Musk requests identical relief for his

 5     aiding and abetting and unjust enrichment claims. See Section IV(B)(2). This contravenes the

 6     federal common law requirement that a plaintiff must establish the lack of an adequate remedy at

 7     law before securing equitable restitution in federal court for past harm under California law.

 8     Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp., 49 F.4th 1300, 1303 (9th Cir. 2022). Because Musk would

 9     have an identical adequate remedy at law under his aiding and abetting claim, his standalone unjust
10     enrichment claim fails as well. Stapleton v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., 779 F. Supp. 3d 1059,

11     1076 (N.D. Cal. 2025); Sepanossian v. National Ready Mixed Concrete Co., 97 Cal. App. 5th 192,

12     207-08 (2023).

13            Finally, even if the Court undertook an independent analysis of Musk’s quasi-contract /

14     unjust enrichment claim, the result would be the same. There is nothing “unjust” about a for-profit

15     company like Microsoft realizing financial gain from a commercial partnership so long as it

16     transacted without knowledge of any underlying wrong. First Nationwide Savings v. Perry, 11

17     Cal. App. 4th 1657, 1663-64 (1992). As stated above, there is no evidence that Microsoft had

18     knowledge of any duties owed to Musk or any breaches of any such unknown duties. On the

19     contrary, OpenAI’s board approved the agreements and OpenAI affirmatively represented that
20     they would not violate any third-party agreements or rights. Accordingly, any benefits Microsoft

21     received from the strategic partnership with OpenAI and its for-profit subsidiary cannot be deemed

22     “unjust.” Id.

23                                          CONCLUSION

24            For the reasons set forth above, the Court GRANTS Microsoft’s motion for summary

25     judgment in full.
