16                                            BACKGROUND
17          This Order presumes familiarity with Plaintiffs’ factual allegations and with the procedural
18   history of the case.
19          On May 1, 2025, the Court dismissed with leave to amend Plaintiffs’ claims in the First
20   Amended Complaint (the “FAC”) for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing
21   and for violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c) and (d). See Dkt. 163 at 3, 9-10. On May 22, 2025,
22   Plaintiffs filed a Second Amended Complaint (the “SAC”). See Dkt. 170. The Court finds that the
23   amended pleading does not cure the defects identified in the May 1 ruling, and therefore dismisses
24   the implied covenant and RICO claims with prejudice.
25                                               ANALYSIS
26          “To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual
27   matter that, when accepted as true, states a claim that is plausible on its face.” Pac. Recovery Sols.

 2   556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)). Any claim grounded in allegations of fraud or false representations must

 4   and why it is false,” and be “specific enough to give defendants notice of the particular misconduct

 5   which is alleged to constitute the fraud charged so that they can defend against the charge and not

 6   just deny that they have done anything wrong.” Yetter v. Ford Motor Co., 428 F. Supp. 3d 210,

 7   219-20 (N.D. Cal. 2019) (first quoting Ebeid ex rel. U.S. v. Lungwitz, 616 F.3d 993, 998 (9th Cir.

 8   2010), then quoting Semegen v. Weidner, 780 F.2d 727, 731 (9th Cir. 1985)). “Rule 9(b)’s

 9   heightened pleading standard” for fraud—which Plaintiffs have conceded applies to their RICO

10   claims, see Dkt. 128 at 15—is particularly “exacting in the context of RICO claims,” Aniel v. PHH

11   Mortg. Corp., 2022 WL 2164702, at *6 (N.D. Cal. June 1, 2022).

12             Plaintiffs’ implied covenant and RICO claims are dismissed under these standards.

13      I.     IMPLIED COVENANT CLAIM (COUNT III)

14             Musk’s implied covenant claim is dismissed for the same reason the Court dismissed it the

15   first time: It is “based on the same conduct” and therefore duplicative of Musk’s implied-in-fact

16   contract claim. See Dkt. 163 at 3.

17             While the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is “implied by law in every

18   contract,” Guz v. Bechtel Nat’l, Inc., 24 Cal. 4th 317, 349 (2000), “in most cases, a claim for breach

19   of the implied covenant can add nothing to a claim for breach of contract,” Lamke v. Sunstate

20   Equip. Co., LLC, 387 F. Supp. 2d 1044, 1047 (N.D. Cal. 2004). The covenant “cannot impose

21   substantive duties or limits on the contracting parties beyond those incorporated in the specific

22   terms of their agreement.” Guz, 24 Cal. 4th at 336-37, 349-50. “[I]nsofar as [a defendant’s] acts are

23   directly actionable as a breach of an implied-in-fact contract term, a claim that merely realleges

24   that breach as a violation of the covenant is superfluous.” Id. at 352; see also, e.g., Sensible Foods,

25   LLC v. World Gourmet, Inc., 2012 WL 566304, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 21, 2012). And insofar as the

26   acts are not actionable as a breach of the implied-in-fact contract, “the claim is invalid” because it

27   seeks to impose obligations “beyond those to which the parties actually agreed.” Guz, 24 Cal. 4th

28   at 352.

 3   Although the SAC identifies three categories of conduct that purportedly violated the implied

 4   covenant, these allegations at bottom “rely[] on the same alleged acts” and seek “the same damages

 5   [and] other relief” as form the basis of Musk’s “companion contract cause of action.” Careau &

 6   Co. v. Sec. Pac. Bus. Credit, Inc., 222 Cal. App. 3d 1371, 1395 (1990).

 7             First, the SAC alleges that Altman and OpenAI, Inc. “systematically and unilaterally

 8   reinterpreted key terms of their agreement in ways that undermined it” by allegedly “evading

 9   conflict-of-interest protections by defining board member ‘independence’ in an extraordinarily

10   narrow fashion.” ¶ 269(a).1 This allegation is just a reformulation of Musk’s claim that Altman and

11   OpenAI, Inc. violated an implied-in-fact contract term requiring that OpenAI, Inc. “‘always

12   assiduously act to minimize conflicts of interest among [its] employees and stakeholders that could

13   compromise’ the non-profit’s mission.” ¶ 247(i); see also ¶ 250(j).

14             Second, Musk alleges that Altman and OpenAI, Inc. “obscured OpenAI’s for-profit

15   activities through complex corporate structures, deliberately designed to prevent Musk and the

16   public from discovering their actual operations, and by purporting to maintain the charity’s control

17   of their for-profit business when, in fact, the opposite is true.” ¶ 269(b). The only distinction

18   between this allegation and similar allegations offered to support Musk’s implied-in-fact contract

19   claim is that the focus is on “obscur[ing] OpenAI’s for-profit activities,” id. (emphasis added),

20   rather than engaging in such activities, see ¶ 250(a), (b), (c), (l) (alleging that Altman and OpenAI,

21   Inc. breached implied-in-fact contract by assigning the non-profit’s intellectual property to the

22   “For-Profit Entities,” allowing the “reap[ing of] significant financial benefits from commercial

23   activities” and “inure[ment]” to private benefit, and supposedly “working to convert the non-profit

24   into a fully for-profit commercial entity”). That does not suffice to plead conduct separately

25   actionable under the implied covenant. See, e.g., Synopsys, Inc. v. Real Intent, Inc., 2024 WL

26   5364480, at *26 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 26, 2024).

10     II.   RICO CLAIMS (COUNTS XX & XXI)

11           In its prior order, the Court dismissed the FAC’s claims for violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c)

12   and (d) because they failed to “identify how the named defendants operated the entity through a

13   pattern of racketeering activity.” Dkt. 163 at 10; see Verde Media Corp. v. Levi, 2015 WL 374934,

14   at *5 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 28, 2015) (plaintiff must allege with particularity “(1) conduct (2) of an

15   enterprise (3) through a pattern (4) of racketeering activity”). The only alleged actions of the

16   “enterprise,” OpenAI, Inc., were not RICO predicates, and the other conduct alleged was not

17   “tethered to the enterprise’s conduct.” Dkt. 163 at 10. The SAC fails to cure this defect.

18           First, the SAC pleads not a “pattern of racketeering activity,” but at best a single RICO

19   predicate with a single purported victim: Musk. As in the FAC, the SAC invokes only the wire

20   fraud statute to try to make a “pattern” showing. Tracking Musk’s common-law fraud claim, the

21   only specific representations identified as having been made with “intent to defraud” are supposed

22   “assurances to Musk” by Altman and Brockman “in September 2017 that they remained committed

23   to the original non-profit structure and mission of OpenAI, Inc.” ¶ 423; compare ¶¶ 301-07. No

24   defendant other than Altman and Brockman is alleged to have committed any predicate act

25   identified in the “Wire Fraud Predicate Offenses” section or the “Pattern of Racketeering Activity”

26   section of Count XX. Assuming these allegations suffice to plead a wire fraud offense with

27   particularity, they do not plead a pattern—much less, as required, a pattern against each named

28   defendant. See Howard v. Am. Online Inc., 208 F.3d 741, 746 (9th Cir. 2000) (a pattern requires

 2   see also Attia v. Google LLC, 983 F.3d 420, 427 (9th Cir. 2020) (affirming dismissal of RICO

 3   claims as to those defendants for whom “no pattern [was] established” because they were not

 4   alleged to have been involved in the two alleged predicate acts).

 5          Other allegations offered to support the RICO counts do not plead wire fraud predicates

 6   with the requisite particularity. Plaintiffs’ allegations of “false and/or misleading representations”

 7   from “December 11, 2015 to today” in “Defendants[’] . . . online marketing (e.g., website),

 8   advertisements, and promotions,” ¶ 428, merely cross-reference a variety of statements mostly

 9   predating December 2015 (¶¶ 82-87) or pleaded as contractual terms rather than supposed

10   fraudulent statements (¶ 247). Plaintiffs make no effort to identify which of the promotional

11   materials among them are supposed to establish wire fraud, and many of the statements identified

12   are not sourced to any defendant named in the RICO claim. None can supply the grounding for a

13   well-pleaded wire fraud predicate. See, e.g., Desoto v. Condon, 371 F. App’x 822, 824 (9th Cir.

14   2010); Seva v. Shri Shirdi Sai Baba Sansthin L.A., 2013 WL 1431673, at *4 (C.D. Cal. Apr. 9,

15   2013). Similarly, Plaintiffs do not plead any additional predicate act with their new allegations that

16   the “For-Profit Entities” cautioned investors in the For-Profit Entities that they should not expect

17   a return on their investment and that the For-Profit Entities “exist[ed] to advance OpenAI, Inc.’s

18   mission.” See ¶ 447. Plaintiffs do not plead which specific entity or entities made these

19   representations, when or in what context they were made, why they were false, or how (or even

20   that) they were made with intent to defraud. See, e.g., Pac. Recovery Sols., 481 F. Supp. 3d at 1028;

21   Cnty. of Marin v. Deloitte Consulting LLP, 836 F. Supp. 2d 1030, 1040 (N.D. Cal. 2011). Nor do

22   Plaintiffs explain how these communications, directed to investors in the for-profit enterprise rather

23   than donors to OpenAI, Inc., could have “reassure[d] Musk and induced him to make further

24   financial contributions” to OpenAI, Inc. ¶ 447.

25          Second, as with the FAC (see Dkt. 163 at 10), the SAC again fails to plead that any supposed

26   racketeering activity was the “conduct” of OpenAI, Inc.’s “affairs”—as opposed to the conduct of

27   the defendants’ own affairs. See 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c); Pac. Recovery Sols., 481 F. Supp. 3d at 1027.

28   As discussed, the only predicate arguably pleaded with particularity centers on conduct of Altman

 7          Third, the SAC’s allegations negate a key element of any § 1962(c) claim: “the existence

 8   of two distinct entities: (1) a person. . .and (2) an ‘enterprise.’” Ferrari v. Mercedes-Benz USA,

 9   LLC, 2016 WL 7188030, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 12, 2016) (emphasis added). According to the SAC,

10   “OpenAI, Inc. and the For-Profit Entities operate together as a unitary enterprise, the primary

11   purpose of which is now for-profit,” and “OpenAI, Altman [and] Brockman [] have become alter

12   egos of one another.” ¶ 237. In other words, there is no “enterprise” distinct from the “persons”

13   alleged to have conducted its affairs through a pattern of racketeering activity.

14          Because Plaintiffs’ substantive RICO count fails, so too does the conspiracy claim on which

15   it is based. See Sanford v. MemberWorks, Inc., 625 F.3d 550, 559 (9th Cir. 2010).

16                                             CONCLUSION

17          For the foregoing reasons, the OpenAI Defendants’ Motion to Dismiss is GRANTED, and

18   Counts III, XX, and XXI of the second amended complaint of Plaintiffs Elon Musk and X.AI Corp.

19   are DISMISSED WITH PREJUDICE.
