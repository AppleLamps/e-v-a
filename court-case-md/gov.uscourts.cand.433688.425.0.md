 1                                            INTRODUCTION
 2          At trial, Musk plans to argue that OpenAI abandoned its nonprofit character and mission,
 3   including by establishing a for-profit affiliate and entering into commercial arrangements with
 4   Microsoft. Musk’s fifth motion in limine seeks to preclude—before any evidence has been
 5   presented—two of the OpenAI Defendants’ experts from testifying to help rebut that charge.
 6          The motion should be denied. It mischaracterizes the expert testimony that the OpenAI
 7   Defendants may offer, and ignores repeated rulings from this Court denying similar premature
 8   motions. E.g., Corcoran v. CVS Pharmacy, Inc., 2021 WL 633809, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 18, 2021)
 9   (Rogers, J.); Rogers v. Terex Utils., Inc., 2022 WL 16544856, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 21, 2022). It
10   also ignores those rulings’ admonitions that (i) testimony from multiple experts is not “cumulative”
11   where, as here, similar topics are “addressed from different perspectives,” and (ii) the Court’s
12   imposition of “time limits at trial” will “curb the threat of duplicative testimony.” Id.
13                                            BACKGROUND
14          The OpenAI Defendants designated two experts to address particular aspects of nonprofit
15   custom and practice. Professor Daniel Hemel, the John R. Shad Professor of Law at New York
16   University School of Law, will offer testimony about the ways in which nonprofit organizations
17   customarily engage with the for-profit sector, including through the use of for-profit affiliates and
18   IP licensing arrangements.
19          The OpenAI Defendants also expect to offer testimony from Professor Peter Frumkin, the
20   Director of the Gradel Institute of Charity at Oxford University and a Chair in Social Policy at the
21   University of Pennsylvania’s School of Social Policy and Practice. Specializing in philanthropy
22   and nonprofit management, Professor Frumkin brings a different perspective to these issues,
23   through his work with nonprofit leaders and managers on strategies to advance their nonprofit
24   missions. Professor Frumkin will offer testimony about the broader trend of nonprofits adopting
25   hybrid organizational models, including to raise needed capital, attract and retain key talent, and
26   engage with consumers and markets. The OpenAI Defendants expect these examinations to be
27   concise, to the point, and helpful to the jury in evaluating Musk’s claims.

 7   to many of Dean Schizer’s claims. Because Dean Schizer’s report also wades into corporate-
 8   governance and transaction-structuring issues, including dealmaking and board process, the
 9   OpenAI Defendants designated John Coates—the John F. Cogan Professor of Law and Economics
10   at Harvard Law School—as a rebuttal expert, to the extent needed after Dean Schizer is examined

12   both the for-profit and nonprofit sectors.
13           The OpenAI Defendants presently estimate that their examinations of Professors Hemel,
14   Frumkin, and Coates would consume a total of 1.25 hours of their allotted trial time, if the decision
15   is made during trial (and after Musk rests) to call all three experts in their case.
16                                                ARGUMENT
17           While “[t]he Court retains discretion to exclude cumulative expert testimony,” Kirola v.
18   City & Cnty. of S.F., 2010 WL 3476681, at *12 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 2, 2010), “[e]vidence should not
19   be excluded on a motion in limine” before trial “unless it is inadmissible on all potential grounds,”
20   Johnson v. Nat. Gas Fuel Sys., Inc., 2024 WL 5047209, at *1, *4 (E.D. Cal. Dec. 9, 2024).
21           “The fact that multiple witnesses may offer similar opinions is not in and of itself
22   cumulative, particularly if the same topic is addressed from different perspectives.” Corcoran v.
23   CVS Pharmacy, Inc., 2021 WL 633809, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 18, 2021); see also, e.g., Hernandez
24   v. Sutter Med. Center of Santa Rosa, 2009 WL 1227903, at *2 (N.D. Cal. May 1, 2009) (“the
25   anticipated testimony from Defendant’s three experts will not be duplicative within the meaning of
26   Rule 403” because “the basis and perspective of their respective opinions are predicated specifically
27   on their particular areas of expertise”). That “the Court has imposed time limits to ensure that the
28   parties are being efficient with the presentation of evidence,” Corcoran, 2021 WL 633809, at *2,

 1   is another bulwark against the “threat” of “duplicative testimony.” Rogers v. Terex Utils., Inc.,
 2   2022 WL 16544856, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 21, 2022); see also In re Apple Inc. Secs. Litig., 2023
 3   WL 4556765, at *7 (N.D. Cal. July 17, 2023) (“Parties’ trial strategy allows for multiple experts
 4   and decisions made later with respect to which expert will testify as to which issues at trial.”).
 5          For these reasons, courts in this District, including this Court, routinely reject attempts—
 6   like Musk’s here—to preclude purportedly duplicative expert testimony before trial: “It would
 7   therefore be premature for the Court to conclude that the testimony is cumulative when such
 8   evidence has yet to be presented.” Corcoran, 2021 WL 633809, at *2 (denying motion in limine to
 9   “limit[] defendant to only one expert on the grounds that the proffered testimony from its three
10   expert witnesses” is “duplicative”); see also Rogers, 2022 WL 16544856, at *3 (denying motion as
11   premature); In re Apple Inc. Secs. Litig., 2023 WL 4556765, at *7 (Rogers, J.) (same); Apple iPod
12   iTunes Antitrust Litig., 2014 WL 12719192, at *1 (Rogers, J.) (N.D. Cal. Nov. 18, 2014) (same);
13   Hernandez, 2009 WL 1227903, at *2 (same). 1
14          The Court should do the same here. First, the testimony is not cumulative. As explained,
15   Professors Hemel and Frumkin address these matters “from different perspectives,” Corcoran,
16   2021 WL 633809, at *2, with Professor Hemel drawing on his expertise in nonprofit structures, and
17   Professor Frumkin on his research in philanthropy and work with nonprofit managers. And
18   Professor Coates is designated only as a rebuttal witness, if needed, to respond to Dean Schizer’s
19   potential testimony on matters relating to corporate governance and dealmaking. Second, the
20   parties’ time limits “will curb the threat of duplicative testimony,” ensuring that the presentation of
21   evidence remains efficient. Given these time constraints, the OpenAI Defendants have neither the
22   intention nor the incentive to offer duplicative testimony. Third, it would be “premature for the
23   Court to conclude that the testimony is cumulative when such evidence has yet to be presented,”
