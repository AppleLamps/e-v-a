 8                                               ARGUMENT

 9           Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), “the court should freely give leave [to amend

10    a pleading] when justice so requires.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2). “Where a court strikes an affirmative

11    defense, leave to amend should be freely given so long as there is no prejudice to the moving party.”

12    Rodgers v. Claim Jumper Rest., LLC, 2014 WL 1760959, at *1 (N.D. Cal. May 1, 2014); Haskins

13    v. Cherokee Grand Ave., LLC, 2012 WL 1110014, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 2, 2012) (same).

14           “[P]ublic policy strongly encourages courts to permit amendments,” Oden v. Voong, 2019

15    WL 3068418, at *1 (N.D. Cal. July 12, 2019) (citing Waldrip v. Hall, 548 F.3d 729, 732 (9th Cir.

16    2008)), and “[t]he policy of allowing amendments ‘is to be applied with extreme liberality.’” Id.

17    (quoting Owens v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc., 244 F.3d 708, 712 (9th Cir. 2001)). “All

18    inferences are drawn ‘in favor of granting the motion,’” Finjan, Inc. v. Qualys Inc., 2020 WL

19    1865264, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 13, 2020), and “the nonmoving party bears the burden of

20    demonstrating why leave to amend should not be granted.” Vasquez v. Libre By Nexus, Inc., 2018

21    WL 6305616, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 3, 2018).

 9           Courts in this Circuit “consider five factors in ruling on a motion for leave to amend: (1) bad

10    faith, (2) undue delay, (3) prejudice to the opposing party, (4) futility of the amendment, and

11    (5) whether the movant has previously amended its pleadings.” Finjan, 2020 WL 1865264, at *1.

12    “Not all factors merit equal weight,” and “[p]rejudice” to the opposing party “is the ‘touchstone of

13    the inquiry under rule 15(a).’” Id. (quoting Eminence Cap., LLC v. Aspeon, Inc., 316 F.3d 1048,

14    1051 (9th Cir. 2003)). “In the absence of ‘strong evidence’ of one or more of these factors, leave

15    to amend must be granted.” Id. (quoting Sonoma Cnty. Ass’n of Retired Emps. v. Sonoma Cnty.,

16    708 F.3d 1109, 1117 (9th Cir. 2013)). Each of the relevant factors supports leave in this case:

17           (1) No bad faith in proposing the amendment. “In order for a court to find that a moving

18    party filed for leave to amend in bad faith, the adverse party must offer evidence that shows

19    wrongful motive on the part of the moving party.” Nat’l Union Fire Ins. Co. of Pitt., Pa. v. Elec.

20    Transit Inc., 2007 WL 3460280, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 14, 2007); see also WiAV Networks, LLC v.

21    Hewlett-Packard Co., 2010 WL 11484493, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 17, 2010) (explaining that “bad

22    faith” in this context means “bad faith in the sense of ‘wrongful motive’ for proposing the

23    amendment”). “Bad faith” has been found where, for example, a proposed amendment “merely []

24    duplicated other pending litigation,” served as “a dilatory tactic,” or sought to “harass or burden

25    the other party.” Finjan, Inc. v. Check Point Software Techs., Inc., 2019 WL 1455333, at *6 (N.D.

26    Cal. Apr. 2, 2019); WiAV Networks, 2010 WL 11484493, at *6. None of these ill motives exists

27    here. The OpenAI Defendants filed this motion promptly to assert key defenses against claims that,

28    if successful, could have substantial adverse impact. WiAV Networks, 2010 WL 11484493, at *6

 8    by submitting a proposed amended pleading simultaneously with their July 10 opposition to address

 9    the alleged deficiencies that Plaintiffs identified for the first time in their motion to strike, and then

10    by bringing this motion for leave within days of the Court’s July 29 order.

11            (3) No prejudice. As to this “most important” factor, Plaintiffs will suffer no prejudice if

12    the amendment is granted. See Cards Against Human., LLC v. Loftek Tech. Co., LLC, 2014 WL

13    120976, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 13, 2014). Plaintiffs have been on notice of these defenses for months:

14    The OpenAI Defendants’ April 9 answer to the FAC contained defenses of statute of limitations,

15    laches, and unclean hands, and the June 5 Answer added statute of frauds and termination of

16    contract. See supra pp. 2-3; see also Gonda v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc., 2015 WL 537770, at

17    *3 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 9, 2015) (granting leave and finding no prejudice where defendants “mentioned”

18    in a prior case management statement “intention” to rely on defense). When on July 10 the OpenAI

19    Defendants proposed a more streamlined and more detailed amended answer in response to

20    Plaintiffs’ motion to strike, Plaintiffs consented to the filing and the withdrawal of their motion.

21    See Dkt. 209 at 3. The proposed amendment here contains even fewer affirmative defenses and

22    even more factual detail than the July 10 proposed amendment to which Plaintiffs have already

23    agreed. Given Plaintiffs’ previous consent and long notice, there is no conceivable prejudice. See

24    Moua v. Int’l Bus. Machs. Corp., 2012 WL 370570, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 31, 2012) (granting leave:

25    “[d]efendants have not met their burden to show prejudice sufficient to outweigh the ‘extreme

26    liberality’ with which the court must examine Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend”).

27            (4) Not futile. A proposed amendment is futile where it “would fail to cure the pleading

28    deficiencies.” Cervantes v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., 656 F.3d 1034, 1041 (9th Cir. 2011).

 1    Futility here also requires showing that “under no set of circumstances could the defense succeed.”

 2    Rodgers, 2014 WL 1760959, at *1. Plaintiffs did not dispute that the earlier proposed amendment

 3    cured the alleged deficiencies they had identified—namely, too many defenses pleaded with too

 4    little detail. Nor did Plaintiffs argue that any of those defenses could not succeed. Now that the

 5    defenses are supported by yet further detail, the adequacy of the cure is all the more apparent.

 6           (5) Movant has not previously amended its pleadings. Finally, this case does not “involve

 7    repeated failure to cure deficiencies by amendments previously allowed.” Reimer v. Snohomish

 8    Cty. Fire Dist. No. 1, 853 F. App’x 59, 61-62 (9th Cir. 2021). The June 5 Answer was the OpenAI

 9    Defendants’ first response to the SAC, and the proposed July 10 amendment was not accepted by

10    the Court for filing. The OpenAI Defendants have not yet had an opportunity to cure, and they

11    would be “severely prejudiced” if forever barred from raising the few affirmative defenses now

12    contained in the revised pleading. See Oden, 2019 WL 3068418, at *1 (granting motion for leave

13    to amend answer to add defense because defendants would be “severely prejudiced if they did not

14    have the opportunity to raise the defense”).

17    defenses, as set forth in the proposed amended answer attached as Exhibit A to the Declaration of

18    William Frentzen, should be granted.
