 2 42-P. DISGORGEMENT OF WRONGFUL GAINS (Plaintiff Only)

 3          Plaintiff contends that Altman, Brockman, OpenAI, Inc., the other OpenAI Defendants, and

 4 Microsoft derived wrongful gains from Musk’s contributions to OpenAI by using those

 5 contributions for purposes different from the charitable purposes for which he contributed them. As

 6 a remedy, Plaintiff seeks an order disgorging those wrongful gains to the OpenAI nonprofit.

 7          Plaintiff may seek an order disgorging wrongful gains for each of the claims for which he

 8 proves liability. You must determine whether Plaintiff has met the requirements for an order

 9 disgorging wrongful gains and, if so, the amount of disgorgement to order.

10

11 PLAINTIFF’S AUTHORITY

12          Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 49(4) (“When restitution is intended to strip the

13 defendant of a wrongful gain, the standard of liability is not the value of the benefit conferred but

14 the amount of the profit wrongfully obtained.”); id. § 51(2), (4) (disgorgement of wrongful gains

15 available for each claim asserted); In re Facebook, Inc. Internet Tracking Litig., 956 F.3d 589, 599-

16 600 (9th Cir. 2020) (“California law recognizes a right to disgorgement of profits resulting from

17 unjust enrichment . . . .”); Ward v. Taggart, 51 Cal. 2d 736, 741 (1959) (“[T]he public policy of this

18 state does not permit one to ‘take advantage of his own wrong.’ ”); Am. Master Lease LLC v. Idanta

19 Partners, Ltd., 225 Cal. App. 4th 1451, 1483 (2014) (“Disgorgement based on unjust enrichment is

20 an appropriate remedy for aiding and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty.”); County of San

21 Bernardino v. Walsh, 158 Cal. App. 4th 533, 542-43 (2007) (where there is “conscious disregard of

22 the rights of another,” “[t]he emphasis is on the wrongdoer’s enrichment, not the victim’s loss,” and

23 the wrongdoer “should be required to disgorge all profit”); id. at 543 (“[D]isgorgement of profits is

24 particularly applicable in cases dealing with breach of a fiduciary duty.”); Meister v. Mensinger,

25 230 Cal. App. 4th 381, 398-99 (2014) (similar).

 1 44-P. DISGORGEMENT – AMOUNT (Plaintiff Only)

 2          If you decide that Plaintiff is entitled to an order for disgorgement of wrongful gains to the

 3 OpenAI nonprofit, you must decide the amount by which Altman, Brockman, OpenAI, Inc., the

 4 other OpenAI Defendants, or Microsoft wrongfully gained from Musk’s contributions to OpenAI.

 5 Plaintiff does not have to prove the exact dollar amount of each Defendant’s wrongful gains.

 6 However, Plaintiff must produce evidence that permits at least a reasonable approximation of the

 7 amount of the Defendant’s wrongful gain. You must not speculate or guess in determining those

 8 amounts.

 9

10 PLAINTIFF’S AUTHORITY

11          Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51(5)(d) (2011) (“A claimant who seeks disgorgement

12 of profit has the burden of producing evidence permitting at least a reasonable approximation of the

13 amount of the wrongful gain. Residual risk of uncertainty in calculating net profit is assigned to the

14 defendant.”).

 2          Plaintiff contends that OpenAI, Inc. and certain other OpenAI Defendants each derived

 3 wrongful gains from Musk’s claimed contributions to OpenAI, Inc. by using those contributions for

 4 purposes different from the charitable purposes for which he contributed them. Plaintiff contends

 5 that Microsoft derived wrongful gains from its knowing substantial assistance of OpenAI’s breach

18          You should also credit Defendants for any money expended in acquiring or preserving the

19 property or in carrying on the business that is the source of the profit or increased value Plaintiff

20 seeks. To do so, you should determine the amount of a Defendant’s profits or increased value

 4 DEFENDANTS’ AUTHORITY

 5          There is no CACI instruction for disgorgement. This proposed instruction is based on

 6 Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51 (2011), and the instructions given in Rodriguez v. Google,

 7 LLC, No. 3:20-cv-04688-RS, Dkt. 666 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 4, 2025); Proofpoint, Inc. v. Vade Secure,

 8 Inc., No. 3:19-cv-04238-MMC, Dkt. 805 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 25, 2021); and Am. Master Lease LLC v.

 9 Idanta Partners, Ltd., No. BC367987 (Cal. Super. Ct. 2017).

10          For the second paragraph, see Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51(5)(d) (2011)

11 (“reasonable approximation”); id. § 51(5)(a) (identifiable, measurable, and not unduly remote); id.

12 § 51 cmt. i (discussing plaintiff ’s burden of proof ); and the Rodriguez instruction (“You must not

13 speculate or guess in awarding damages.”).

14          For the third paragraph, see the Proofpoint instructions (disgorgement reaches “benefit that

15 he would not have otherwise achieved had he not breached that contract”); the Rodriguez instruction

16 (disgorgement reaches “benefit that it otherwise would not have achieved but for the at-issue data”);

17 and Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51 cmt. e (2011) (discussing “the increase over profits that

18 would otherwise have been realized, attributable to the defendant’s interference with the claimant’s

19 interests”).

20          For the final paragraph, see the Am. Master Lease instructions (“[y]ou should give

21 Defendants credit for money expended in acquiring or preserving the property or in carrying on the

22 business that is the source of the profit subject to an award of unjust enrichment”); the Rodriguez

23 instruction (directing the jury to “calculate the amount [of disgorgement]” by determining Google’s

24 “total revenue,” determining Google’s “expenses in obtaining that gross revenue,” and deducting

25 “Google’s expenses from that revenue”); and Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51 cmts. f & h

26 (2011) (discussing questions of causation, credits, and deductions).

 1 PLAINTIFF’S POSITION

 2          Plaintiff objects to Proposed Instruction 46-D’s statement that “Plaintiff contends that
 3 Microsoft derived wrongful gains from its knowing substantial assistance of OpenAI’s breach

 4 of charitable trust that arose from Musk’s charitable contributions” as misstating Plaintiff ’s

 5 claims and the law. Aiding and abetting is a species of secondary liability, so if the jury finds

 6 that Microsoft is liable for aiding and abetting the OpenAI Defendants’ breaches of charitable

 7 trust, Microsoft is liable for all wrongful gains that Microsoft derived as a result of the OpenAI

 8 Defendants’ breaches of charitable trust, not merely the wrongful gains that Microsoft derived

 9 from its substantial assistance to those breaches. See Frame v. PricewaterhouseCoopers LLP,

10 36 Cal. Rptr. 3d 209, 223 (2005) (“As an aider and abettor, PwC would be jointly and severally

11 liable to the victims of the fraud, which allegedly include Six Sigma and the Investors.”), review

12 granted, 132 P.3d 210 (Cal. 2006); Dan B. Dobbs et al., The Law of Torts § 435 (2d ed.) (“One

13 who knowingly provides substantial aid or encouragement to another’s commission of a tort is

14 also jointly and severally liable for it along with the person who actually carries out the tortious

15 acts.”); Richard B. LeVine, Inc. v. Higashi, 131 Cal. App. 4th 566, 579 (2005) (“Liability based

16 on an aiding and abetting or conspiracy theory is also ‘derivative,’ i.e., liability is imposed on

17 one person for the direct acts of another.”).

18          Plaintiff further objects to the statement “That means that any award to Musk may only
19 include increased profits or value that would not have otherwise been achieved had the

20 underlying wrong not occurred” on the ground that it misstates the law. Disgorgement of

21 wrongful gains does not require a showing of strict “but for” causation. See Restatement

22 (Third) of Restitution § 51 cmt. f (2011) (“Absence of but-for causation does not necessarily

23 exonerate the wrongdoer, because a finding that the defendant would have realized the profit

24 in any event does not compel the conclusion that the defendant, under the circumstances, has

25 not been unjustly enriched. To take an obvious example, a trustee who makes a profit from the

26 personal use of trust assets could not escape liability in restitution by proving that he could have

27 (and would have) made the same profit legitimately, supposing that his access to the trust assets

28 had been hindered in some way.”); Uzyel v. Kadisha, 188 Cal. App. 4th 866, 894 (2010); SEC

11 DEFENDANTS’ POSITION

12          The Defendants object to Musk’s Proposed Jury Instructions Nos. 42-P to 45-P. Despite
13 needlessly spanning four separate instructions, Musk’s proposed instructions omit several important

14 requirements in determining the amount of disgorgement: (1) that wrongful gains be identifiable,

15 measurable, and not unduly remote from the underlying wrong; (2) that wrongful gains be

16 attributable to the underlying wrong, and not include gains that Defendants would have accrued

17 anyway; and (3) that Defendants be credited for money expended in carrying on the business that is

18 the source of the gains sought to be disgorged. The Defendants’ Proposed Instruction No. 46-D

19 accounts for these requirements, while also instructing the jury on the issue of disgorgement in a

20 single, readily digestible instruction.

21          1. The Defendants’ proposed instruction includes the requirement that the alleged wrongful
22 gains “must be identifiable, measurable, and not unduly remote from the underlying wrong.” This

23 language is supported by the Restatement, which is clear that “[i]n determining net profit,” one

24 “rule[]” that applies is that the profit must be “identifiable and measurable and not unduly remote.”

25 Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51(5)(a). Musk does not object to this requirement.

26          2. The Defendants’ proposal includes the following paragraph: “In determining the amount
27 of a Defendant’s wrongful gains, you may only consider the amount of the net profit or increased

28 value that is attributable to the underlying wrong. That means that any award to Musk may only

 1 include increased profits or value that would not have otherwise been achieved had the underlying

 2 wrong not occurred.” Musk objects to the second sentence, but the language is supported by both

 3 the primary authority upon which Musk relies and recent Northern District of California jury

 4 instructions. See Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51 cmt. e (2011) (discussing “the increase

 5 over profits that would otherwise have been realized, attributable to the defendant’s interference

 6 with the claimant’s interests”); Rodriguez v. Google, LLC, No. 3:20-cv-04688-RS, Dkt. 666 (N.D.

 7 Cal. Sept. 4, 2025) (“benefit that it otherwise would not have achieved but for the at-issue data”);

 8 Proofpoint, Inc. v. Vade Secure, Inc., No. 3:19-cv-04238-MMC, Dkt. 805 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 25, 2021)

 9 (“benefit that he would not have otherwise achieved had he not breached that contract”).

10          3. The Defendants’ proposed instruction provides for credit “for any money expended in

11 acquiring or preserving the property or in carrying on the business that is the source of the profit or

12 increased value Musk seeks.” This language, too, is supported by the Restatement and California

13 law. See Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51 cmts. f & h (2011); Am. Master Lease LLC v.

14 Idanta Partners, Ltd., 225 Cal. App. 4th 1451, 1491-92 (2014) (“the trial court should instruct the

15 jury that it should give defendants credit for money expended in acquiring or preserving the property

16 or in carrying on the business that is the source of the profit subject to disgorgement”). Musk does

17 not object to this portion of the proposed instruction.

18          It is critical that the instruction reflect these requirements, which serve to reinforce “[t]he

19 object of restitution,” “to eliminate the profit from wrongdoing while avoiding, so far as possible,

20 the imposition of a penalty.” Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51(4) (2011).

 2         In addition to disgorgement of wrongful gains, Plaintiff seeks an injunction from the

 3 Court ordering Defendants to do or refrain from doing certain things, such as requiring OpenAI
