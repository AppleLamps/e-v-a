 1                             STATEMENT OF ISSUES TO BE DECIDED

 2          1.        Whether Plaintiffs Musk and xAI have satisfied their burden of establishing an

 3   entitlement to a preliminary injunction on their claim under Section 1 of the Sherman Act, 15 U.S.C.

 4   § 1, where (a) Musk and xAI have no standing to bring this claim; (b) there is no evidence of an

 5   agreement or conspiracy to restrain trade; (c) there is no evidence of an unreasonable restraint of

 6   trade; and (d) Musk and xAI make no showing of irreparable harm.

 7          2.        Whether Plaintiffs Musk and xAI have satisfied their burden of establishing an

 8   entitlement to a preliminary injunction on their claim under Section 8 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C.

 9   § 19, where (a) there are no current “interlocking directorates” between Microsoft and OpenAI;

10   (b) Musk and xAI have no standing to bring this claim; (c) no alleged “interlocking directorships”

11   violated Section 8; and (d) Musk and xAI make no showing of irreparable harm.

12          3.        Whether Plaintiff Musk has satisfied his burden of establishing an entitlement to a

13   preliminary injunction on his breach of charitable trust claim under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code

14   § 17510.8 where (a) as a former donor, he has no standing to bring this claim under Cal. Corp.

15   Code § 5142(a); (b) he fails to make a showing that he attached any conditions to his donations, let

16   alone that those conditions were breached; and (c) he makes no showing of irreparable harm.

17          4.        Whether Plaintiffs Musk and Zilis have met their burden of establishing an

18   entitlement to a preliminary injunction on their self-dealing claim under Cal. Corp. Code § 5233

19   where (a) they have no standing to bring this claim under Cal. Corp. Code § 5233(c); (b) they make

20   no showing of self-dealing; and (c) they make no showing of irreparable harm.

21          5.        Whether Plaintiffs’ request for preliminary injunctive relief should be denied for the

22   additional, independent reasons that the balance of equities and the public interest each weighs

23   against issuing a preliminary injunction in this case.

 2          Elon Musk moves this Court to deploy its equitable power to benefit his competitive

 3   position in the development of artificial general intelligence. The motion fails. Since early 2024,

 4   Musk has filed three vexatious complaints against OpenAI and its leadership, piling far-fetched

 5   legal claims on top of false allegations, but never seeking injunctive relief with the dispatch equity

 6   requires. Musk adduces no evidence to support this late-breaking request for equitable relief. He

 7   claims he will suffer irreparable harm absent judicial intervention, but provides no plausible

 8   account of how or why. Musk doesn’t even have standing to sue. And the sweeping relief he seeks

 9   would debilitate OpenAI’s business, board deliberations, and mission to create safe and beneficial

10   AI—all to the advantage of Musk and his own AI company. The motion should be denied:

11          (1)     Invoking the Sherman Act, Musk seeks an injunction against a supposed agreement

12   to boycott investment in xAI, Musk’s competing AI business. No facts support the existence of this

15   “major investor” is not identified anywhere in Musk’s submissions, and there is no evidence—or

16   even an allegation—that the mystery investor’s decision resulted from a boycott agreement

17   involving OpenAI. That’s no surprise, because no such agreement exists—as evidenced by the fact

18   that xAI has just raised nearly $6 billion in record time.

22   Microsoft and OpenAI had competed long enough for the Clayton Act’s prohibition to kick in. The

 1   B.   Musk’s state court suit

 7           To have Article III standing, an antitrust plaintiff must have suffered an “injury in fact that

 8   is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent,” City of Oakland v. Oakland Raiders, 20 F.4th

 9   441, 452 (9th Cir. 2021), and that “bears a causal connection to the alleged antitrust violation,”

10   Gerlinger v. Amazon.com Inc., 526 F.3d 1253, 1255-56 (9th Cir. 2008). Clearing this bar in the

11   antitrust context requires, at minimum, a “substantial probability” that plaintiff suffered damages

12   caused by the defendant’s anticompetitive conduct, premised on a theory that is neither

13   “counterintuitive” nor “highly attenuated.” Oakland Raiders, 20 F.4th at 453.

14           To ensure antitrust litigants do not abuse the antitrust laws for competitive advantage, courts

15   also insist that antitrust plaintiffs demonstrate “antitrust standing.” Id. at 455. In assessing antitrust

16   standing, courts consider “(1) the nature of the plaintiff’s alleged injury; that is, whether it was the

17   type the antitrust laws were intended to forestall; (2) the directness of the injury; [and] (3) the

18   speculative measure of the harm.” Id. at 452. Damages are not “direct” for purposes of this inquiry

19   unless there is a tight “chain of causation between the plaintiff’s injury and the alleged restraint of

20   trade.” Id. at 458-60 (finding alleged harm from Section 1 violation was insufficiently direct where

21   “there [was] no way of knowing [] what would have occurred” absent the alleged restraint (cleaned

22   up)). Nor can “only speculative” damages support antitrust standing. Id. at 460; see also Eagle v.

23   Star-Kist Foods, Inc., 812 F.2d 538, 542 (9th Cir. 1987).

20   company stemming from the transactions they identify. See, e.g., Taylor v. Biglari, 971 F. Supp.

21   2d 847, 854 (S.D. Ind. 2013). As noted above, the purported “self-dealing” transactions appear to

22   have benefited OpenAI, see ¶¶ 137, 139-44, and there is nothing to suggest they were at other than

23   market rates. The FAC’s vague allusion to the threat of “financial and reputational” harms to

24   OpenAI, Mot. at 23, are not backed up either. The only purported “reputational” harm identified is

25   a copyright lawsuit by the New York Times that has already been brought, is being heavily

26   contested, is in no way traceable to any purported “self-dealing,” and would not be remedied by an

27   injunction. See id. So if Plaintiffs had standing (they do not), and if they had any likelihood of

28   success (they do not), their claim would still be entirely remediable in damages.

 1   V. THE BALANCE OF EQUITIES AND THE PUBLIC INTEREST WEIGH AGAINST
        AN INJUNCTION
 2

 3            For all the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs come nowhere near satisfying the first two elements

 4   required for the extraordinary relief of a preliminary injunction. Their showing on the remaining

 5   factors—balance of equities and public interest—is at least as feeble.

 6            On one side of the scale, Musk has been trying to wield the legal process for nearly a year

 7   to gain competitive advantage, based on claims that have no legal support and are grounded only

 8   in conclusions and conjecture. He has identified no harm to himself or xAI should the Court decline

 9   to issue the injunction he seeks—relief that he could have sought months or years ago if any of his

10   purported concerns were legitimate. And the assortment of speculative harms to third parties he

11   alleges do not weigh in the balance. See Stormans, Inc. v. Selecky, 586 F.3d 1109, 1138 (9th Cir.

12   2009).

13            On the other side of the scale, granting Musk’s proposed injunction risks serious harm to

14   OpenAI, an innovative pioneer in a rapidly evolving industry whose core mission is to develop safe

15   and beneficial AGI, and to help others to do so.

16            Plaintiffs argue that the requested injunction is “uniquely important to the public interest”

17   because it will “preserv[e] [] competitive markets in th[e] [generative AI] sector.” Mot. at 24. The

18   opposite is true. Musk has brought this motion to try to restrain the technological leader in the AI

19   field he wants to dominate. His aim is not to level the playing field, but to tilt it in his favor. Courts

20   rightly exercise “extreme caution” when “judicial intervention in a competitive situation [could]

21   itself upset the balance of market force.” California v. Sutter Health Sys., 130 F. Supp. 2d 1109,

22   1137 (N.D. Cal. 2001) (cleaned up); see also Epic Games, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 493 F. Supp. 3d 817,

23   832 (N.D. Cal. 2020) (“[t]he [] legal landscape cautions against preliminarily finding[s]” with

24   respect to allegations of competitive harm). Nowhere is that caution more warranted than here.

25                                              CONCLUSION

26            The Court should deny Plaintiffs’ motion for a preliminary injunction in its entirety.
