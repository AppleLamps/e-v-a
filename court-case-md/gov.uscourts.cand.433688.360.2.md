 1                                            INTRODUCTION
 2           The briefing on this motion shows that plaintiff Elon Musk’s claims all rely on departures
 3    from well-settled legal rules—special exemptions he seeks for himself alone.
 4           Musk seeks to enforce a California charitable trust on the basis of donations made by DAFs.
 5    No case has ever permitted that result. Musk seeks to enforce a California charitable trust on the
 6    basis of general-purpose donations. No case has ever permitted that result. Musk invokes California
 7    law to enforce a charitable trust even though he has no reversionary or other interest in the
 8    donations. No case has ever permitted that result. Musk’s charitable trust claim thus seeks
 9    dispensations that have never been accorded to any other party under California law.
10           Musk’s other claims are similarly deficient. He asserts fraud on the basis of alleged
11    misrepresentations he swore under oath he knew were untrue. He asserts unjust enrichment without
12    bothering to attach the claim to an underlying actionable wrong. He pursues all these claims years
13    after the expiration of the applicable limitations periods. His claims fail on all these grounds.
14           They fail as well under the rules allocating regulatory responsibility over non-profit
15    corporations. Musk claims for himself the authority to enforce the terms of OpenAI’s mission. But
16    in all except the most unusual circumstances, none present here, that authority falls to the Attorney
17    General, not to donors or private citizens. The Attorneys General of both California and Delaware
18    have exercised their authority here, forcefully. As Musk notes, OpenAI completed the
19    recapitalization of its for-profit affiliate on October 28. That recapitalization proceeded following
20    intensive regulatory review and subject to commitments requested by and made to the Attorneys
21    General confirming OpenAI’s continued adherence to its public mission. Exs. 83-86. Musk’s
22    lawsuit has from the beginning sought to usurp the function of the Attorneys General and to advance
23    his own competitive objectives. There is no warrant for that outcome in law, logic, or policy.

 5           A.      Musk lacks Article III standing because he donated only to DAFs and YC.
 6           Musk does not dispute that he made his claimed cash donations to DAFs and YC, rather

11    and YC a “mere intermediary.” Opp. 10-14. These labels don’t change the facts. Musk donated to

12    the DAFs and YC—not to OpenAI. Musk reaped the tax benefits when he donated to the DAFs and

13    YC—not when the DAFs and YC later donated to OpenAI. The ultimate decision whether to donate

14    to OpenAI rested with the DAFs and YC—not Musk. And any rights associated with the donations

15    to OpenAI accrued to the DAFs and YC—not Musk. Br. 6-8; SUF 10-20.

17    That observation only restates a DAF’s defining feature: “a charitable giving vehicle that allows

18    donors to take a present-year income tax deduction, while distributing the funds to charity at a later

19    time.” Pinkert v. Schwab Charitable Fund, 48 F.4th 1051, 1052-53 (9th Cir. 2022). That Musk

20    donated funds to one charity (the DAF) that later donated to a second charity (OpenAI) imbues him

21    with no legal rights as to the DAF’s ultimate donation. To the contrary: Musk’s DAF donation was

22    “tax-deductible only” because once Musk made his donation, the DAF “own[ed] and control[led]

23    the assets that [were] donated.” Id. (quoting 26 U.S.C. § 4966(d)(2)). Equally unavailing is Musk’s

24    observation that the DAFs “never refused to make a contribution to OpenAI” that Musk

25    recommended. Opp. 14. A donor to a DAF lacks standing even where the DAF “followed his advice

26    in the past by donating funds from his DAF to charities he supports.” Pinkert, 48 F.4th at 1056.

 9           Holt aside, Musk lacks common law standing because the Third Restatement would not

12    duties are “severed into intervals.” Lamont v. Time Warner, Inc., 586 F. App’x 357, 357 (9th Cir.

13    2014); see also Honey Baked Ham, Inc. v. Honey Baked Ham Co., 2021 WL 5990164, at *8 (C.D.

14    Cal. Nov. 4, 2021) (continuous accrual requires “a series of severable obligations”). Musk claims

15    OpenAI was required to “be open source and a nonprofit”                 Musk Tr. 54:15-23, 285:19-

16    286:24. There is no evidence these alleged duties were severed into intervals or constituted

17    severable obligations, and Musk does not claim they were.8 E.g., Hamilton v. U.S. Bank Nat’l Ass’n,

18    2022 WL 1736 8815, at *7 (C.D. Cal. Oct. 14, 2022) (deed of trust not “divisible or severable”);

19    Phoenix Techs. Ltd. v. VMware, Inc., 2017 WL 1289863, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 6, 2017) (perpetual

20    license “not severable”). Continuous accrual does not apply and the claim remains time-barred.
