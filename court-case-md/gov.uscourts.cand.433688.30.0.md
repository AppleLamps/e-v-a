12      1. Count I: Promissory Fraud
13          The elements of promissory fraud are “‘(1) a promise made regarding a material fact
14 without any intention of performing it; (2) the existence of the intent not to perform at the time the
15 promise was made; (3) intent to deceive or induce the promisee to enter into a transaction; (4)
16 reasonable reliance by the promisee; (5) nonperformance by the party making the promise; and (6)
17 resulting damage to the promise[e].’” Missakian v. Amusement Indus., Inc., 69 Cal. App. 5th 630,
18 653-54 (2021) (quoting Gruber v. Gruber, 48 Cal.App.5th 529, 540 (2020)); see also Lazar v.
19 Superior Ct., 12 Cal. 4th 631, 638 (1996).
20      2. Count II: Constructive Fraud
21          “‘To state a claim for constructive fraud under California law, a plaintiff must allege: (1) a
22 fiduciary or confidential relationship; (2) an act, omission or concealment involving a breach of
23 that duty; (3) reliance; and (4) resulting damage.’” Fabian v. LeMahieu, No. 19-cv-0054-YGR,
24 2019 WL 4918431, at *14 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 4, 2019) (quoting Sacramento E.D.M., Inc. v. Hynes
25 Aviation Indus., 965 F. Supp. 2d 1141, 1152 (E.D. Cal. 2013), citing Cal. Civ. Code § 1573); see
26 also Prakashpalan v. Engstrom, Lipscomb & Lack, 223 Cal. App. 4th 1105, 1131 (2014).
27      3. Count III: Aiding and Abetting Fraud
28          To state a claim for aiding and abetting fraud, a plaintiff must allege (1) an underlying

 6      4. Count IV: Federal Civil RICO, 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c)

 7          “The elements of a civil RICO claim are: ‘(1) conduct (2) of an enterprise (3) through a
 8 pattern (4) of racketeering activity (known as ‘predicate acts’) (5) causing injury to the plaintiff’s
 9 ‘business or property.’” Cooney v. City of San Diego, No. 4:21-cv-1721-YGR, 2022 WL 1991211,
10 at *3 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2022) (quoting Grimmett v. Brown, 75 F.3d 506, 510 (9th Cir. 1996)).

11          “‘[E]nterprise’ is defined as including ‘any individual, partnership, corporation,
12 association, or other legal entity[.]’” United States v. Turkette, 452 U.S. 576, 580 (1981) (quoting
13 18 U.S.C. § 1961(4)). “[T]o conduct or participate, directly or indirectly, in the conduct of such
14 enterprise’s affairs,” 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c), a defendant must have “participated in the operation or
15 management of the enterprise,” and “not just their own affairs.” Reves v. Ernst & Young, 507 U.S.
16 170, 183, 185 (1993); see also Ferrari v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, No. 15-cv-04379-YGR, 2016
17 WL 7188030, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 12, 2016).
18          “An enterprise is ‘operated’ not just by upper management but also by lower rung
19 participants in the enterprise who are under the direction of upper management. An enterprise also
20 might be ‘operated’ or ‘managed’ by others ‘associated with’ the enterprise who exert control over
21 it as, for example, by bribery.” LD v. United Behav. Health, 508 F. Supp. 3d 583, 602-03 (N.D.
22 Cal. 2020) (quoting Reves, 507 U.S. at 184).
23          A pattern of racketeering activity requires at least two related acts undertaken in violation
24 of enumerated federal statutes, including the wire fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1343. See 18 U.S.C.
25 §§ 1961(5), 1961(1)(B). To state a claim for wire fraud, a plaintiff must allege: “(1) that the
26 defendant formed a scheme to defraud; (2) used the United States wires . . . in furtherance of the
27 scheme; and (3) did so with a specific intent to deceive or defraud.” Pac. Recovery Sols. v. United
28 Behav. Health, 481 F. Supp. 3d 1011, 1028 (N.D. Cal. 2020) (citing Schreiber Distrib. Co. v.

 2 F. Supp. 3d 583, 603 (N.D. Cal. 2020) (same).
 3      5. Count V: Conspiracy to Violate Federal Civil RICO, 18 U.S.C. § 1962(d)

 4          Title 18, Section 1962(d) provides that “[i]t shall be unlawful for any person to conspire to
 5 violate” section 1962(c). A plaintiff must allege either “an agreement which is a substantive
 6 violation of RICO (such as conducting the affairs of an enterprise through a pattern of
 7 racketeering)” or “the defendant’s participation or agreement to participate in two predicate
 8 offenses.” Baumer v. Pachl, 8 F.3d 1341, 1346 (9th Cir. 1993).
 9      6. Count VI: Breach of Express Contract
10          “To state a breach of contract claim, a plaintiff must assert: (1) the existence of a contract;
11 (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for non-performance; (3) breach of the terms of the contract;
12 and (4) that the breach caused harm to the plaintiff.” Smith v. Nationstar Mortg. LLC, No. 18-cv-
13 5612-YGR, 2019 WL 1745121, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 18, 2019) (citing Wall St. Network, Ltd. v.
14 New York Times Co., 164 Cal. App. 4th 1171, 1178 (2008)); see also Verde Media Corp v. Levi,
15 No. 14-cv-00891-YGR, 2015 WL 374934, at *7 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 28, 2015).
16          Contract formation requires “mutual assent (usually accomplished through the medium of
17 an offer and acceptance) and consideration.” Levy v. Only Cremations for Pets, Inc., 57 Cal. App.
18 5th 203, 211 (2020) (quoting Div. of Lab. L. Enf’t v. Transpacific Transp. Co., 69 Cal. App. 3d
19 268, 275 (1977)); Fabian, 2019 WL 4918431, at *10 (same). “Any benefit conferred, or agreed to
20 be conferred, upon the promisor, by any other person, to which the promisor is not lawfully
21 entitled, or any prejudice suffered, or agreed to be suffered, by such person, other than such as he
22 is at the time of consent lawfully bound to suffer, as an inducement to the promisor, is a good
23 consideration for a promise.” Cal. Civ. Code § 1605.

28 conduct (Civ. Code, § 1621).” Div. of Lab. L. Enf’t, 69 Cal. App. 3d at 275. The elements of

 3      8. Count VIII: Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

 4             “A plaintiff asserting a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair
 5 dealing must allege the following elements: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) the plaintiff did all,
 6 or substantially all of the significant things the contract required; (3) the conditions required for
 7 the defendant’s performance had occurred; (4) the defendant unfairly interfered with the plaintiff’s
 8 right to receive the benefits of the contract; and (5) the plaintiff was harmed by the defendant’s
 9 conduct.” Oculus Innovative Scis., Inc. v. Nofil Corp., No. C 06-01686, 2007 WL 2600746, at *4
10 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 10, 2007) (citation omitted).

11      9. Count IX: Breach of Quasi-Contract/Unjust Enrichment
12             To state a claim for breach of quasi-contract (or “unjust enrichment”), a plaintiff must
13 allege “receipt of a benefit and unjust retention of the benefit at the expense of another.”
14 Prakashpalan, 223 Cal. App. 4th at 1132 (quoting Lectrodryer v. SeoulBank, 77 Cal. App. 4th
15 723, 726 (2000)).

 1      11. Count XI: Unfair Competition Under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17200 et seq.

 2          California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”) prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or
 3 fraudulent business act or practice[.]” Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200. “The UCL creates a cause
 4 of action for business practices that are (1) unlawful, (2) unfair, or (3) fraudulent. Each ‘prong’ of
 5 the UCL provides a separate and distinct theory of liability.’” Doe v. Epic Games, Inc., 435 F.
 6 Supp. 3d 1024, 1049 (N.D. Cal. 2020) (citing Lozano v. AT & T Wireless Servs., Inc., 504 F.3d
 7 718, 731 (9th Cir. 2007)). “[A] person asserting an unfair competition claim must allege that (1) he
 8 or she ‘suffered injury in fact,’ and (2) ‘lost money or property as a result of such unfair
 9 competition.’” Lozano, 504 F.3d at 731-32 (quoting Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17204). “To state a
10 claim under the unlawful prong of the UCL, plaintiff may allege the commission of any act

11 ‘forbidden by law, be it civil or criminal, federal, state, or municipal, statutory, regulatory, or
12 court-made.’” Dejong v. Nationstar Mortg. LLC, No. 17-cv-3653-YGR, 2017 WL 3968539, at *5
13 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 7, 2017) (quoting Saunders v. Sup. Ct., 27 Cal. App. 4th 832, 838-39 (1994)).
14 “With respect to the unfair prong, an act or practice is unfair if the practice ‘offends an established
15 public policy or when the practice is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous or substantially
16 injurious to consumers.’” Id. (quoting Lueras v. BAC Home Loans Servicing, L.P., 221 Cal. App.
17 4th 49, 81 (2013)). “With respect to the fraudulent prong, the UCL requires ‘only a showing that
18 members of the public are likely to be deceived’ by the allegedly fraudulent practice.” Id. (quoting
19 Lueras, 221 Cal. App. 4th at 81).
20      12. Count XII: False Advertising Under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17500 et seq.
21
            To state a claim for false advertising under California law, a plaintiff must allege that, with
22
     the “intent directly or indirectly to dispose of real or personal property or to perform services . . .
23
     or to induce the public to enter into any obligation relating thereto,” a defendant “ma[d]e or
24
     disseminate[d] or cause[d] to be made or disseminated . . . in any newspaper or other publication,
25
     or any advertising device . . . or in any other manner or means whatever, including over the
26
     Internet,” a statement “which is untrue or misleading, and which is known, or which by the
27
     exercise of reasonable care should be known, to be untrue or misleading.” Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code
28
     § 17500. “[A] statement is false or misleading if members of the public are likely to be deceived.”

 4      13. Count XIII: Aiding and Abetting Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 5          “‘Liability may . . . be imposed on one who aids and abets the commission of an
 6 intentional tort if the person (a) knows the other’s conduct constitutes a breach of duty and gives
 7 substantial assistance or encouragement to the other to so act or (b) gives substantial assistance to
 8 the other in accomplishing a tortious result and the person’s own conduct, separately considered,
 9 constitutes a breach of duty to the third person.’” Casey, 127 Cal. App. 4th at 1144 (quoting
10 Saunders, 27 Cal. App. 4th at 846).

11          “The elements of a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty are the existence of a
12 fiduciary relationship, its breach, and damage proximately caused by that breach.” Marzec v. Cal.
13 Pub. Emps. Ret. Sys., 236 Cal. App. 4th 889, 915 (2015) (quotation marks and citations omitted).
14      14. Count XIV: Tortious Interference with Contract
15          “The elements necessary to state a cause of action for intentional interference with
16 contractual relations are ‘(1) a valid contract between plaintiff and a third party; (2) defendant’s
17 knowledge of this contract; (3) defendant’s intentional acts designed to induce a breach or
18 disruption of the contractual relationship; (4) actual breach or disruption of the contractual
19 relationship; and (5) resulting damage.’” Mintz v. Blue Cross of Cal., 172 Cal. App. 4th 1594,
20 1603 (2009) (quoting Pac. Gas & Elec. Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co., 50 Cal. 3d 1118, 1126 (1990)).
21      15. Count XV: Declaratory Relief
22          “Pursuant to the Declaratory Judgment Act, ‘[i]n a case of actual controversy,’ the Court
23 ‘may declare the rights and other legal relations of any interested party seeking such declaration,
24 whether or not further relief is or could be sought.’” Epic Games, Inc., 435 F. Supp. 3d at 1044
25 (quoting 28 U.S.C. § 2201(a)). “To determine whether a declaratory judgment action presents a
26 justiciable case or controversy, courts consider ‘whether the facts alleged, under all the
27 circumstances, show that there is a substantial controversy, between parties having adverse legal
28 interests, of sufficient immediacy and reality to warrant the issuance of a declaratory judgment.’”
