 1    I.    INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY

 2           Musk agrees that to survive summary judgment, he must show (a) on his aiding-and-

 3    abetting claim that Microsoft actually knew that OpenAI, Altman, or Brockman owed, and

 4    breached, fiduciary duties to donors and that Microsoft intended to take actions to aid and abet the

 5    breach, and (b) on his unjust enrichment claim that Microsoft knowingly and “wrongfully”

 6    retained a benefit. And because Musk brought this case claiming the breach of specific duties

 7    owed to him, he must also show that Microsoft actually knew of a breach of specific duties owed

 8    to Musk, and that Microsoft knowingly and wrongfully retained a benefit at Musk’s expense.

 9           Unable to point to any such evidence, Musk asks the Court to make several unsupported
10    leaps. First, Musk seeks to infer actual knowledge from an email written by Microsoft’s Chief

11    Technology Officer (the “Scott Email”) that says nothing about duties owed to any donor, let alone

12    to Musk, as well as the mere fact that Microsoft was aware of OpenAI’s nonprofit mission (which

13    again is silent about duties owed to Musk or anyone else). Second, Musk seeks to infer intent

14    because Microsoft entered commercial transactions with OpenAI’s for-profit subsidiary, which

15    Musk admits the OpenAI nonprofit Board, including former co-Plaintiff Zilis, approved. And

16    third, Musk seeks to establish unjust enrichment simply because Microsoft benefited from its

17    commercial relationship with that for-profit subsidiary. He asks the Court to make these leaps

18    even though he acknowledges that Microsoft contributed $14 billion to OpenAI, which has helped

19    to fund one of the largest nonprofits in the world. Microsoft’s contribution was necessary for
20    OpenAI to pursue its mission and vastly exceeded Musk’s alleged $38 million financial donation.

21    But as explained below, none of these raises an issue of material fact on essential elements of

22    Musk’s claims or comes close to justifying a trial against Microsoft.

26    Microsoft about any duties owed to Musk or their breach. Given that undeniable fact, Musk

27    retreats to arguing about the importance of circumstantial evidence, but he then fails to offer any

28    circumstantial evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer that Microsoft had actual

 3           No Circumstantial Evidence. In the absence of direct evidence, a plaintiff may rely on

 4    substantial circumstantial evidence, but it must still be evidence of actual knowledge. AngioScore,

 5    70 F. Supp. 3d at 957. Martinez v. Bank of Am. Nat’l Tr. & Sav. Ass’n, 82 Cal. App. 4th 883, 891

 6    (2000), which is not an aiding-and-abetting case, does not alter the applicable standard. While the

 7    court held that circumstantial evidence that the bank “must have known” certain information

 8    sufficed, id. at 891 (emphasis in original), the court also found that a suggestion it “should have

 9    known” did not. Id. at 891 n.2. Thus, under Martinez, “must have known” is just “circumstantial
10    evidence” raising an “inference” of “actual knowledge.” Id.

13    and others after Altman first told Scott that OpenAI planned to launch a commercial venture to

15                  3.      Constructive Knowledge Does Not Suffice, and There Is No Evidence

17            Musk agrees that aiding-and-abetting liability requires actual knowledge of duty and

18    breach, but he fundamentally relies on constructive knowledge. That, the law does not allow.

19            Where constructive knowledge is permitted, it exists where the law attributes or imputes
20    knowledge to a person when, using reasonable care and diligence, that person should have known.

21    In re Columbia Pipeline Grp., Inc. Merger. Litig., 342 A.3d 324, 356 n.194 (Del. June 17, 2025);

22    see also Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm., 589 U.S. at 185 (constructive knowledge is imputed by

23    law). In contrast, actual knowledge is “[r]eal knowledge” that is “clear and direct.” In re Columbia

24    Pipeline Grp., Inc., 342 A.3d at 356 n.194, citing Intel Corp. Inv. 589 U.S. at 184-85 (unread

25    disclosures did not provide “actual” knowledge triggering statute of limitations for ERISA breach

26    of fiduciary duty claim; to have “actual knowledge” of information, one must “in fact be aware of

27    it”).

                    1.      Unjust Enrichment Requires Actual Knowledge and Microsoft Had No
 3                          Reason to Know in any Event

 4           Innocent third parties without knowledge of underlying violations do not owe restitution,

 5    the remedy for unjust enrichment. Mot. at 23. For the reasons discussed, Microsoft had no

 6    knowledge—and no reason to know—about any alleged duties owed to Musk or their breach.

 7    Moreover, the Opposition’s “reason to know” argument does not suffice for unjust enrichment,

 8    and Musk’s three pleading-stage cases do not say otherwise. Pro. Tax Appeal v. Kennedy-Wilson

 9    Holdings, Inc., 29 Cal. App. 5th 230 (2018), involved special rules regarding constructive notice

10    of recorded real property liens. In that narrow context, the court found that plaintiff alleged

11    subsequent owner-defendants knew or had reason to know of plaintiff’s claim against an earlier

12    property owner, id. at 237, 241. But the court specifically noted that it was not ruling the plaintiff

13    could prove knowledge, id. at 241. First Nationwide Sav v. Perry, 11 Cal. App. 4th 1657 (1992),

14    arose in a similar context, and is a case in which the defendant “clearly had notice of the

15    reconveyance” because it received a mail copy. Id. at 1669. The court’s statement that where a

16    defendant “knew or should have known” it can be unjustly enriched (id. at 1671) is dicta because

17    the court also noted that “knowledge is critical” to an unjust enrichment claim and found there

18    was such knowledge. Id. at 1669. And Gen Star Indem. Co. v. First Am. Title Ins. Co. of Napa,

19    2021 WL 916850, *4 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 10, 2021), merely quoted First Nationwide but did not

20    analyze the question of knowledge.

 2                  3.     Under Applicable Federal Common Law, the Unjust Enrichment
                           Claim Fails Where an Adequate Legal Remedy Exists
 3

 4           The equitable unjust enrichment claim also fails under Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp.,

 5    49 F. 4th 1300 (9th Cir. 2022), because Musk had an adequate remedy at law under that the aiding

 6    and abetting claim when he pled it. Musk argues that the Sonner rule applies only to relief sought

 7    under the same claim, Opp. at 22. Not so. Sonner itself dismissed a UCL claim for restitution

 8    because the plaintiff asserted a separate CLRA claim for damages, id. at 1302. Federal courts

 9    routinely dismiss unjust enrichment claims when, as here, a plaintiff brings a claim providing an
10    adequate remedy and a separate unjust enrichment claim, treating the latter as an equitable remedy

11    rather than a standalone cause of action. Stapleton v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., 779 F. Supp.

12    3d 1059, 1076 (N.D. Cal. 2025). For the Sonner rule to apply, the claim elements and proof need

13    not be identical. Pietosi v. HP, Inc., 2025 WL 30365909, *3 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 30, 2025) (different

14    fraud and quasi-contract claim elements).

15           Musk ignores this federal common law and focuses solely on inapposite state law cases.

16    Tanforan v. Tanforan, 173 Cal. 270 (1916) (not addressing Sonner-type adequacy of legal

17    remedy). And Jogani v. Superior Ct., 165 Cal. App. 4th 901, 911 (2008), agreed that unjust

18    enrichment is not a separate cause of action. Here, Musk seeks restitution/disgorgement via a

19    constructive trust, SAC ¶¶ 279, 416, so the restitution he seeks is equitable in nature.7 And he
20    bases the unjust enrichment claim on the same facts, SAC ¶¶ 275, 409, and seeks identical

21    remedies, id. ¶¶ 280, 417, as the aiding-and-abetting claim. As a result, the unjust enrichment

22    claim cannot survive because Musk had an adequate remedy at law.
