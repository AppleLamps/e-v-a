 1                                           INTRODUCTION

 2          The OpenAI Defendants (“OpenAI”) seek to introduce evidence that the California and

 3 Delaware Attorneys General investigated and ultimately did not oppose OpenAI’s October 2025

 4 conversion into a for-profit public benefit corporation. Those decisions are not relevant to this case.

 5 The attorneys general applied different standards that do not govern Musk’s claims. They assumed,

 6 rather than decided, that OpenAI would comply with various representations about how it would

 7 operate. And they relied solely on information provided by OpenAI.

 8          That evidence is also severely prejudicial. It would invite the jury to defer to the state

 9 attorneys general rather than reaching their own conclusions based on the law and evidence at trial.

10 It also fails the standards for admitting a public record over a hearsay objection. The evidence

11 should be excluded under Federal Rules of Evidence 402, 403, and 802.

12                                            BACKGROUND

13          On October 28, 2025, the California and Delaware Attorneys General both issued press

14 releases announcing that they would not object to OpenAI’s proposed conversion into a for-profit

15 public benefit corporation. Ex. 1; Ex. 2. The California Attorney General’s decision was reflected

16 in a memorandum of understanding entered into with OpenAI the previous day. Ex. 3. The

17 Delaware decision was reflected in a formal statement of non-objection. Ex. 4. Both press releases

18 indicate that the attorneys general had been investigating the proposed conversion for over a year.

19 Ex. 1; Ex. 2 at 1.

24 problem-solving that Californians need,” that OpenAI “generates thousands of jobs and over a

25 billion dollars of positive economic impact for California annually,” and that OpenAI “will continue

26 to increase its hiring of employees in the state, expand its office presence in its home state of

27 California, and generate material local and state tax revenue for the state.” Ex. 3 at 1. The California

28 Attorney General declined to object to the conversion in part because OpenAI agreed to “maintain

 9 II.      THE ATTORNEY GENERAL DECISIONS THREATEN UNDUE PREJUDICE

10          The attorney general decisions also threaten jury confusion and other prejudice under Rule

11 403. Government investigations and non-enforcement decisions pose substantial risks of jury

12 confusion because they “invite[ ] the jury to improperly substitute [the government’s] findings . . .

13 for its own.” United States v. Pac. Gas & Elec. Co., 178 F. Supp. 3d 927, 948 (N.D. Cal. 2016).

14 The jury may rely on speculation about “whatever evidence the agency did or did not consider”

15 rather than “the jury’s reasoned decision based on the evidence presented at trial.” BoDeans Cone

16 Co. v. Norse Dairy Sys., L.L.C., 678 F. Supp. 2d 883, 898 (N.D. Iowa 2009). The jury will also

17 likely give “undue weight or deference” to the government’s actions. Sanders v. Univ. of Idaho,

18 Coll. of Law, 634 F. Supp. 3d 923, 932-33 (D. Idaho 2022) (EEOC determination not to continue

19 investigation was of “little probative value” but “create[d] significant risk of undue prejudice”); see

20 also N.W. v. City of Long Beach, No. 14-cv-1569, 2016 WL 9021966, at *3 (C.D. Cal. June 7, 2016)

21 (similar).

22          Those risks are acute here. The attorneys general are the chief law enforcement officers of

23 their states. They are often embroiled in high-profile litigation on behalf of the citizens of those

24 states, including the Californians that will comprise the jury pool.            Even mentioning their

25 investigations or decisions creates a severe risk that the jury will simply defer to their decisions over

26 the jury’s own assessment of the evidence at trial. That is precisely the type of “improper basis that

27 renders evidence unfairly prejudicial.” Pac. Gas & Elec., 178 F. Supp. 3d at 948.

 7 III.     THE ATTORNEY GENERAL DECISIONS ARE HEARSAY

 8          Finally, the attorney general decisions are inadmissible hearsay under Rule 802. Rule 803(8)

 9 provides a hearsay exception for public records that reflect an agency’s “factual findings from a

10 legally authorized investigation.” Fed. R. Evid. 803(8)(A)(iii). But that exception does not apply

11 when the “circumstances indicate a lack of trustworthiness.” Fed. R. Evid. 803(8)(B). OpenAI’s

12 evidence fails those standards on multiple grounds.

13          First, the attorney general decisions are not “factual findings.” The attorneys general relied

14 on OpenAI’s representations about what it would do, not factual findings about what it had done.

15 Ex. 3 at 2-5; Ex. 4 at 2-6. Moreover, the decisions primarily consist of legal conclusions rather than

16 factual findings admissible under Rule 803(8). Ex. 3 at 2-5; Ex. 4 at 2-7.

17          Second, there are plenty of reasons to doubt the trustworthiness of the decisions. The

18 attorneys general – elected officials in both California and Delaware – relied on overtly political

19 factors like promoting job growth for Californians. See, e.g., Ex. 3 at 1; Ex. 1. They also followed

20 an entirely one-sided process, relying solely on information provided by OpenAI (a party with

21 overwhelming incentives to secure its desired result) while denying other parties like Musk any

22 opportunity to participate. Ex. 3 at 6 ¶ 28; Ex. 4 at 6. Those factors are more than sufficient to

23 justify exclusion of the decisions. See, e.g., Sullivan v. Dollar Tree Stores, Inc., 623 F.3d 770, 778

24 (9th Cir. 2010) (affirming exclusion of Department of Labor report because, among other things,

25 “[n]o hearing was held”).

26                                            CONCLUSION

27          The Court should exclude OpenAI’s proposed trial exhibits concerning the California and

28 Delaware Attorney General decisions as well as all related testimony and argument.
