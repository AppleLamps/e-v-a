 1          Pursuant to this Court’s April 21, 2026 Order [Dkt. 487], Microsoft Corporation fully joins

 2   the OpenAI Defendants’ Response [Dkt. 491] to Plaintiff’s Notice Regarding Verified Waiver of

 3   Remedies [Dkt. 484] and Plaintiff’s Notice Regarding Fraud Claims [Dkt. 485] for the reasons

 4   stated in that Response. Microsoft submits this brief separate Response to address an additional,

 5   Microsoft-specific issue raised by Plaintiff’s waiver of remedies.

 6          Mr. Musk now purports to seek an “award … entered in favor of the OpenAI charity

 7   directly” and “irrevocably commits to that remedy if it is available.” Id. at 1. This newly-disclosed

 8   remedy Mr. Musk now irrevocably seeks — disgorgement in favor of the defendant nonprofit —

 9   is, if available at all, the province of the California Attorney General (“AG”) and those who are
10   entitled to sue derivatively on behalf of the nonprofit. Mr. Musk is neither. He expressly sought

11   relator status from the AG to sue on the AG’s behalf, but the AG denied that request, see Dkt. 157

12   (notice of AG’s denial of relator status). And this Court previously found that he lacks derivative

13   standing, see Dkt. 163 at 8.

14          There is yet another problem. Under Musk’s newly disclosed remedy,1 the defendant

15   nonprofit would effectively become the plaintiff-beneficiary of a claim based on transactions that

16   same nonprofit approved as authorized and consistent with the nonprofit’s mission. In other words,

17   Mr. Musk is asking the Court to direct disgorgement to the very entity he claims was a wrongdoer

18   — i.e., the nonprofit that approved the transactions he claims violated duties to him. That would

19   result in an anomalous and untenable situation in which the “wrongdoer … enjoy[s] the fruits of
20   his transgression.” Kendall-Jackson Winery, Ltd. v. Superior Court, 76 Cal. App. 4th 970, 978

21   (1999) (cit. omit.); see generally Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 63

22   (“Equitable Disqualification (Unclean Hands)”) (recovery in restitution “to which an innocent

23   claimant would be entitled may be limited or denied because of the claimant’s inequitable conduct

24

25
     1
26     By belatedly disclosing his new remedy (disgorgement to the OpenAI nonprofit), Mr. Musk also
     deprived Microsoft of the ability to prepare its expert, Dr. Arnold, to affirmatively value benefits
27   to the nonprofit from Microsoft’s collaboration and opine on whether they offset the purportedly
     wrongful gains that Mr. Musk now seeks to direct to the nonprofit. This prejudice is yet another
28   reason why Mr. Musk’s eve-of-trial disgorgement remedy should be rejected.

 1   in the transaction that is the source of the asserted liability.”). Not surprisingly, that is a result that

 2   courts sitting in equity reject.

 3           In fact, courts routinely apply basic equitable principles to deny recovery to a party in the

 4   posture Mr. Musk now proposes. For instance, in City of Long Beach v. Mansell, 3 Cal. 3d 462,

 5   488 (1970), the California Supreme Court held that “[t]he vital principle is that he who by his

 6   language or conduct leads another to do what he would not otherwise have done shall not subject

 7   such person to loss or injury by disappointing the expectations upon which he acted. Such a change

 8   of position is sternly forbidden.” (cit. omit.). See also Precision Instrument Mfg. Co. v. Automotive

 9   Maint. Mach. Co., 324 U.S. 806, 814 (1945) (the doctrine that “he who comes into equity must
10   come with clean hands” “is a self-imposed ordinance that closes the doors of a court of equity to

11   one tainted with inequitableness or bad faith relative to the matter in which he seeks relief … [and]

12   is rooted in the historical concept of [a] court of equity as a vehicle for affirmatively enforcing the

13   requirements of conscience and good faith [which] presupposes a refusal on its part to be ‘the

14   abetter of iniquity’”) (cit. omit.). Equity does not permit what Musk seeks to do here, and the NP

15   is simply not an appropriate recipient of disgorgement funded by Microsoft’s alleged gains.

16           For the above reasons, regardless of the ultimate liability determination, the Court should

17   reject Musk’s effort to direct disgorgement from Microsoft to the defendant nonprofit — which is

18   now the only monetary relief that Mr. Musk has “irrevocably commit[ted]” to seeking.
