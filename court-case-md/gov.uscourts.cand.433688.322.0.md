                   27   in the California Constitution “extends to one’s confidential financial affairs as well as the details
                   28   of one’s personal life.”). When there is an attempt to invade this right, courts must engage in

                    8           Here, the balance tips squarely in Mr. Sutskever’s favor. His financial information is not
                    9   necessary to prove any of Plaintiffs’ claims. They seek this information solely to argue Mr.
                   10   Sutskever—a witness whose testimony they subpoenaed—is self-interested and biased. See
                   11   Plaintiffs’ Sealed Ex. 1 (“Letter Brief”), Dkt. No. 300-2 at 3. But they have already elicited

                   14   has compared to that of other executives. Sutskever Dep. Tr. 349:22–25; 351:20–352:13; 351:7–

                   16   interest in the outcome of these proceedings, which is beyond doubt from the admissions they
                   17   already obtained in deposition. To protect his constitutional privacy privilege, Mr. Sutskever was
                   18   instructed not to answer only a narrow additional question—the precise value of his interest. Id.
                   19   351:3–6. It is exactly this type of balancing that the case law contemplates. See Buchholtz, 2013
                   20   WL 1694830, at *6 (finding the right to privacy protected the disclosure of financial and retirement
                   21   information where the Plaintiff had already elicited testimony about the non-party’s general intent
                   22   to retire).

                   27   of a nonparty to show bias where “relevant, admissible, and potentially persuasive evidence [could]
                   28   be obtained without intruding on the nonparty’s privacy interests.”). And their cited authority is
