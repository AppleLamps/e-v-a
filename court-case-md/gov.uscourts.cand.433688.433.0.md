 1 7.       WHAT IS NOT EVIDENCE

 2          In reaching your verdict, you may consider only the testimony and exhibits received into

 3 evidence. Certain things are not evidence, and you may not consider them in deciding what the

 4 facts are. I will list them for you:

 5              1. Arguments and statements by lawyers are not evidence.           The lawyers are not

 6          witnesses. What they say in their opening statements, closing arguments and at other times

 7          is intended to help you interpret the evidence, but it is not evidence. If the facts as you

 8          remember them differ from the way the lawyers have stated them, your memory of them

 9          controls.
10              2. Questions and objections by lawyers are not evidence. Attorneys have a duty to their
11          clients to object when they believe a question is improper under the rules of evidence. You
12          should not be influenced by the objection or by the court’s ruling on it.
13              3. Testimony that is excluded or stricken, or that you have been instructed to disregard,
14          is not evidence and must not be considered.
15              4. Anything you may have seen or heard when the court was not in session is not
16          evidence. You are to decide the case solely on the evidence received at the trial.

 1 9.       DIRECT AND CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

 2          Evidence may be direct or circumstantial. Direct evidence is direct proof of a fact, such as

 3 testimony by a witness about what that witness personally saw or heard or did. Circumstantial

 4 evidence is proof of one or more facts from which you could find another fact. By way of example,

 5 if you wake up in the morning and see that the sidewalk is wet, you may find from that fact that it

 6 rained during the night. However, other evidence, such as a turned-on garden hose, may provide a

 7 different explanation for the presence of water on the sidewalk. Therefore, before you decide that

 8 a fact has been proved by circumstantial evidence, you must consider all the evidence in the light of

 9 reason, experience, and common sense.
10          You should consider both kinds of evidence. The law makes no distinction between the
11 weight to be given to either direct or circumstantial evidence. It is for you to decide how much

12 weight to give to any evidence.

13

 1 10.     RULING ON OBJECTIONS

 2         There are rules of evidence that control what can be received into evidence. When a lawyer

 3 asked a question or offered an exhibit into evidence and a lawyer on the other side thought that it

 4 was not permitted by the rules of evidence, that lawyer may have objected.

 5         If I overruled the objection, the question could be answered, or the exhibit was received. If

 6 I sustained the objection, the question could not be answered, or the exhibit was not received. If I

 7 sustained an objection to a question, you must ignore the question and must not guess what the

 8 answer might have been.

 9         I may have ordered that evidence be stricken from the record and that you disregard or ignore
10 that evidence. That means when you are deciding the case, you must not consider the stricken

11 evidence for any purpose.

 8 law. See Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51 cmts. f & h (2011); Am. Master Lease LLC v.

 9 Idanta Partners, Ltd., 225 Cal. App. 4th 1451, 1491-92 (2014) (“the trial court should instruct the
10 jury that it should give defendants credit for money expended in acquiring or preserving the property

11 or in carrying on the business that is the source of the profit subject to disgorgement”). Musk does

12 not object to this portion of the proposed instruction.

13          It is critical that the instruction reflect these requirements, which serve to reinforce “[t]he
14 object of restitution,” “to eliminate the profit from wrongdoing while avoiding, so far as possible,

15 the imposition of a penalty.” Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 51(4) (2011).

 1 47.       PUNITIVE DAMAGES

 2           If you decide that a Defendant is liable for [breach of charitable trust, fraud, constructive

 3 fraud, and/or aiding and abetting breach of charitable trust], you must decide whether that conduct

 4 justifies an award of punitive damages. The purposes of punitive damages are to punish a wrongdoer

 5 for the conduct that harmed the plaintiff and to discourage similar conduct in the future.

 6           You may award punitive damages against an Individual Defendant (Altman or Brockman)

 7 only if Musk proves by clear and convincing evidence that the Individual Defendant engaged in that

 8 conduct with malice, oppression, or fraud.

 9           You may award punitive damages against an Entity Defendant (OpenAI, Inc. or Microsoft)

10 only if Musk proves that that Entity Defendant acted with malice, oppression, or fraud. To do this,

11 Musk must prove one of the following by clear and convincing evidence:

12                1. that the malice, oppression, or fraud was conduct of one or more officers, directors,
             or managing agents of the Entity Defendant, who acted on behalf of the Entity Defendant;
13
             or
14
                  2. that the conduct constituting malice, oppression, or fraud was authorized by one or
15
             more officers, directors, or managing agents of the Entity Defendant; or
16
                  3. that one or more officers, directors, or managing agents of the Entity Defendant knew
17
             of the conduct constituting malice, oppression, or fraud and adopted or approved that
18
             conduct after it occurred.
19
             “Malice” means that a defendant acted with intent to cause injury or that a defendant’s
20
      conduct was despicable and was done with a willful and knowing disregard of the rights or safety
21
      of another. A defendant acts with knowing disregard when the defendant is aware of the probable
22
      dangerous consequences of the defendant’s conduct and deliberately fails to avoid those
23
      consequences.
24
             “Oppression” means that a defendant’s conduct was despicable and subjected Musk to cruel
25
      and unjust hardship in knowing disregard of his rights.
26
             “Despicable conduct” is conduct that is so vile, base, or contemptible that it would be looked
27
      down on and despised by reasonable people.
