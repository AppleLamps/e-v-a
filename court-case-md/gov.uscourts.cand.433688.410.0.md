 1          The OpenAI Defendants move to exclude Plaintiff’s Trial Exhibits (“PX”) 324, 362, and
 2   363 (Exs. A-C of this motion), and to preclude testimony concerning the same. These exhibits are
 3   third party news articles and social media posts relating to (1) the removal and reinstatement of
 4   Sam Altman as CEO of OpenAI, and (2) OpenAI’s safety practices. They are hearsay barred by
 5   Federal Rule of Evidence 802.
 6                                            BACKGROUND
 7          Plaintiff Elon Musk’s claim rests on the (false) contention that OpenAI, Altman, and Greg
 8   Brockman made two “fundamental promises” to him a decade ago: that OpenAI would be “open-
 9   source” and “nonprofit.” Ex. D (Musk Tr. 286:17-24). Plaintiff claims these supposed promises
10   were “broken” when OpenAI purportedly transformed into a “closed source for-profit.” Id. Musk
11   testified that the “boiling point” for him was reached in January 2023 when OpenAI’s nonprofit
12   board of directors approved a $10 billion investment from Microsoft, which Musk says revealed to
13   him that OpenAI wasn’t what he thought it was. Id. 287:12-14. The directors who approved that
14   transaction—confident they were acting in accordance with their fiduciary duties to the nonprofit
15   and its mission—included Tasha McCauley, Helen Toner, and Shivon Zilis. Ex. E (McCauley Tr.
16   195:25-196:10); Ex. F (Zilis Tr. 264:15-18).
17          Alongside these claims, however, Musk appears intent on introducing evidence on collateral
18   matters with little or no relevance to the issues to be tried. Musk plans to introduce evidence about
19   certain now-departed directors’ decision to remove (and immediately reinstate) Altman as CEO in
20   November 2023, nearly a year after those same directors approved the Microsoft deal, and for
21   purported reasons having nothing to do with that transaction. Musk also appears determined to paint
22   artificial intelligence as a whole as a dangerous technology that is likely to harm jurors and their
23   families. See MIL # 1.
24          To prop up these irrelevant collateral issues, Musk proposes to put in front of the jury news

26         Ex. A (PX 324) is an anonymously sourced Wall Street Journal article published March 28,
27   2025 that purports to recount the events surrounding Mr. Altman’s dismissal and reinstatement as

10                                               ARGUMENT
11          Hearsay is a statement “the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or
12   hearing,” and that “a party offers in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the
13   statement.” Fed. R. Evid. 801(c). As this Court has recognized, “newspaper articles are classic
14   hearsay and, in a court of law, cannot be relied upon for the truth of statements made therein.” Musk
15   v. OpenAI, Inc., 769 F. Supp. 3d 1017, 1026 (N.D. Cal. 2025). That is precisely what Musk wishes
16   to do with Exs. A-C. All are inadmissible under Rule 802.
17          Hearsay related to Altman’s removal and reinstatement should be excluded. The 2025
18   Wall Street Journal article concerning Altman’s removal and reinstatement (Ex. A) is hearsay and
19   should be excluded. An excerpt from the writer’s book, the article relies on “dozens” of anonymous
20   sources to sensationalize events that occurred sixteen months before the article was published.
21   Toner’s and McCauley’s after-the-fact recounting of their perspectives on these same events in the
22   Economist (Ex. B)—published long after Toner and McCauley had resigned from OpenAI’s
23   board—is likewise patent hearsay; an out-of-court statement offered to establish the truth of the
24   facts it describes. Fed. R. Evid. 801(c).
25          Musk now seeks to backdoor this hearsay into the trial record through his deposition
26   designations. During McCauley’s and Toner’s depositions, Musk’s counsel introduced both articles
27   as exhibits, then incorporated the substance of the articles into leading questions, asking the
28   witnesses to confirm whether the portions read were accurate, then using the articles to guide the

 6   a springboard for further leading questions that incorporated the substance of the hearsay. See Ex.
 7   G at 155:5-157:14.
 8          None of this permissible. As the Ninth Circuit has emphasized, “a line of questioning that
 9   repeatedly incorporates inadmissible evidence can be just as improper as the direct admission of
10   such evidence.” United States v. Sine, 493 F.3d 1021, 1031–32 (9th Cir. 2007) (collecting
11   authorities and rejecting “suggestive shortcut” of introducing hearsay through witness questioning);
12   United States v. Rusnak, 981 F.3d 697, 707 (9th Cir. 2020) (improper to introduce “hearsay
13   statements” on direct “in the guise of speaking questions”); Thomas v. Stephens-Lyman, Denny,
14   Caetana, & Turner, 2007 WL 2669497, at *5 (E.D. Cal. Sept. 7, 2007) (striking deposition
15   questions and testimony that “contain hearsay”); United States v. Check, 582 F.2d 668, 678 (2d
16   Cir. 1978) (rejecting questioning and testimony that served “as a transparent conduit for the
17   introduction of inadmissible hearsay”). Questions and answers from the McCauley and Toner
18   depositions premised on the inadmissible hearsay in Exs. E and G to this motion should be stricken.
19   Pursuant to the Court’s pre-trial procedures, additional examples of objectionable questions and
20   answers from these depositions will be submitted to the Court in the parties’ forthcoming joint
21   submission of disputes over designations and excerpts from discovery material.
22          Hearsay statements of former OpenAI employee should be excluded. Social media posts

26   seeks to rely on out-of-court statements to prove the truth of matters collateral to the issues to be
27   tried—to present unfounded criticism of OpenAI’s approach to AI risks in 2024, nearly a decade
