               The Defendants propose that the jury first be given a verdict form addressing the statute of
 5
      limitations defenses. This approach is consistent with the Court’s ruling to have the jury first decide
 6
      the application of the statute of limitations to each claim.
 7
               Plaintiff objects to having the jury address the statute of limitations defenses before
 8
      addressing liability. But courts “routinely” adopt that sequence by, for example, bifurcating trials
 9
      so that statute of limitations questions are tried first. Grisham v. Philip Morris, Inc., 2009 WL
10
      9102320, at *4 (C.D. Cal. Dec. 3, 2009); see, e.g., Antonick v. Elec. Arts, Inc., 841 F.3d 1062, 1065
11
      (9th Cir. 2016) (jury trial bifurcated to address statute of limitations first). Here, the Court is taking
12
      a more modest step, short of having a separate trial on the statute of limitations, by directing the
13
      jury to address at the outset whether Musk’s claims are time-barred before turning to liability
14
      questions if they find any claim is timely. That decision is well within the Court’s wide discretion
15
      to fashion and sequence verdict forms.
16
               Plaintiff insists that the applicability of the continuous accrual doctrine in this case means
17
      that the jury will be confused if it addresses the statute of limitations before considering liability.
18
      That argument is based on a false premise, as the continuous accrual doctrine is inapplicable here.
19
      Joint Pretrial Conference Statement (Disputed Legal Issues Pt. 5). In any event, Plaintiff offers no
20
      reason why juries are capable of addressing other timeliness issues at the threshold, such as the
21
      discovery rule and reasonable diligence, but not continuous accrual.
22
               Thus, the Court should adhere to its determination to have the jury first decide the
23
      application of the statute of limitations to each claim, and use the Defendants’ proposed verdict
24
      form.

 1           The laches question is based on the laches verdict form in S.F. Bay Area Rapid Transit Dist.
 2    v. Spencer, No. 3:04-cv-04632-SI (N.D. Cal.), and the Defendants’ proposed instruction for the
 3    laches defense. The unclean hands question is based on the unclean hands verdict form in Zhu v.
 4    Li, No. 4:19-cv-02534-JSW (N.D. Cal.), and the Defendants’ proposed instruction for the unclean
 5    hands defense.
 6           Musk objects to the laches question on the ground that his claims are legal rather than
 7    equitable. But Musk’s claims in this case are equitable, not legal. The primary remedy Musk seeks,
 8    disgorgement of profits, is a quintessentially equitable remedy. See Joint Pretrial Conference
 9    Statement (Disputed Legal Issues Pt. 1). The only other monetary remedy Musk seeks is an award
10    of exemplary damages, which is likewise equitable because, in the absence of compensatory
11    damages, it necessarily rests on disgorgement of profits. Id. Pt. 3. Because Musk seeks only
12    equitable remedies in this case, his claims are equitable. Furthermore, breach of charitable trust is
13    an equitable cause of action, and unjust enrichment is an equitable theory. Id. Thus, the laches
14    question should be asked.
15           Musk objects to the unclean hands question with respect to his breach of charitable trust
16    and aiding and abetting claims on the ground that the defense may not be used to “wholly defeat a
17    claim based on a public policy expressed by the Legislature in a statute.” Salas v. Sierra Chem.
18    Co., 59 Cal. 4th 407, 432 (2014). But Salas concerned a statutory cause of action, for violation of
19    the Fair Employment and Housing Act. Id. at 416-17. Musk’s charitable trust and aiding and
20    abetting claims are not for violation of a statute, but for breach of common law trust duties or aiding
21    and abetting the same. That Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17510.8 may provide one way to show the
22    creation of a charitable trust does not change that result, particularly given that the provision is by
23    its own terms “declarative of existing trust law principles.” In any event, the unclean hands question
24    should be asked because, as Musk concedes, it is a valid defense to his other claims, and even under
25    Salas the unclean hands defense “may guide the court in fashioning relief,” including by reducing
26    damages. 59 Cal. 4th at 432.

 1    OBJECTIONS TO PLAINTIFF’S PROPOSED VERDICT FORMS
 2           The OpenAI Defendants object to Musk’s proposed liability stage verdict form on several
 3    grounds in addition to the statute of limitations issues identified above.
 4           First, Musk’s proposed liability question for the breach of charitable trust claim erroneously
 5    invokes the preponderance standard. As set forth in the OpenAI Defendants’ proposed instructions
 6    for the breach of charitable trust claim, the “clear and convincing evidence” standard applies to a
 7    portion of the claim.
 8           Second, Musk’s proposed form fails to ask any question regarding the laches affirmative
 9    defense.
10           Third, Musk’s proposed question regarding the unclean hands affirmative defense
11    erroneously invokes the clear and convincing evidence standard. Under California law, the unclean
12    hands defense need only be proved by a preponderance of the evidence. Mattco Forge, Inc. v.
13    Arthur Young & Co., 52 Cal. App. 4th 820, 846 (1997) (affirming unclean hands jury instruction
14    requiring proof by a preponderance of the evidence); Ajaxo, Inc. v. E*Trade Fin. Corp., 48 Cal.
15    App. 5th 129, 171-72 (2020) (noting use of preponderance standard for unclean hands defense in
16    prior trial). Musk erroneously relies on the clear-and-convincing evidence standard applied to the
17    unclean hands defense in Lanham Act cases. Pinkette Clothing, Inc. v. Cosmetic Warriors Ltd., 894
18    F.3d 1015, 1018, 1029 (9th Cir. 2018) (Lanham Act trademark litigation); see Vineyard House,
19    LLC v. Constellation Brands U.S. Operations, Inc., 515 F. Supp. 3d 1061, 1078 (N.D. Cal. 2021)
20    (Gonzalez Rogers, J.) (“A party must demonstrate unclean hands in a trademark action by clear,
21    convincing evidence.” (emphasis added)).
22           Microsoft joins these objections relating to the statute of limitations issues and the laches
23    and unclean hands defenses.

 1           The typical practice is to ask juries just two questions about disgorgement: whether the
 2    plaintiff has proved entitlement to disgorgement and, if so, the amount. Rodriguez v. Google, LLC,
 3    No. 3:20-cv-04688-RS, Dkt. 670 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 4, 2025) (“Have Plaintiffs proved, by a
 4    preponderance of the evidence, that they are entitled to disgorgement …”; “[I]dentify the amount
 5    of unjust enrichment damages you award each class on the following lines.”); Guardant Health,
 6    Inc. v. Natera, Inc., No. 3:21-cv-04062-EMC, Dkt. 736-1 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 25, 2024) (“Should
 7    Natera disgorge profits in addition to damages … for its false advertising?”; “What sum of money,
 8    if any, fairly and reasonably represents Natera’s profits from the actions you found in your answers
 9    to Question 4?”); EchoSpan, Inc. v. Medallia, Inc., No. 5:22-cv-01732-NC, Dkt. 385 (N.D. Cal.
10    Oct. 30, 2023) (“Did Medallia’s acquisition or use of this information cause Medallia to be unjustly
11    enriched?”; “[P]lease add the total amount of unjust enrichment you found Medallia received
12    through improper acquisition or use of EchoSpan’s trade secret(s).”).
13           The Defendants’ proposed verdict form is consistent with standard practice. It asks first:
14    “Did Musk prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he is entitled to disgorgement of profits
15    from any of these Defendants?” If the answer is yes, the form then asks: “What amount of
16    disgorgement of profits, if any, do you award Musk from these Defendants?” These questions are
17    simple, balanced, and supported by precedent.
18           Musk proposes to depart from this practice and instead ask the jury eight detailed questions
19    on disgorgement based on the methodology of Musk’s damages expert—a methodology that
20    Defendants vigorously dispute. For example, Musk would ask: “What is OpenAI For-Profit’s
21    current value?”; “What percentage of OpenAI For-Profit’s value is attributable to OpenAI
22    Nonprofit?”; “What percentage of OpenAI Nonprofit’s value is attributable to Musk’s monetary
23    and non-monetary contributions?” These questions presume the validity of, and closely track, the
24    methodology of Musk’s damages expert, C. Paul Wazzan, who “calculates the amount of OpenAI’s
25    wrongful gains as the product of three numbers: (1) the current value of the OpenAI for-profit
26    entity, times (2) the OpenAI nonprofit’s share of the for-profit, times (3) the portion of the
27    nonprofit’s value that is fairly attributable to Mr. Musk’s monetary and nonmonetary
