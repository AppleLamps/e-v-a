14                                            BACKGROUND

15          In 2015, Sam Altman, Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever, and Elon Musk launched OpenAI as

16 a laboratory dedicated to developing safe and beneficial artificial general intelligence (“AGI”) for
17 the public good. Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 80-88, 91. Musk made substantial donations to OpenAI, Inc. from
18 2016 until September 2020, totaling over $44 million. Id. ¶ 94. In March 2023, Musk created X.AI
19 Corp. (“xAI”), which now competes with OpenAI in the generative AI market. Id. ¶¶ 10, 224-25.
20          Musk filed this federal action on August 5, 2024. Dkt. 1. On November 14, 2024, he filed

21 the FAC, including antitrust claims, and added as defendants Microsoft, Reid Hoffman, and
22 Deannah Templeton. Dkt. 32. After granting in part and denying in part motions to dismiss filed by
23 OpenAI and Microsoft, Dkt. 163, the Court granted Plaintiffs leave to amend Count III (breach of
24 the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing), Count XX (RICO), and Count XXI (RICO
25 conspiracy). Id. at 10-11. OpenAI has now filed a renewed motion to dismiss addressed to Counts
26 III, XX, and XXI. Dkt. 175.

 1                                           LEGAL STANDARD

 2          “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted

 3 as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678

 4 (2009) (quoting Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). The Court must accept
 5 all well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draw reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff.
 6 See id. “Dismissal for failure to state a claim is proper only where there is no cognizable legal
 7 theory or an absence of sufficient facts alleged to support a cognizable legal theory.” Shroyer v.
 8 New Cingular Wireless Servs., Inc., 622 F.3d 1035, 1041 (9th Cir. 2010) (internal quotation marks
 9 omitted). “[W]here a complaint includes allegations of fraud, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b)
10 requires more specificity including an account of the time, place, and specific content of the false
11 representations as well as the identities of the parties to the misrepresentations.” Swartz v. KPMG
12 LLP, 476 F.3d 756, 764 (9th Cir. 2007) (internal citations omitted).
13                                                 ANALYSIS

14          After careful consideration of the parties’ arguments and the allegations in the SAC, the

15 Court finds that Plaintiffs have stated plausible claims against the Defendants sufficient to survive a
16 motion to dismiss.
17          I.      Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing (Count III)

18          The SAC states a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

19 Every contract contains an implied covenant requiring parties to act in good faith and not to
20 frustrate the contract’s benefits. Carma Devs. (Cal.), Inc. v. Marathon Dev. Cal., Inc., 2 Cal. 4th
21 342, 372 (1992). Contrary to Defendants’ assertion, this claim is not duplicative because it focuses
22 on Defendants’ abuse of discretion in performing the contract, rather than technical breaches of its
23 express terms.
24          Specifically, the SAC alleges Defendants exercised in bad faith any discretion under the

25 implied-in-fact agreement Musk alleged by (1) systematically and unilaterally reinterpreting key
26 terms of the agreement so as to undermine the agreement and its objectives; (2) obscuring OpenAI’s
27 for-profit activities through complex corporate structures that violated their fiduciary duties to
28 Musk; and (3) engaging in activities alleged to breach the parties’ agreement that are determined not

 2          II.    RICO (Count XX)

 3          The SAC sufficiently pleads civil RICO violations under 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c). First, the only

 4 defect identified by the Court in its order on OpenAI’s prior motion to dismiss, and therefore the

 5 only proper subject of a renewed motion to dismiss, was Plaintiffs’ failure to allege the conduct of

 6 an enterprise through racketeering activity. Dkt. 163 at 9-11. Nevertheless, for the sake of

 7 comprehensiveness, the Court addresses all of OpenAI’s arguments.

 8                 A.      The SAC Pleads Multiple Predicate Acts with Rule 9(b) Particularity.

 9          The SAC alleges a pattern of wire fraud with the specificity Rule 9(b) requires by alleging

10 the same acts the Court has already deemed sufficient to plead Defendants’ fraudulent conduct in

11 Count VII. Dkt. 163 at 8.

12                 B.      The Predicate Acts Form a Cognizable Pattern.

13          The SAC establishes both relatedness and continuity required for a RICO pattern. H.J. Inc.

14 v. Northwestern Bell Telephone Co., 492 U.S. 229, 239 (1989). The acts satisfy the relatedness

15 criterion because every predicate act served the common purpose of transforming OpenAI from a

16 charity to a for-profit while maintaining a philanthropic façade. Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 307-10, 421, 440. The

17 scheme had common participants (Altman and Brockman), common victims (Musk, other donors,

18 AI scientists, the public), and a common method (fraudulent misrepresentations via interstate

19 wires). Id. ¶¶ 421, 428, 447.

20          The acts satisfy the continuity criterion because they span nearly a decade, from initial

21 fraudulent solicitations in 2015 through ongoing deceptions today. Id. ¶¶ 82-88, 428, 439. Even

22 focusing solely on Musk’s fraudulently induced wire transfers, these span from May 2016 through

23 September 2020—over four years. Id. ¶ 424. The threat of continued racketeering is manifest as

24 Defendants actively work to complete OpenAI’s for-profit conversion. Id. ¶¶ 193, 428.

25                 C.      Defendants Conducted OpenAI, Inc.’s Affairs Through
26                         Racketeering Activity.

27          The SAC specifically alleges how each Defendant participated in conducting OpenAI, Inc.’s

28 affairs through a pattern of racketeering activity, satisfying Reves v. Ernst & Young, 507 U.S. 170,

 1 183 (1993). As CEO and President/CTO, respectively, Altman and Brockman held ultimate

 2 operational authority and used their positions within OpenAI, Inc. to “execute and direct the
 3 enterprise’s core activities through the commission of multiple predicate acts of wire fraud.” Dkt.

 4 170 ¶¶ 109, 114, 149, 445(a). When a charity CEO lies to donors to fund operations for personal
 5 enrichment, he is conducting the enterprise’s affairs through fraud. Reves, 507 U.S. at 179.
 6          The For-Profit Entities served as the vehicles by which Defendants looted OpenAI’s

 7 technology and employed AI scientists recruited through misrepresentations about its charitable
 8 mission. Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 127-30, 433. They “knowingly implemented decisions of” Altman, Brockman,
 9 and Microsoft and were “indispensable to achievement of the enterprise’s goal.” Ketayi v. Health
10 Enrollment Grp., 516 F. Supp. 3d 1092, 1136 (S.D. Cal. 2021).
11          Microsoft exercised operational control through the placement of Deannah Templeton, and

12 facilitation of Reid Hoffman’s service, on OpenAI, Inc.’s Board, the exclusive supply and control of
13 its compute resources giving it veto power over decisions, use of that power to quickly reinstate
14 Altman after his termination, and by directing strategic decisions including OpenAI, Inc.’s effective
15 for-profit conversion, without regulatory approval. Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 110, 146-59, 161, 167, 193-94, 358.
16                 D.      The Enterprise-Person Distinctness Requirement Is Satisfied.

17          Section 1962(c) requires only that the “enterprise” be legally distinct from the “person[s]”

18 conducting its affairs. Cedric Kushner Promotions, Ltd. v. King, 533 U.S. 158, 161 (2001). OpenAI,
19 Inc., as a § 501(c)(3) non-profit corporation, maintains separate legal existence from Altman and
20 Brockman (natural persons), Microsoft (a separate corporation), and the For-Profit Entities (separate
21 legal entities). Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 38, 87, 114-21, 147, 160-65, 223. This satisfies § 1962(c)’s
22 distinctiveness requirement under Cedric Kushner, where the president and sole owner of a
23 corporation was held to could be distinct from his closely-held company. 533 U.S. at 163.
24                 E.      xAI Has Standing to Assert RICO Claims.

25          xAI suffered direct competitive injuries from Defendants’ pattern of wire fraud. The scheme

26 enabled Defendants to monopolize AI talent, engage in predatory pricing, and dominate the
27 market—all through fraudulently obtained funding. Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 198, 220-22, 318(e)-(f), (n), 421.
28 Unlike Anza v. Ideal Steel Supply Corp., 547 U.S. 451 (2006), which involved merely indirect

 5          III.    RICO Conspiracy (Count XXI)

 6          OpenAI does not present argument concerning the conspiracy count other than to state that if

 7 Count XX is dismissed, this count must be as well. Even if the Court were to find the substantive
 8 RICO claim inadequate, however, the conspiracy claim survives. As the Supreme Court has
 9 explained concerning RICO, “It is elementary that a conspiracy may exist and be punished whether
10 or not the substantive crime ensues.” Salinas v. United States, 522 U.S. 52, 65 (1997). As the Ninth
11 Circuit has further explained, “[i]t is the mere agreement to violate RICO that § 1962(d) forbids; it
12 is not necessary to prove any substantive RICO violations ever occurred as a result of the
13 conspiracy.” Oki Semiconductor Co. v. Wells Fargo Bank, 298 F.3d 768, 774-75 (9th Cir. 2002).
14          The SAC alleges OpenAI Defendants agreed to participate in the RICO conspiracy through

15 their: (1) false representations to Musk and other contributors that OpenAI, Inc. would be a non-
16 profit devoted to the open source development of AI/AGI for the public’s benefit, Dkt. 170 ¶ 456;
17 (2) receipt of financial and other contributions, including wired funds, in response to those
18 fraudulent communications, id. ¶ 458; and (3) systematic conduct over multiple years demonstrating
19 specific intent to further their fraudulent scheme. This pattern of coordinated activity establishes the
20 requisite agreement through the OpenAI Defendants’ “words, actions, [and] interdependence of
21 activities and persons involved.” Oki Semiconductor Co., 298 F.3d at 775. By failing to challenge
22 these allegations, Defendants have waived any objection to the conspiracy count’s sufficiency.
23                                              CONCLUSION

24          For the foregoing reasons, OpenAI’s Motion to Dismiss is DENIED.
