                    1          Non-party Ilya Sutskever objects under Rule 72(a) to the Magistrate Judge’s October 17,
                    2   2025 Discovery Order (Dkt. 324).
                    3          The Order compels Mr. Sutskever to reveal granular financial information in violation of
                    4   his constitutional right to privacy without performing the required balancing test, contrary to law.
                    5   It also failed to consider that Mr. Musk’s counsel apparently violated the Protective Order as to the
                    6   exact same type of financial information regarding a senior OpenAI executive.
                    7          Further, the Order compels production of the “Brockman memo,” even though Plaintiffs
                    8   entered into a binding agreement, evidenced by a sworn declaration, that left the Brockman memo
                    9   outside the scope of production. Further, Plaintiffs never requested that memo before Mr.
                   10   Sutskever’s deposition. The Order ignored these facts, accepted an unsworn attorney representation
                   11   from Mr. Musk’s counsel contradicting the facts, ignored the binding production agreement Mr.
                   12   Musk entered into, and ordered both production and a second deposition of a non-party based solely
                   13   on an uninformed view of the perceived relevance of the document.
                   14   I.     The Order erroneously compels disclosure of private financial information.
                   15          When discovery threatens the fundamental right to privacy in financial information, Valley
                   16   Bank of Nev. v. Superior Ct., 15 Cal. 3d 652, 656 (1975), relevance alone is insufficient. Courts
                   17   must conduct a “careful balancing” of the competing interests: the actual need for the specific
                   18   information versus the seriousness of the privacy intrusion. Id. at 657. Critically, this balancing
                   19   must account for non-parties, who deserve “extra protection from the courts.” High Tech Med.
                   20   Instrumentation v. New Image Indus., Inc., 161 F.R.D. 86, 88 (N.D. Cal. 1995); see also U.S. v.
                   21   Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 666 F.2d 364, 371–72 (9th Cir. 1982).
                   22          The Order fails to afford Mr. Sutskever the “extra protection” the law requires for non-
                   23   parties by misapplying the standards governing financial privacy. High Tech Med. Instrumentation,
                   24   161 F.R.D. at 88 (N.D. Cal. 1995). The Court was required to balance actual need, the severity of
                   25   the intrusion (including the risk of disclosure), and the availability of less intrusive means. See Hill
                   26   v. Nat’l Collegiate Athletic Assn., 7 Cal. 4th 1, 37–40 (1994). But the Order skipped the required
                   27   analysis, and concluded that relevance alone justified the intrusion, relying on United States v.
                   28   Harris, 185 F.3d 999, 1008 (9th Cir. 1999). Dkt. 324 at 1. That was legal error. Harris addressed

                    8   II.    The Order erroneously disregards the subpoena’s negotiated scope.
                    9          The Order erroneously ignored the evidence before it and ordered production of a document
                   10   Mr. Musk agreed not to seek, and a second deposition, imposing a substantial burden on a non-
                   11   party with no justification. The Order should be set aside, or modified to reduce the burden on Mr.
                   12   Sutskever by, for example, excising the second deposition.
                   13          Since the firing of Sam Altman in November 2023, the public, including Elon Musk
                   14   himself, have expressed extensive curiosity about what happened; that curiosity extended to two
                   15   memos reported on extensively but never leaked to the public on which the Board relied: one about
                   16   Mr. Altman and one about Mr. Brockman. Agnolucci Decl. ¶ 6. Recognizing this, Mr. Sutskever,
                   17   upon receiving Mr. Musk’s subpoena, carefully negotiated a scope of production in correspondence
                   18   with Mr. Musk’s then-primary counsel that narrowed that subpoena to material of core relevance.
                   19   That scope is found in two sworn declarations from Mr. Sutskever’s counsel. ECF 322-1 (Holland
                   20   Decl.) ¶ 3; Agnolucci Decl. ¶¶ 3-4; Ex. A. Portions of the Altman memo fell within the negotiated
                   21   scope, so Mr. Sutskever produced the Altman memo (along with nearly a thousand pages of other
                   22   documents). Agnolucci Decl. ¶ 7. The Brockman memo did not, because none of the carefully
                   23   negotiated topics encompass any portion of that memo, so Mr. Sutskever did not produce it. Id. ¶
                   24   8. From May through September of this year, Mr. Musk’s counsel never requested the Brockman
                   25   memo by name, even knowing that the Altman memo had been produced. Id. ¶ 9. As far as the
                   26   undersigned knew on the day of Mr. Sutskever’s October 1 deposition, Mr. Musk had no interest
                   27   in seeking the Brockman memo, and a binding agreement with Mr. Musk precluded its production.

                   10          There are three reasons the Order should be set aside or modified to reduce burden.
                   11          First, the Order disregarded the agreement Mr. Musk entered into. Mr Musk argued, and
                   12   the Magistrate Judge agreed, that “ultimately this issue does not matter” because the document is
                   13   relevant. Dkt. 324 at 2. That was clear error and contrary to law. “When parties agree to specific
                   14   discovery protocols, courts enforce these commitments as binding.” United States v. Peters, 2025
                   15   WL 1474737, at *5 (E.D. Cal. May 22, 2025). By casting aside the agreement, the Order
                   16   “thwart[ed] the meet-and-confer process by undermining the parties’ agreement.” Greene v.
                   17   California Dep’t of Corr. & Rehab., 2025 WL 790498, at *4 (E.D. Cal. Mar. 12, 2025).
                   18          This principle is especially crucial for non-parties, who are entitled to extra protection. Mr.
                   19   Sutskever was entitled to rely on the negotiated scope of the subpoena and review documents for
                   20   responsiveness. Gamefam, Inc. v. WowWee Grp. Ltd., 2024 WL 1181001, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Mar.
                   21   18, 2024). This system relies on the “trust that a party produces documents after a review and
                   22   assessment for relevance and responsiveness” within the agreed-upon boundaries. UAB
                   23   “Planner5D” v. Meta Platforms, Inc., 746 F. Supp. 3d 806, 808 (N.D. Cal. 2024). The Order cast
                   24   all this aside with an ex post relevance judgment call with significant ramifications.
                   25          Second, the Order disregarded incontrovertible evidence that Mr. Musk’s counsel were
                   26   negligent, which is no reason to penalize a non-party by upending an agreement and ordering a
                   27   second deposition. A sworn declaration demonstrated that the agreement existed, and Mr. Musk
                   28   did not deny the details of the negotiated scope of production. See Dkt. 322-1 ¶ 3. Nor did Mr.

                   16   of whether a copy still existed. This series of events is astonishing; a non-party should not be
                   17   subjected to such burden and after-the-fact second-guessing because a litigant cannot get their act
                   18   together.
                   19           Third, the Order failed to protect Mr. Sutsekver’s interest in confidentiality in light of the
                   20   inappropriate interest in irrelevant material. This Court should not allow itself to become a forum
                   21   for satisfying the idle curiosity of private actors through civil discovery, especially when the private
                   22   actor in question is unique among nearly all litigants for his wealth, power, influence, and particular
                   23   interest in the subject matter, and his counsel have proven themselves untrustworthy with
                   24   confidential information. If the Brockman memo were so important to Mr. Musk’s case (as opposed
                   25   to his own curiosity), he would have asked for it before the day of Mr. Sutsekver’s deposition.
