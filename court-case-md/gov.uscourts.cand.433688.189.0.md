 1   I.     INTRODUCTION

 2          The Court should grant with prejudice Microsoft’s motion to dismiss Plaintiffs’ RICO

 3   claims against it for three reasons:

 4          First, as the Microsoft and OpenAI Defendants showed in their motions to dismiss, the

 5   Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) does not do what the Court instructed any amendment must

 6   do—i.e., “demonstrate how the defendants named under Section 1962(c) conducted OpenAI’s

 7   affairs in a manner RICO was intended to reach.” Motion to Dismiss Order, Dkt. 163 (“Order”) at

 8   10. Quite the contrary, the SAC adds no factual allegations demonstrating that Defendants

 9   operated through a pattern of fraudulent predicate acts. Microsoft’s Motion to Dismiss (“MTD”),
10   Dkt. 173, at 4-5; OpenAI Defendants’ Motion to Dismiss (“OAI MTD”), Dkt. 175, at 7-11. In their

11   Opposition, Plaintiffs make no effort to show that the SAC pleads anything more substantial—and

12   nor could they, as the SAC simply repeats many of the conclusory allegations in the First Amended

13   Complaint (“FAC”). See Dkt. 182 (“Opposition” or “Opp.”). They also do not address the

14   inadequacy of their efforts in the SAC to amend the pattern-of-racketeering-activity allegations,

15   amendments which Microsoft showed comprised of one adjective, one adverb, and zero facts. SAC

16   ¶¶ 438-441, see also Order at 10 (dismissing FAC because the alleged pattern of predicate acts was

17   “not tethered to the enterprise’s conduct”). Plaintiffs did not heed the Court’s Order, and the Court

18   should dismiss their RICO claims with prejudice.

19          Second, as Microsoft showed, the RICO claims in the SAC continue to suffer from the same
20   defects specific to Microsoft as in the FAC, requiring dismissing the RICO claims against Microsoft

21   for this additional reason.1 Namely, Plaintiffs plead (1) no predicate acts committed by Microsoft,

22   MTD at 5-6; (2) no specific intent by Microsoft to defraud, MTD at 6-7; and (3) no facts showing

23   Microsoft participated in the operation or management of the RICO enterprise, MTD at 7-8. In

24   response, Plaintiffs fail to identify any alleged predicate acts undertaken by Microsoft itself, Opp.

 4          B.      The SAC Does Not Remedy the Defects Specific to Microsoft.

 5           Count 20 against Microsoft also fails because the SAC alleges no predicate acts committed

 6    by Microsoft, no specific intent by Microsoft to defraud, and no facts showing Microsoft

 7    participated in the operation or management of the alleged RICO enterprise.

 8                  1.     The SAC does not allege any predicate acts by Microsoft.

 9           The SAC, like the FAC, still does not allege with particularity any predicate acts by
10    Microsoft. See MTD at 5-6. Rather than refute this point, Plaintiffs raise three arguments, each of

11    which misses the mark.

12           First, Plaintiffs argue without support that predicate acts alleged against other Defendants

13    should be imputed to Microsoft because they say Microsoft was involved in “perpetuating” the

14    allegedly “continuing” scheme. Opp. at 3. But a RICO Plaintiff must allege, at the “bare minimum

15    . . . that a defendant personally committed or aided and abetted the commission of two predicate

16    acts.” McLaughlin v. Anderson, 962 F.2d 187, 192 (2d Cir. 1992). Plaintiffs do not cite a single

17    case holding otherwise, and the SAC fails to allege any specific predicate act aided or undertaken

18    by Microsoft. Plaintiffs also offer no authority for the proposition that Microsoft could be held

19    liable for RICO when it did not commit or aid and abet any predicate acts. See MTD at 5-6. Indeed,
20    the Court has already ruled that the predicate acts alleged—that Altman and Brockman “made

21    false promises via emails from 2015 through early 2019”—occurred prior to “Microsoft’s

22    involvement . . . [which] did not begin until July 2019.” See Order at 6.

23           Plaintiffs further try to impute others’ alleged conduct to Microsoft, arguing that while the

24    alleged scheme between Microsoft and OpenAI started after the initial predicate acts were

25    purportedly undertaken by Altman and Brockman, Microsoft is liable for later joining a

      another defendant’s brief. Regardless, Microsoft set forth the basis for dismissal in its own brief,

11           C.      The SAC Does Not Save the RICO Conspiracy Claim.

12           As Microsoft explained, the Section 1962(d) RICO conspiracy claim fails not only because

13    the underlying RICO claim fails, see Sanford v. MemberWorks, Inc., 625 F.3d 550, 559 (9th Cir.

14    2010), but also because the SAC still fails to allege facts establishing that Microsoft knew about

15    and agreed to participate in a fraudulent scheme. MTD at 8; see Am. Online Inc., 208 F.3d at 751;

16    see also Lewis v. Sporck, 612 F. Supp. 1316, 1324 (N.D. Cal. 1985). Plaintiffs ask the Court to

17    infer this element as to Microsoft because they claim Microsoft later participated in non-fraudulent

18    acts. Opp. at 9-10. But as the Court already held, Plaintiffs did not plausibly allege Microsoft’s

19    knowledge of the allegedly fraudulent nature of the representations that Altman and Brockman

20    supposedly made to Musk (Order at 6), and the SAC contains no new facts showing otherwise.

21    As such, the SAC fails to plausibly allege that Microsoft knowingly agreed to participate in the

22    purported fraud, requiring dismissing Plaintiffs’ RICO conspiracy claim.

23    III.   CONCLUSION

24           The SAC does not “demonstrate how the defendants named under Section 1962(c)

25    conducted OpenAI’s affairs in a manner RICO was intended to reach.” Nor does it cure the FAC’s

26    pleading defects specific to Microsoft. And Plaintiffs have not shown that Microsoft agreed to

27    participate in any fraud. For these reasons and the reasons in Microsoft’s opening motion, the

28    Court should grant Microsoft’s Motion to Dismiss Counts 20-21 with prejudice.
