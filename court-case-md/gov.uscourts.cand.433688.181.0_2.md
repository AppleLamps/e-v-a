 9                                               ARGUMENT

10   I.     THE IMPLIED COVENANT CLAIM STATES A COGNIZABLE CAUSE OF
11          ACTION DISTINCT FROM BREACH OF CONTRACT.

12          Defendants’ Motion rests on a fundamental misapprehension of California law. They contend

13 that because the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing cannot create new substantive

14 obligations, any claim based on it must be duplicative of a breach of contract claim. Dkt. 175 at 3-7.

15 Musk’s implied covenant claim creates no new obligations—it addresses Defendants’ bad faith

16 subversion of existing duties to frustrate the agreement’s purpose and deny Musk its intended

17 benefits. Such bad faith is actionable under California law, separate and apart from a breach of

18 contract claim. California recognizes this cause of action for precisely this scenario—to address

19 conduct, that while argued not to technically breach contractual terms, frustrates the purpose and

20 benefits of the agreement. See Carma Devs. (Cal.), Inc. v. Marathon Dev. Cal., Inc., 2 Cal. 4th 342,

21 372 (1992). By definition, this cause of action is not duplicative of a breach of contract claim. See

22 Thrifty Payless, Inc. v. Americana at Brand LLC, 218 Cal. App. 4th 1230, 1244 (2013) (explaining

23 how “engaging in conduct which (while not technically transgressing the express covenants)

24 frustrates the other party’s rights to the benefits of the contract” violates the implied covenant of good

25 faith and fair dealing).

10          B.      The SAC Alleges Two Categories of Distinct Bad Faith Conduct
11                  In Addition to Defendants’ Abuses of Discretion.

12          Separate and apart from OpenAI’s effort to squeeze California’s good faith performance

13 requirement out of its implied-in-fact agreement, its Motion should be denied because the SAC also

14 carefully delineates how Defendants’ bad faith frustrated the contract’s charitable purpose through

15 two additional categories of conduct entirely distinct from breach.

16                  1.      Systematic Manipulation Through Redefinition.

17          The first category alleged in the SAC involves Defendants’ calculated manipulation of

18 contractual concepts to evade their obligations while maintaining surface compliance. Dkt. 170

19 ¶ 269(a). This goes to the heart of bad faith performance.

20          Consider the alleged duty to “assiduously act to minimize conflicts of interest[.]” Id. ¶ 247(i).

21 One can imagine various ways to perform this obligation. While good faith would require robust

22 conflict policies and independent oversight, and even weak conflict policies might achieve technical

23 compliance, Defendants instead strategically redefined “independence” so narrowly that obviously

24 conflicted directors still qualified. By limiting conflicts to “directly or personally held equity,” id. ¶

25 269(a), while ignoring significant indirect holdings, interlocking directorates, and financial

26 dependencies, Defendants subverted the duty’s purpose while maintaining its façade.

27          This conduct differs categorically from breach. A party can maintain weak conflict policies

28 without breaching a duty to “minimize conflicts”—a term that admits degrees. But systematically

 4                  2.     Concealment Through Corporate Subterfuge.

 5          The second category alleged in the SAC addresses Defendants’ elaborate concealment

 6 campaign—not merely failing to disclose material information, but actively creating complex

 7 corporate structures to evade detection and hide their ongoing transformation of the charity to a fully

 8 for-profit enterprise for the enrichment of the charity’s principals. Id. ¶ 269(b).

 9          Defendants mischaracterize this as a “failure to disclose contractual breaches.” Dkt. 175 at 6.

10 The allegation is not that Defendants failed to announce their breaches, but that they engineered an

11 elaborate shell game—consisting of over twenty opaque entities with hidden ownership—specifically

12 designed to obscure their profiteering activities, while maintaining OpenAI, Inc.’s charitable façade.

13 Defendants filed minimal public disclosures and refused to reveal ownership stakes, Dkt. 170 ¶¶ 124-

14 25, and made repeated public statements touting OpenAI’s non-profit mission while quietly planning

15 its for-profit conversion, id. ¶¶ 310, 428.

16          This violated the fundamental expectation inherent in charitable giving—that fiduciaries

17 exercise discretion openly and honestly, not through byzantine corporate structures designed to

18 conceal self-dealing. The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing demands transparency and

19 honesty from charity fiduciaries managing donor funds, not elaborate schemes to obscure private

20 enrichment.

26 good faith performance.

10 RICO conspiracy claims to ones where there are substantive RICO violations. In fact, it says the

11 opposite: “To establish a violation of section 1962(d), Plaintiffs must allege either an agreement that

12 is a substantive violation of RICO or that the defendants agreed to commit, or participated in, a

13 violation of two predicate offenses.” Howard, 208 F.3d at 751 (emphasis added). That is, an

14 agreement alone is sufficient, and district courts have regularly so held since. Ghebreyesus v. Fed.

15 Dem. Rep. of Ethiopia, No. 22-CV-1717-RFB-EJY, 2023 WL 6392611, at *10 (D. Nev. Sept. 30,

16 2023) (“[I]t is not necessary to prove any substantive RICO violations ever occurred as a result of the

17 conspiracy. Rather, [i]t is the mere agreement to violate RICO that § 1962(d) forbids.” (internal

18 quotation marks and citations omitted)); Tatung Co., Ltd. v. Shu Tze Hsu, 217 F. Supp. 3d 1138, 1170

19 (C.D. Cal. 2016) (same); Nat’l Immunogenics Corp. v. Newport Trial Grp., No. 15-CV-2034 JVS

20 (JCGx), 2016 WL 11520711, at *13 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 1, 2016) (same).

21          OpenAI did not challenge the conspiracy count’s pleading of an agreement to violate RICO.

22 Dkt. 170 ¶ 455. Whether or not Plaintiffs’ substantive RICO count survives, OpenAI’s waiver means

23 the conspiracy count will stand.
