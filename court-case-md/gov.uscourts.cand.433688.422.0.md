 1          At trial, Musk plans to impugn OpenAI’s October 2025 recapitalization transaction as an
 2   abandonment of its mission, a “conversion” into a for-profit enterprise, and a breach of
 3   commitments to the public. He will attack the nonprofit directors who approved the transaction as
 4   beholden to for-profit investors and corrupted by for-profit motives. He will assail the process that
 5   led to the transaction, and claim that the nonprofit got a raw deal. And he will portray himself as a
 6   public champion suing to stop the “conversion” “in the name of the People.” Dkt. 64-20 at 2.
 7          Musk wants to do this without allowing the jury to consider the full picture. As OpenAI’s
 8   nonprofit directors will testify, the structure and terms of the recapitalization embody commitments
 9   by OpenAI to the Attorneys General of California and Delaware, the principal regulators of
10   charitable trusts in their respective states, and the principal representatives of the public Musk
11   claims to represent. Those terms and commitments—designed to ensure the continued primacy of
12   OpenAI’s nonprofit mission and governance and to create one of the most well-resourced
13   nonprofits in history—were reached after nearly a year of intensive negotiation with the AGs, are
14   memorialized in written agreements with the AGs that were a central focus of the board’s
15   deliberations, and form an integral part of the final transaction.
16          Musk does not want the jury to hear this. He now moves to exclude the agreements between
17   OpenAI and the AGs (Musk Exs. 3-4); the AGs’ official releases announcing the same (Musk Exs.
18   1-2); and all testimony about OpenAI’s engagement with the AGs respecting the very transaction
19   he attacks. Musk’s motion should be denied. The recapitalization cannot be separated from the AG
20   process that shaped it. And if Musk attacks the recapitalization, its terms, and the process by which
21   it was approved, basic fairness requires that the jury consider evidence of what the terms actually
22   are, how they were reached, and why. Anything less would prejudice OpenAI by allowing Musk to
23   present a one-sided and incomplete narrative to the jury.

11                                              ARGUMENT
12          The AG evidence is relevant. Musk asserts without explanation that the AG evidence is
13   irrelevant because the AGs purportedly reviewed OpenAI’s restructuring “under a different
14   standard” and “for a different purpose.” MIL at 2. That is both wrong and beside the point.
15   Regardless of the circumstances of the AGs’ review, the jury cannot fairly consider the
16   recapitalization without understanding how it was negotiated, how and why it evolved over time,
17   what the directors considered when approving it, and the substantial role of regulators in shaping
18   it, all of which requires presentation of the AG evidence. The jury is also entitled to understand that
19   core aspects of OpenAI’s structure are enshrined in binding agreements with and commitments to
20   the AGs that cannot be unilaterally altered. And it would be impossible for OpenAI’s witnesses to
21   describe the nonprofit’s adherence to its charitable purpose without reference to its work,
22   undertaken in consultation with the AGs, to determine a structure that would protect its mission.
23   That discussion, inconsistent with Musk’s narrative that OpenAI absconded with charitable assets,
24   is plainly relevant and inextricable from the narrative.
25          Moreover, the AG evidence satisfies even Musk’s standard of relevance. Where, as here,
26   the AGs reviewed “the same conduct” to be tried “and for the same reason,” the evidence is
27   “undoubtedly probative.” Microsoft Corp. v. Motorola, Inc., 795 F.3d 1024, 1056 (9th Cir. 2015).
28   The AGs evaluated the same transaction that Musk challenges, and examined precisely the same

 1   issues. See Musk Ex. 2 at 2 (Delaware AG review); Musk Ex. 3 at 1 (CA AG). And the AGs applied
 2   the same legal principles as will be applied in this case. See Restatement (Third) of Trusts § 93
 3   (2012) (defining breach of trust).
 4          The AG evidence is not unduly prejudicial. Musk argues he will be prejudiced because the
 5   jury will “substitute” the AGs’ “findings for its own.” MIL at 3. But the AGs did not make any
 6   “findings” (as Musk agrees)—they negotiated agreements with OpenAI to structure the
 7   recapitalization in the public interest. In any event, as Musk’s cases make clear, his concern
 8   warrants exclusion under Rule 403 only when the sole purpose of the proffered evidence is to
 9   establish liability or its absence. In PG&E, for example, the government sought to admit findings
10   from a civil proceeding to support its criminal prosecution. United States v. Pac. Gas & Elec. Co.,
11   178 F. Supp. 3d 927, 948 (N.D. Cal. 2016). And in Long Beach, defendants in a civil wrongful
12   death suit sought to escape liability by introducing the district attorney’s determination not to bring
13   criminal charges. N.W. v. City of Long Beach, 2016 WL 9021966, at *3 (C.D. Cal. June 7, 2016).
14   Here, however, the AG evidence is intertwined with the very transaction Musk challenges—which
15   cannot be understood without accounting for the involvement of the AGs, the undertakings reached,
16   and OpenAI’s binding obligations to its primary regulators. Musk can offer his own evidence to
17   attack the recapitalization but should not be permitted to present an account excluding vital facts.
18          The AG evidence is not inadmissible hearsay. The AG agreements are “legally operative
19   document[s]” with “independent legal significance,” and thus “do not constitute hearsay.”
20   Forreststream Holdings Ltd. v. Shenkman, 2017 WL 1112963, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 24, 2017). All
21   of the AG exhibits are independently admissible “public records” under Rule 803(8)(A)(i)—which
22   Musk ignores—because they “set[] out” “the office’s activities.” Phillips v. Oosterbaan, 508 F.
23   Supp. 3d 1103, 1114-15 (D. Utah 2020); Terry v. Wasatch Advantage Grp., LLC, 2024 WL
24   3471297, at *4 (E.D. Cal. July 18, 2024) (admitting DOJ press release). Musk has not met his
25   burden under Rule 803(8)(B) of showing that the AG evidence is untrustworthy. The case he cites
26   involved an “incomplete” draft report from an “unknown” author. Sullivan v. Dollar Tree Stores,
27   Inc., 623 F.3d 770, 778 (9th Cir. 2010). The AG exhibits are signed agreements and statements
28   from the chief law enforcement officers of California and Delaware.
